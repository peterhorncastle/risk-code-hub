Era History
Pre-1960 — Early Multinational Insurance

Simple Open Covers and Overseas Extensions

Before the great post-war multinational corporate expansion, insurance of overseas operations was handled through a patchwork of ad hoc arrangements: open covers at Lloyd's, overseas branches of domestic insurers, and informal agreements between related carriers in different countries. There was no systematic need for DIC/DIL because most companies had relatively simple international operations — often just export sales offices or agency arrangements rather than owned manufacturing plants. EST

Lloyd's had long provided overseas risks cover through facultative placements for colonial and trade-era risks — shipping, cargo, and some property at overseas trading posts. But the concept of a structured master policy sitting above a network of locally-admitted primary policies did not yet exist. Each country's risk was essentially a standalone placement.

The admitted vs non-admitted distinction was already present but less rigorously enforced in many markets, particularly in developing countries and British Commonwealth territories where Lloyd's had de facto access through colonial commercial relationships.

1960–1980 — Multinational Expansion and the Birth of DIC/DIL

US MNCs Go Global; INA Pioneers the Global Programme

The post-war economic boom drove rapid multinational corporate expansion. US manufacturers, chemical companies and retailers established owned subsidiaries in Europe, Latin America and Asia at an unprecedented rate. For the first time, a single corporate risk manager was responsible for insuring factories and offices in 20 or 30 countries simultaneously — each with different admitted insurance requirements, different local policy terms, and different local insurer quality standards. EST

Insurance Company of North America (INA) — later to become CIGNA — is widely credited as the pioneer of the global multinational insurance programme structure in the 1960s. INA developed the concept of a US master policy providing coordinated global coverage, with local admitted "underlying" policies in each country and the master stepping in where local coverage was inadequate. This was the direct precursor to what became the DIC/DIL structure. EST

Lloyd's entered this market in earnest through the late 1960s and 1970s, offering non-admitted master policy capacity that US domestic markets could not always match. Lloyd's flexibility on policy terms and willingness to provide broad "all risks" cover above local admitted policies made it a natural choice for multinationals seeking a single source of master programme capacity. The concept of Controlled Master Programmes (CMP) began to take shape. EST

1980–1990 — Regulatory Scrutiny and Admitted Rules Tighten

Tax Authorities and Regulators Notice Premium Flows

As multinational programmes grew in size and sophistication, tax authorities and insurance regulators in key markets began to scrutinise them. The central concern: significant premium was flowing out of local markets (and out of local tax jurisdictions) to master policy insurers in London or New York, without going through locally-licensed admitted carriers. Regulators feared this undermined local insurance market development and deprived governments of premium tax revenue. VER

The period 1980–1990 saw a significant tightening of admitted insurance rules in many markets, particularly in Latin America and Asia. Brazil (SUSEP), India (GIC), and later China (PICC/CBIRC) all developed regulations that made it progressively more difficult to place insurance directly with non-admitted carriers. The need for local fronting arrangements — where a locally-admitted insurer issues the local policy and reinsures the risk back to the master programme carrier — became the standard solution in restrictive markets. VER

Lloyd's responded by developing the Controlled Master Programme (CMP) framework — a structured approach to multinational programme management that explicitly addressed admitted rules compliance, premium allocation, and claims coordination between local policies and the master. The Lloyd's overseas market information system (OMIS) began tracking non-admitted placement permissions by jurisdiction. VER

1990–2005 — Globalisation Accelerates; DIC/DIL Market Matures

Lloyd's Multinational Market Grows; Zurich and AIG Enter the Space

The 1990s saw explosive growth in multinational corporate operations, driven by NAFTA, EU single market integration, and the opening of Chinese and Eastern European markets. Insurance programme complexity grew in parallel: a typical Fortune 500 company might now have operations in 50–80 countries, each requiring a locally-admitted primary policy and all coordinated under a single master programme. EST

AIG and Zurich Global Corporate emerged as significant competitors to Lloyd's in the multinational programme market during this period, offering global networks of admitted carriers that could provide local policies in many markets without the need for fronting. This put pressure on Lloyd's to demonstrate the advantages of its non-admitted master programme structure — particularly its superior coverage terms and NatCat capacity — over network programme alternatives. VER

The OECD during this period began work that would eventually lead to BEPS — as multinationals used captive insurance subsidiaries and intra-group premium arrangements to shift profits to low-tax jurisdictions, the arm's-length pricing of insurance transactions between related parties came under increasing academic and regulatory scrutiny. VER

Lloyd's DC Market Context: By 2000, Lloyd's had established itself as the premier market for multinational programme DIC/DIL capacity, particularly for NatCat-exposed property risks and for Financial Lines (D&O, PI) master programmes. Syndicates such as XL, ACE (both at Lloyd's before corporatisation), and dedicated multinational programme specialists provided broad terms that US and European admitted markets could not match. The Lloyd's DC code formalised this capacity under a single risk classification.
2005–2015 — NatCat Events Expose DIC/DIL Weaknesses

Thailand, Christchurch and Japan: DIC/DIL Triggered en Masse

The period 2005–2015 brought a succession of major NatCat events that tested DIC/DIL programmes at scale. The 2010–2011 Christchurch earthquakes in New Zealand triggered DIC for perils that local admitted policies excluded or sublimited, and DIL where earthquake sublimits were exhausted by the severity of the events. New Zealand's insurance market, while sophisticated, had inadequate earthquake sublimits relative to the exposure at major urban concentrations. EST

The Thailand floods of 2011 were the single most significant DIC/DIL event in the class's history. Unprecedented flooding inundated seven major industrial estates north of Bangkok where Japanese, US and European manufacturers had concentrated production. Many local Thai admitted property policies either excluded flood or had flood sublimits far below actual exposures. DIC master policies at Lloyd's and other international markets were triggered on a massive scale, with insured losses from DIC/DIL programmes contributing significantly to the $20bn+ total insured loss figure. VER

The Japan earthquake and tsunami of 2011 exposed supply chain BI DIC/DIL — where contingent business interruption losses from disrupted Japanese supply chains triggered DIC claims in automotive and electronics sectors globally. BI indemnity period sublimits in local admitted policies proved woefully inadequate for supply chain recovery times of 12–24 months. EST

Hurricane Sandy (2012) triggered DIC widely in the US for flood losses, as standard US admitted property policies exclude flood (covered by NFIP) — businesses with Lloyd's DIC master programmes recovered flood losses that would otherwise have been uncovered. VER

2015–Present — BEPS, NatCat Hardening, and Post-COVID Complexity

Transfer Pricing, Rate Hardening and Supply Chain BI Awareness

The OECD's BEPS Action Plan, finalised in 2015, brought the arm's-length pricing of intra-group insurance transactions into sharp focus. Multinational programme premium allocations between parent and subsidiaries must now be supported by robust transfer pricing documentation. Tax authorities in France, Germany, Australia and elsewhere have challenged multinationals' insurance premium deductions, particularly where captive insurance subsidiaries are involved in the DIC/DIL structure. VER

The NatCat seasons of 2017–2022 (Harvey, Irma, Maria; Australian cyclones; European floods; Turkey earthquake) drove a significant hardening of NatCat-exposed DIC/DIL property rates. Sublimit DIC capacity — the most common property DIC/DIL application — became both more expensive and more restricted, with underwriters imposing NatCat sublimit requirements on DIC/DIL master policies themselves. The concept of "DIC on DIC" emerged: multinationals seeking to top up even their DIC sublimits with additional capacity layers. EST

COVID-19 created a new frontier for DIC/DIL: where local admitted policies excluded pandemic BI, did the DIC master policy respond? The answer depended critically on exact policy wording — many DIC policies had been written on "all risks of physical loss or damage" bases that were not triggered by government orders in the absence of physical damage, while others were broader. The COVID BI/DIC interface generated significant legal uncertainty and is an area of ongoing wording development. EST

The Ukraine war (2022) created operational challenges for multinational programmes covering Russian and Ukrainian operations — local admitted policies were cancelled, admitted insurance markets withdrew, and the structure of DIC/DIL master cover for these territories was tested by sanctions, market withdrawal, and total loss of local admitted underlying coverage.

Key Milestones
~1965

INA Pioneers Global Programme

Insurance Company of North America develops the coordinated multinational insurance programme concept — direct precursor to the modern DIC/DIL master structure.

~1980

Lloyd's DIC/DIL Formalised

Lloyd's syndicates formalise the DIC/DIL master policy structure; DC risk code introduced to categorise multinational programme business at Lloyd's.

1985

Controlled Master Programme (CMP)

Lloyd's develops the CMP framework addressing admitted rules compliance, premium allocation methodology, and claims coordination between local and master policies.

2010–11

NZ Earthquakes — DIC Triggers

Christchurch earthquakes trigger DIC for excluded/sublimited perils in New Zealand admitted policies. First large-scale DIC trigger in developed-market property.

2011

Thailand Floods — Mass DIC/DIL

$20bn+ insured loss; mass DIC triggers for flood exclusions in Thai admitted policies and DIL triggers where sublimits exhausted. The defining DIC/DIL loss event.

2011

Japan Earthquake — Supply Chain BI DIC

Tsunami disrupts Japanese supply chains globally; BI indemnity period sublimits in local policies exhausted; CBI DIC triggers for automotive and electronics sectors.

2012

Hurricane Sandy — Flood DIC, USA

US admitted property policies exclude flood; Lloyd's DIC master policies trigger for flood losses. Highlights gap between NFIP coverage and full replacement value for commercial property.

2015

OECD BEPS Action Plan

BEPS Actions 8–10 require arm's-length pricing for intra-group insurance transactions. Multinational programme premium allocations require robust transfer pricing documentation going forward.

2017–22

NatCat Hardening Cycle

Harvey/Irma/Maria, Australian floods, European floods and Turkey earthquake drive NatCat DIC/DIL rates up 40–80%. NatCat sublimit DIC capacity restricted and repriced.

2022

Ukraine War — Programme Disruption

War exclusions trigger in local admitted and master policies; sanctions restrict premium payment; multinational programme coverage for Russian/Ukrainian operations withdrawn or invalidated.
