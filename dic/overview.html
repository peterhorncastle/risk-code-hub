Lloyd's Risk Code Coverage

DC — Difference in Conditions / Difference in Limits

The DC risk code at Lloyd's covers Difference in Conditions (DIC) and Difference in Limits (DIL) policies — the master policy layer of multinational insurance programmes that sits above and harmonises local admitted policies worldwide. DC is a structural/portfolio product applied across Property, Casualty, Financial Lines, Marine and other classes, wherever a multinational company needs consistent global coverage above its patchwork of local admitted policies.[I4]

DIC — Difference in Conditions

Difference in Conditions responds when a local admitted policy does NOT cover a loss at all — i.e., there is a coverage gap. Classic examples: local admitted property policy excludes flood; local D&O policy excludes securities claims. The DIC master policy provides the missing coverage on broader terms than the local policy. DIC is inherently riskier than DIL because it covers perils the local insurer declined to accept.[I2]

DIL — Difference in Limits

Difference in Limits responds when the local admitted policy covers a loss but its limit is insufficient. Example: local policy has a €5m earthquake sublimit; the agreed master programme standard is €20m. DIL provides the additional €15m. DIL is typically lower risk than DIC because the local insurer has already accepted the peril — the DIL simply tops up the layer. Both DIC and DIL typically sit above local admitted primary policies.[I2]

Programme Tower: How DIC/DIL Sits Above Local Admitted Policies

DIC Trigger — Coverage Gap

The local admitted policy does NOT respond to the loss at all — there is a gap in coverage between local terms and master programme terms. The DIC master policy steps in to fill this gap.

Example: Factory flood in Thailand. Local admitted property policy excludes flood (common in Thai market). Master DIC at Lloyd's covers flood on broader terms. DIC pays flood loss after local policy declines.

DIL Trigger — Limit Shortfall

The local admitted policy responds to the loss but its limit is exhausted before the full loss is paid. The DIL master policy tops up to the master programme limit.

Example: Earthquake in New Zealand. Local policy has NZ$5m earthquake sublimit. Master programme limit is NZ$20m. After local pays NZ$5m, DIL pays the remaining NZ$15m to reach master limit.

Coverage Taxonomy

Property DIC/DIL

Physical damage to buildings, plant and machinery, stock and contents. The most common DIC/DIL application — NatCat sublimits (flood, earthquake, windstorm) in local admitted policies are the most frequent DIC trigger. [I10]

Casualty DIC/DIL

General liability, employers liability and workers compensation DIC/DIL for international operations. Where local GL/EL limits are insufficient or terms narrower than master programme standard.[I2]

Financial Lines DIC/DIL

D&O, Professional Indemnity and Cyber DIC/DIL — wherever a multinational wants consistent global coverage above patchwork local policies. Local D&O policies may exclude securities class actions; DIC fills the gap.[I2]

Business Interruption DIC/DIL

BI coverage following a property loss DIC/DIL trigger. Local admitted BI sublimits are frequently inadequate for complex multinational supply chains — BI DIC/DIL extends indemnity period and limit to master standard.[I10]

Contingent Business Interruption DIC/DIL

CBI losses from supplier/customer property damage. Thailand floods 2011 and Japan tsunami 2011 exposed massive CBI exposure — DIC/DIL master policies were triggered where local CBI sublimits were exhausted or the peril was excluded.[I10]

NatCat Sublimit Top-Up

Flood, earthquake, windstorm, storm surge — local admitted policies in many markets apply strict sublimits. DIC/DIL top-up is the most common application. NatCat hardening 2017–2022 significantly increased demand for sublimit top-up DIC.[I6]

Admitted vs Non-Admitted: In most countries, insurance must be placed with a locally-licensed (admitted) insurer. DIC/DIL is typically written at Lloyd's as non-admitted — permitted in most markets where a local admitted policy already exists as primary. This creates the "master + local" structure that is the standard in multinational property and financial lines programmes. In highly restrictive markets (Brazil, China, India), local fronting arrangements or reinsurance structures are required. [I3]
BEPS Warning: OECD Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) Action Plan 8–10 requires that intra-group insurance premium allocations between related parties be at arm's length. Multinationals must maintain transfer pricing documentation for DIC/DIL premium charged to local subsidiaries. Tax authority scrutiny of multinational programme premium flows has intensified since 2015. Non-compliant structures face premium disallowance and penalties. [L4]
