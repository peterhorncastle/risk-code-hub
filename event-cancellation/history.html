Era History
Pre-1920 — Origins at Lloyd's Coffee House

Contingency Roots: Marine Heritage and Early Bespoke Covers

The concept of insuring against the financial consequences of an uncertain event — the very essence of contingency insurance — is as old as Lloyd's itself. Edward Lloyd's coffee house in the City of London provided a marketplace for merchants to transfer risks from the 17th century. Marine insurance formed the foundation, but underwriters were willing to entertain almost any bespoke wager or financial guarantee. [L1]

Early contingency-style covers at Lloyd's included life assurance on the outcome of duels, wagers on election results, and guarantees against the non-delivery of goods. By the late Victorian era, Lloyd's syndicates were writing increasingly sophisticated non-marine contingency covers as the entertainment and sporting industries began to develop commercially. The economic logic was simple: a promoter who had invested substantially in an event wanted protection against the financial catastrophe of that event not taking place.

Weather insurance — the earliest form of what would become pluvius insurance — emerged from the agricultural sector, where the financial consequences of adverse weather for outdoor activities were well understood. British seaside resorts and garden party organisers were among the first non-agricultural purchasers of weather contingency cover from Lloyd's underwriters. [I5]

1920–1970 — Pluvius Develops; Entertainment Industry Emerges

Outdoor Event Weather Insurance and Early Entertainment Covers

The interwar period saw the formalisation of pluvius insurance — named after Pluvius, the Roman god of rain — as a distinct product line at Lloyd's. UK seaside resort operators, Wimbledon tennis (an early and historically significant Lloyd's account), and outdoor sporting venues began purchasing structured weather covers that would pay out if rainfall exceeded a defined threshold during specified event hours. The parametric structure — automatic trigger on weather measurement, no proof of individual financial loss required — was a practical innovation that made the product simple to operate and attractive to event organisers. [I4]

The growing cinema and entertainment industry of the 1930s created demand for production completion guarantees and contingency covers for film productions — not strictly Lloyd's PC code, but a precursor to the modern PA (contingency general) market. In the US, early Hollywood studios sought guarantees against the death or incapacity of leading stars whose appearance was financially critical to a film's commercial success. Lloyd's specialist underwriters developed early non-appearance covers for these risks.

The post-war entertainment boom of the 1950s and 1960s — rock and roll, mass-market outdoor concerts, the birth of the modern pop music industry — created a new and expanding client base for event contingency insurance. Promoters investing significant sums in touring artists recognised that the financial exposure from a cancelled tour was material and insurable. The Lloyd's contingency market grew quietly but steadily through this period, underpinned by the judgement of a handful of specialist underwriters. [I3]

1970–1990 — Terrorism, Sport & Non-Appearance

Munich 1972: Terrorism Enters the Frame; Concert Tour Covers Mature

The 1972 Munich Olympics massacre — in which Palestinian terrorists killed 11 Israeli athletes and coaches — was a watershed moment for event contingency insurance. For the first time, a major international sporting event was halted by terrorism with massive global broadcast and financial consequences. The event demonstrated that the financial exposure from catastrophic event interruption was not merely theoretical. Underwriters began to think more systematically about terrorism as an insured peril in event cancellation policies. [I3]

The 1970s and 1980s saw the formalisation of non-appearance insurance as a standalone product. Concert promoters booking major touring artists — from the Rolling Stones to Michael Jackson — faced enormous advance cost exposure. A single cancelled show could cost a promoter hundreds of thousands of pounds in venue costs, production costs and, increasingly, advance ticket refund obligations. Lloyd's specialist contingency syndicates developed structured non-appearance policies covering death, illness, injury, and (controversially) the insured artist's decision not to perform. The negotiation of health exclusions and pre-existing condition clauses became a specialist skill.

Live Aid in 1985 — a massive outdoor benefit concert at Wembley Stadium and simultaneously in Philadelphia — illustrated both the scale of the events market and the weather exposure inherent in outdoor events. Organisers worked closely with meteorologists and developed sophisticated weather risk management for the event. Prize indemnity as a distinct product grew through the 1980s as golf tournaments, charity competitions and promotional events proliferated — hole-in-one insurance became one of the more colourful niches in the Lloyd's contingency market. [I7]

1990–2000 — Gulf War, Woodstock & Growing Market

Political Risk Enters EC; Prize Indemnity Matures; Lloyd's Specialist Syndicates

The 1991 Gulf War triggered a wave of event cancellations across the Middle East and internationally as promoters and corporates cancelled conferences, exhibitions and travel events in response to the security environment. Gulf War event cancellation claims were among the first to test the interface between terrorism coverage and government-order cancellations in EC policies. Underwriters clarified that "government order" could include orders arising from geopolitical crises rather than only domestic events. [I1]

Woodstock 1994 and 1999 produced some of the first large documented festival cancellation and curtailment claims in the modern era. The 1999 Woodstock festival, which descended into riots and property destruction, raised questions about the scope of EC coverage for voluntary curtailment where insured and uninsured perils were intermingled. These events drove clearer policy drafting on what constitutes "uncontrollable circumstances" sufficient to trigger the policy.

By the late 1990s, Lloyd's had established a recognised group of specialist contingency underwriters whose expertise covered the full range of PC, PA, PN, PU and PZ risks. The market was characterised by long-term relationships between brokers, clients and underwriters, high levels of underwriting discretion, and bespoke policy wordings rather than standard forms. This period saw significant growth in prize indemnity, driven by golf tournament proliferation and corporate promotional activity. [I7]

2000–2020 — 9/11, SARS, Ash Cloud & Pre-COVID Complexity

Terrorism Exclusions, Pandemic First Debates, and the Ash Cloud

The 11 September 2001 attacks on New York and Washington were the first major test of terrorism coverage in EC policies at scale. Large numbers of events — sports fixtures, concerts, corporate gatherings — were cancelled in the immediate aftermath. The Lloyd's market's response to 9/11 in the EC class paralleled its response in property: terrorism was re-examined as an insured peril and, for many policies, moved to an optional extension rather than a default inclusion. Terrorism-specific EC endorsements — requiring explicit underwriting of terrorism cover at an additional premium — became standard market practice after 2001. [L3]

SARS in 2003 was the first occasion on which the EC market debated whether communicable disease could constitute an insured peril under event cancellation policies. SARS caused cancellations of events in Hong Kong, Singapore and Toronto but was geographically contained and the financial exposure was manageable. Underwriters noted the issue but did not universally act on it — a decision that would have profound consequences 17 years later. H1N1 influenza in 2009 prompted a further discussion but again did not produce universal action on pandemic exclusions.

The Eyjafjallajökull ash cloud in April 2010 — which closed European airspace for six days — caused estimated losses across travel, aviation and events of over £1.3bn. EC policies were triggered for events cancelled because key performers or speakers could not travel. The event was a significant reminder that government-order cancellations arising from natural catastrophes could produce systemic EC losses even without any security or pandemic element. [I1]

Glastonbury Festival and other major outdoor events continued to generate significant weather claims through the 2010s. The introduction of increasingly sophisticated weather modelling, on-site meteorological services and parametric weather insurance structures refined the market. Brexit uncertainty created a new source of political risk considerations for large European events planned for UK venues. [I5]

Lloyd's EC Market Pre-COVID: By 2019, the Lloyd's event cancellation and contingency market was a mature, competitive and generally soft market. Estimated GWP of approximately $1.5–2bn globally with Lloyd's accounting for a substantial share. Pandemic or communicable disease exclusions were not standard in most market wordings. The market had not experienced a systemic loss event since 9/11. Competition was intense and rates had been under pressure for several years. The stage was set for the largest loss event in the class's history. [I3]
2020–Present — COVID-19: Total Market Catastrophe and Restructuring

The Pandemic Shuts Down the World's Events; £141m Wimbledon; Complete Market Rewrite

In March 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic caused governments around the world to impose legal prohibitions on public gatherings. Virtually the entire global events calendar — concerts, festivals, sporting events, conferences, trade exhibitions, award ceremonies, weddings — was cancelled simultaneously. This was without precedent: no prior scenario had produced such universal, simultaneous and prolonged event cancellation across all geographies. [I2]

The financial consequences for the EC insurance market were catastrophic. Estimated insured losses of $4–6bn across 2020 and 2021 made COVID-19 the largest loss event in the history of the event cancellation class. Many 2019-vintage policies contained no pandemic or communicable disease exclusion, and "government order" was an insured peril in numerous wordings. Insurers faced claims across their entire portfolio simultaneously — the antithesis of the independent, non-correlated loss profile that contingency underwriters had historically assumed.

Wimbledon (AELTC) stands as the emblematic COVID EC success story. The All England Lawn Tennis Club had — uniquely — purchased pandemic insurance specifically for 17 consecutive years following the SARS scare in 2003. When Wimbledon 2020 was cancelled, the club claimed £141m on its pandemic event cancellation policy — one of the largest single EC insurance claims ever paid. This is the exceptional case: the vast majority of event organisers had no pandemic cover. [I4]

By mid-2021, the Lloyd's EC market had undergone a complete structural rewrite. Pandemic / communicable disease exclusions are now universally standard. Terrorism covers remain but are separately rated and more carefully worded. Capacity has partially returned but rates remain materially higher than pre-COVID levels. The market learned the hard way that contingency insurance is not immune to systemic, correlated catastrophe loss. [I3]

Key Milestones
Pre-1900

Lloyd's Bespoke Contingency

Lloyd's coffee house underwriters accept ad hoc contingency risks; weather covers for outdoor events emerge from agricultural insurance roots.

1920s–30s

Pluvius Insurance Formalised

Structured rainfall threshold covers for UK outdoor events; Wimbledon among earliest and most enduring Lloyd's EC accounts.

1972

Munich Olympics Massacre

Terrorism kills 11 at the Games; EC market begins considering terrorism as a distinct insured peril for major international events.

1980s

Non-Appearance Matures

Concert tour promoters purchase systematic PN cover for headline artists; health exclusions and pre-existing condition clauses refined.

1980s

Prize Indemnity Growth

Hole-in-one golf insurance and promotional prize indemnity become established Lloyd's contingency niches.

1991

Gulf War Event Cancellations

First major test of government-order cancellation perils; geopolitical crisis triggers widespread event cancellations.

2001

9/11 — Terrorism Extension Introduced

Post-9/11, terrorism moves to an optional, separately-rated extension in EC policies; terrorism exclusions become market standard in base wordings.

2003

SARS — First Pandemic Debate

Market discusses communicable disease as EC peril; action not universally taken. Wimbledon uniquely begins purchasing pandemic EC cover this year.

2010

Eyjafjallajökull Ash Cloud

European airspace closure for 6 days causes £1.3bn+ in travel and event cancellation losses; government-order perils tested at scale.

2020

Wimbledon COVID Claim £141m

AELTC claims £141m — one of the largest EC claims ever paid — on pandemic cover uniquely purchased since 2003. The rest of the market faces uninsured losses.

2020–21

COVID — $4–6bn EC Loss

Largest loss event in EC class history. Pandemic/communicable disease exclusion immediately adopted universally across the market.

2021+

Market Restructuring Complete

New pandemic exclusion standard; rates harden materially; capacity returns selectively; market enters cautious recovery phase.
