Era History
Pre-1910 — Common Law Negligence Era

The Unholy Trinity: Fellow-Servant, Contributory Negligence & Assumption of Risk

Before the advent of workers compensation statutes, injured American workers had to sue their employers in common law negligence to recover damages for workplace injuries. This was, in practice, nearly impossible. Employers had three devastating defences — collectively known as the "unholy trinity" — that defeated the vast majority of employee claims.

The fellow-servant rule barred recovery if the injury was caused by a co-worker rather than the employer directly. Contributory negligence barred recovery if the worker had contributed even 1% to the accident. Assumption of risk held that workers who understood the dangerous nature of their jobs had impliedly accepted the risk of injury as a condition of employment.

The human cost was staggering. In 1907, the US industrial accident toll was estimated at 35,000 deaths and 2 million injuries annually. Despite reform efforts — including the Crystal Springs Brewery litigation of 1904 that exposed the inadequacy of employer defences — injured workers and their families were routinely left destitute. Employers had little financial incentive to invest in safety, because negligence actions rarely succeeded.[L1]

1910–1930 — The Birth of Workers Compensation

Wisconsin 1911: The "Grand Bargain" That Transformed American Industry

Wisconsin enacted the first comprehensive workers compensation law in 1911 — the product of a deliberate social compromise between employers and labour. Employers gave up their negligence defences and accepted strict liability for workplace injuries; workers gave up their right to sue in tort and accepted fixed statutory benefits as full compensation. This "grand bargain" was the foundation of modern WC.

The WC model spread with remarkable speed. By 1920 all but six states had adopted WC statutes; by 1948 all 48 states had coverage. The Maryland system (1902, an earlier attempt) had been struck down as unconstitutional; Wisconsin's elective model avoided this by allowing employers to opt out — though opting out exposed them to full tort liability without the three defences, making opting out commercially untenable. [L1]

The Federal Longshoremen and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act (LHWCA) was enacted in 1927 to fill the gap left by a Supreme Court decision (Southern Pacific Co. v Jensen, 1917) that state WC laws could not apply to maritime workers on navigable waters. The LHWCA created a federal compensation scheme for dock workers, ship repairers and maritime workers — the direct predecessor of Lloyd's W6 code. Benefits were set at two-thirds of average weekly wages with no dollar cap on medical.[L2]

1930–1970 — Federal Expansion: Social Security, FECA & Black Lung

Federal Programmes Extend the Safety Net; Occupational Disease Recognised

The Social Security Act 1935 created the broader federal income support framework that would interact with WC through total and permanent disability provisions. The Federal Employees' Compensation Act (FECA) of 1946 consolidated and modernised compensation for federal government workers — creating a parallel federal WC programme administered by the US Department of Labor (OWCP).[L6]

The Defense Base Act (DBA) of 1941 extended LHWCA coverage to workers on overseas US military bases and government contracts — critical legislation for US cold-war era contractors and later for contractors in Vietnam, Iraq and Afghanistan. DBA exposure under the W6 code has generated some of the largest individual WC claims in history due to war-zone injuries and deaths.[L3]

The Black Lung Benefits Act 1969 (30 U.S.C. §901) recognised coal workers' pneumoconiosis as a compensable occupational disease and established a federal compensation programme funded initially by miners and later by the coal industry's Black Lung Excise Tax. The Black Lung litigation wave of the 1970s–2000s, and its re-emergence in the 2010s driven by longer working hours and finer coal dust, demonstrated the catastrophic long-tail potential of occupational disease in WC.[L5]

1970–1990 — OSHA, National Commission & Experience Rating Matures

Federal Safety Regulation Arrives; NCCI Builds the Modern Rating System

The Occupational Safety and Health Act 1970 (OSHAct) was a watershed moment — the first comprehensive federal workplace safety statute, creating OSHA to promulgate and enforce safety standards across US industry. OSHAct did not change WC benefit structures, but it created an entirely new regulatory compliance obligation for employers and a major loss-prevention infrastructure that would ultimately reduce WC frequency.[L4]

The National Commission on State Workmen's Compensation Laws (1972 report) — mandated under OSHAct — found that most state WC benefit levels were woefully inadequate, that injured workers with permanent total disabilities often received less than poverty-level income, and that occupational diseases were drastically under-compensated. The Commission's 84 recommendations drove a wave of state benefit reforms through the 1970s that significantly increased claim costs — and required corresponding rate increases.[I1]

The NCCI (National Council on Compensation Insurance) developed and refined the experience modification factor (EMR) system during this era, creating the payroll-based premium rating system that remains the foundation of US WC pricing. The EMR — a multiplier based on 3 years of actual versus expected losses — gives the WC market its most powerful individual risk pricing tool, rewarding safe employers and penalising hazardous ones.

1990–2010 — Reform Waves, Managed Care & Fraud Surge

WC Cost Crisis Drives State Reforms; Managed Care Reshapes Medical Delivery

The late 1980s and early 1990s saw a severe WC cost crisis across multiple states — particularly California, Florida and New York — driven by benefit cost escalation, medical cost inflation, declining safety management, widespread fraud, and the entry of aggressive plaintiff attorney practices. Combined ratios in some states exceeded 130%. The WC insurance market hardened sharply in 1992–94.[I5]

States responded with sweeping reforms: managed care for WC medical (medical provider networks, UR, IME), anti-fraud legislation, benefit restructuring (California's 1993 reform, Florida's 1990 reform), and tightened disability definitions. Managed care dramatically reduced medical cost inflation for well-managed books, but sparked ongoing disputes over treatment approval and physician independence that continue today.

The September 11, 2001 WTC attacks generated one of the largest WC claims events in US history — approximately $2–3bn in WC claims for the 2,996 fatalities, injuries to first responders, and subsequent long-latency occupational disease claims by police, fire and other responders who developed cancers and respiratory diseases from WTC exposure. The WTC Health Program (Zadroga Act 2010) extended federal compensation for first responders, but many of these claims had already penetrated the private WC market.[I1]

2010–Present — Opioids, COVID, Inflation & the Gig Economy

Opioid Crisis Transforms WC Claims; COVID Presumption Laws; Platform Worker Classification

The opioid epidemic, which peaked in US overdose deaths around 2017, had its most severe WC impact from approximately 2008–2018. WC claimants were prescribed opioids at dramatically higher rates than the general population, driven by physician practice patterns and insurer/employer reluctance to challenge prescribing. RAND Institute research estimated opioid prescribing in WC added ~15–20% to claim costs at the height of the crisis.[I4] Most states have since enacted prescription drug monitoring programmes (PDMPs) and adopted ODG prescribing guidelines; the opioid-driven claim severity increase has substantially moderated since 2018.

COVID-19 generated significant WC exposure through presumption laws — statutes enacted by most states from 2020 onward that presume COVID-19 infection in certain worker categories (healthcare workers, first responders, essential workers) to be occupationally caused, reversing the burden of proof. The full cost of COVID WC claims has been significantly lower than initially feared, but reopening of disability reserves and long-COVID claims remain active issues through 2025.[I1]

Medical cost inflation in 2021–22 (post-COVID supply chain, staffing shortages, pharmaceutical prices) drove WC severity higher across all states. The gig economy presents an ongoing classification challenge: platform workers (rideshare drivers, delivery couriers) are typically classified as independent contractors and excluded from WC, but multiple state court decisions and legislative actions (California AB5) have contested this classification, with significant WC exposure implications. The Lloyd's USL&H market has also seen significant DBA exposure from US military contracting activity in the Middle East and Africa throughout this period.

Lloyd's USL&H Market Context: Lloyd's writes W6 (USL&H/DBA) as a specialist niche within the US casualty portfolio. The Lloyd's market is a significant provider of excess and surplus lines WC and USL&H coverage for high-hazard employers — offshore oil & gas, marine construction, dock operations — where admitted carriers are unwilling or unable to write. DBA coverage for US defence contractors in conflict zones has been a significant Lloyd's line since the post-9/11 military expansion, generating both large losses (combat deaths, traumatic brain injury) and complex coverage disputes over war exclusions and scope of employment.[I9]
Key Milestones
1904

Crystal Springs Brewery Case

Landmark negligence action exposing the inadequacy of employer defences — catalysed early WC reform debates in state legislatures.

1911

Wisconsin WC Act — The Grand Bargain

First comprehensive state WC statute. Established the no-fault strict liability model that became universal across the US.

1927

LHWCA Enacted

Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act creates federal WC for maritime workers on navigable waters — direct predecessor of Lloyd's W6 code.

1941

Defense Base Act

Extends LHWCA to US contractors on overseas military bases and government projects — creates DBA W6 exposure for US military contracting.

1970

OSHAct — Federal Safety Regulation

First comprehensive federal workplace safety law; OSHA created to set and enforce standards. Launched era of systematic WC loss prevention.

1972

National Commission Report

84 recommendations for WC reform; drove major state benefit increases throughout the 1970s and corresponding premium increases.

1990–1994

WC Cost Crisis & State Reforms

Combined ratios exceed 130% in major states; managed care, anti-fraud legislation and benefit reform waves transform the market.

2001

9/11 WTC — WC Mass Casualty

~$2–3bn in WC claims from fatalities, injuries and long-latency occupational disease among first responders. Largest US WC catastrophe event.

2008–2018

Opioid Crisis — Claim Cost Inflation

Opioid prescribing in WC adds est. 15–20% to claim costs at peak. Most states now have PDMPs and ODG prescribing guidelines.

2020

COVID Presumption Laws

Most states enact presumption statutes for healthcare workers and first responders; COVID WC costs moderate but long-COVID remains an active issue.
