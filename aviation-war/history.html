Era History
Pre-1939 — Early Aviation; No War Cover

Pioneers, Airships and the Absence of War Clauses

The earliest aviation insurance, written at Lloyd's in the 1910s and 1920s, was broad all-risks cover for a nascent industry. With no formal distinction between war and non-war perils in aviation policy wordings, early losses from WWI military aviation — predominantly biplanes and airships used for bombing and reconnaissance — were absorbed within all-risks or excluded entirely by insurers as uninsurable wartime risks.

The interwar period (1919–1939) saw rapid growth of commercial aviation — Imperial Airways, Lufthansa, KLM — and Lloyd's syndicates began developing dedicated aviation underwriting. Early aviation policies contained no specific war exclusion clause; the risk was simply that commercial aviation in wartime would cease and losses would be uninsurable. The conceptual separation of war and all-risks perils in aviation was not formally codified until WWII forced the issue. EST

1939–1945 — WWII: Aviation War Losses Crystallise as a Distinct Class

The War Forces Separation of War and All-Risks Perils

World War II destroyed or confiscated huge numbers of commercial aircraft operated by airlines across Europe, Asia and the Pacific. The destruction of the pre-war commercial fleet by military action — bombing of airports and aircraft, requisitioning of aircraft by governments, and loss to enemy fire — created enormous unresolved insurance disputes. The fundamental question emerged: does an aviation all-risks policy cover war losses?

Lloyd's wartime market addressed this by explicitly excluding war perils from all-risks aviation policies. Where governments requisitioned aircraft (effectively a confiscation), compensation was provided through government schemes rather than the insurance market. The practical result was that WWII aviation war losses were largely uninsured in the commercial market — but the conceptual framework for separately insuring war perils was established. VER

The post-war rebuilding of commercial aviation — with US aircraft manufacturers supplying airlines globally and new flag carriers emerging — created a structured aviation insurance market at Lloyd's. The distinction between all-risks (mechanical, weather, pilot error) and war perils (enemy action, confiscation) was now well understood by both underwriters and insureds.

1945–1970 — Cold War and the First Hijacking Wave

Korean and Vietnam War Losses; Cuba 1958 — Hijacking Begins

The Cold War era created new aviation war exposures. Civilian aircraft operating near conflict zones were occasionally intercepted or shot at; the shoot-down of a civilian aircraft over restricted airspace became a genuine risk. Korean War (1950–53) and Vietnamese conflict era airspace restrictions forced airlines to route around conflict zones, foreshadowing modern overfly risk management. The Lloyd's aviation war committee began developing market-wide approaches to conflict zone rating. VER

The modern era of aircraft hijacking began in 1958 with the first Cuban hijackings. By the 1960s, hijacking — typically for political asylum rather than ransom — became a regular occurrence in the Americas. The aviation war market began systematically including hi-jacking as a covered peril, recognising that the seizure of an aircraft by non-state actors was analogous to the war perils already covered. The Tokyo Convention 1963 provided the first international legal framework for offences committed on board aircraft in flight. VER

This era established the principle that aviation war cover should include both state-actor (government confiscation, military action) and non-state-actor (hijacking, terrorism) perils — a broadening of scope that remains the foundation of modern RX policies.

1970–1990 — Golden Age of Hijacking; AVN 48B Clause Development

Dawson's Field, Entebbe, Lockerbie — and the Codification of AVN 48B

The 1970s saw the peak of politically motivated hijacking by Palestinian groups, nationalists and ideological extremists. The PFLP (Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine) pioneered multiple simultaneous aircraft hijackings — Dawson's Field, Jordan, September 1970: four aircraft hijacked and three destroyed on the ground after passengers were evacuated. EST The insurance implications were significant: three aircraft written-off in a single coordinated act. The aviation war market paid hull war claims on all three aircraft.

The Entebbe crisis (June 1976) saw an Air France aircraft hijacked by Palestinian and German terrorists, flown to Uganda with the connivance of Idi Amin's government — a state-supported hijacking that blurred the line between non-state terrorism and state actor confiscation. The Israeli Defence Force's Operation Thunderbolt rescue damaged additional aircraft at Entebbe airport, raising questions about military action by a third state as an aviation war peril.

The bombing of Pan Am 103 over Lockerbie, Scotland (December 1988) — 270 killed — marked a new dimension of aviation terrorism. A bomb placed in checked luggage destroyed the aircraft in flight. Insurance recovery under hull war and liability war was substantial ($300m+ estimated). The event, attributed to Libyan state intelligence, again tested the boundary between non-state terrorism and state-sponsored attack — a distinction that matters for coverage and subrogation purposes. EST

Through the 1970s–80s, the Lloyd's aviation market codified the AVN 48B clause — the War, Hi-jacking and Other Perils Exclusion Clause — as the standard exclusion in all aviation all-risks policies. AVN 48B brought clarity and uniformity: all-risks policies excluded war perils systematically, and the aviation war policy (forerunner of modern RX) reinstated them. This two-policy structure has been the market standard ever since. VER

1990–2001 — Gulf War and the Calm Before the Storm

Gulf War 1991 Airspace Closures; Pre-9/11 Low-Frequency Market

The Gulf War (August 1990 – February 1991) demonstrated the materiality of airspace closure risk. The closure of Kuwaiti, Iraqi and parts of Saudi Arabian airspace disrupted international routes significantly. Several aircraft were destroyed or damaged. More importantly for the war market, the conflict demonstrated the speed with which a benign war risk environment could deteriorate — airlines and lessors found themselves with aircraft exposed in or near conflict zones without adequate insurance arrangements. EST

The 1990s market was characterised by relatively low aviation war losses and correspondingly soft rates. Terrorism was included within war cover as standard; the post-Cold War geopolitical environment seemed relatively benign. The market underpriced terrorism risk significantly — a structural mispricing that 9/11 would brutally expose. Airlines had little incentive to purchase high war limits; lessors were not systematically monitoring where their aircraft were deployed. The calm of the 1990s bred complacency.

Lloyd's RX Market — Pre-9/11 Structure: The aviation war market at Lloyd's was a small, specialist class written by a handful of syndicates with expertise in both the aviation all-risks and war sectors. Premium volumes were modest — estimated at well under $200m global GWP. Capacity was adequate for the exposures underwriters perceived; in retrospect, the market was entirely inadequately capitalised for a 9/11-scale loss. The event would reshape the class permanently. EST
2001–2014 — 9/11 and the Restructuring of Aviation War Coverage

9/11: $3.5bn Loss; Immediate Terrorism Cancellation; TRIA Required

The September 11, 2001 attacks — four commercial aircraft used as weapons destroying the World Trade Center and damaging the Pentagon — were the largest aviation war loss in history at the time. Estimated insured losses from hull war and liability war across the four aircraft exceeded $3.5bn. The liability war claims — wrongful death and injury claims from passengers and ground casualties — dwarfed the hull claims and tested aviation war limits to destruction. EST

Within hours of the attacks, Lloyd's aviation war underwriters exercised 7-day cancellation rights for terrorism coverage across fleet policies worldwide. The aviation war market had effectively exited terrorism coverage before the dust had settled. The consequence was an immediate coverage gap for airlines globally — no terrorism coverage was available at any price for several weeks. US domestic aviation temporarily could not operate with adequate war and terrorism insurance. VER

The US government responded with the Aviation and Transportation Security Act 2001 and eventually the Terrorism Risk Insurance Act (TRIA) 2002, which required federal backstop for certified acts of terrorism. The aviation market responded by separating terrorism from war: war cover was reinstated relatively quickly; terrorism cover required explicit government-backed reinsurance structures in the US market. VER

The post-9/11 decade saw hardened war rates, tightened policy conditions, and the systematic introduction of 48-hour cancellation provisions for individual aircraft operating in or over designated war zones (as distinct from the 7-day fleet-level cancellation). Afghanistan and Iraq created new overfly risk zones. MANPADS threats to aircraft below 15,000ft were taken seriously for the first time. The market slowly normalised through 2005–2014 as losses were manageable and geopolitical stability (relatively) returned.

2014 — MH17: Ukraine's First Aviation War Lesson

Malaysia Airlines MH17 — Shot Down by SA-11 Buk Missile

On 17 July 2014, Malaysia Airlines Flight MH17 — a Boeing 777 operating Amsterdam to Kuala Lumpur — was shot down over eastern Ukraine by an SA-11 Buk surface-to-air missile fired by Russian-backed separatists. All 298 people on board were killed. The Dutch Safety Board investigation (2015) confirmed the missile type and trajectory. The Joint Investigation Team (2019) attributed the missile to the 53rd Anti-Aircraft Missile Brigade of the Russian Armed Forces. EST

The insured loss — hull war (Boeing 777 agreed value ~$97m) plus liability war (wrongful death claims for 298 passengers under the Montreal Convention) — totalled an estimated $180m. The hull claim was straightforward; the liability war claims were more complex and developed over several years. MH17 galvanised the aviation industry's attention on the risks of flying over conflict zones: at the time of the shoot-down, dozens of airlines were routing over eastern Ukrainian airspace to save fuel and time. EST

Post-MH17, ICAO and IATA issued guidance on conflict zone overfly risk assessment. Ukraine airspace restrictions were imposed from 2014. Several major airlines proactively re-routed to avoid eastern Ukrainian airspace — a decision that proved prescient when Russia's full-scale invasion in February 2022 led to complete Ukrainian airspace closure. The MH17 loss was a pivotal moment: it demonstrated that political conflict zones create genuine aviation war exposures for commercial operators, not merely military aircraft.

2021–Present — Belarus Forced Landing, Russia Confiscation and the New War Paradigm

FR4978 Forced Landing 2021; Russia 2022 — $10–13bn Confiscation

On 23 May 2021, Ryanair Flight FR4978 (Athens to Vilnius) was intercepted by a Belarusian MiG-29 fighter and forced to land in Minsk. Journalist Roman Protasevich and his partner were arrested. The forced diversion of a commercial aircraft by a state military aircraft — with a false bomb threat used as a pretext — represented a new form of state-directed aviation interference sitting between hijacking and confiscation. The aviation war market examined whether the event triggered hi-jacking coverage or confiscation coverage, and whether Belarus had committed an act of war against the aircraft. EST

Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 triggered the largest confiscation event in aviation history. Western sanctions required aircraft lessors (AerCap, Air Lease Corporation, SMBC, GECAS and others) to terminate leases with Russian airlines and repossess their aircraft. Russian airlines, under Russian government direction, refused to return approximately 900 aircraft valued at $10–13bn. Russia passed legislation permitting Russian-registered aircraft to remain in Russian jurisdiction regardless of lessor ownership. VER

This was a textbook confiscation / expropriation scenario — exactly the peril that aviation war policies were designed to cover. Claims flooded into the Lloyd's market and international reinsurers. The total claimed amount ($10–13bn) dwarfed any previous aviation war loss and exceeded the total accumulated GWP of the global aviation war market for multiple years. As of May 2026, litigation continues in Irish, US, UK and Bermudian courts. Russia is not a party to the relevant international aviation conventions in a meaningful enforcement sense, and recovery prospects through Russian courts are negligible. VER

Lloyd's RX Market — Post-Russia 2022: The Russia confiscation event has fundamentally reset the aviation war market. Rates for confiscation and expropriation have hardened dramatically. Underwriters are scrutinising lessor clients' exposure to state-controlled carriers in sanctioned or politically unstable jurisdictions. The 7-day cancellability mechanism — which could not prevent confiscation losses already crystallised at the moment of sanctions — is being supplemented by more granular sanctions monitoring and lease structure requirements. The market continues to price and write aviation war cover but the Russia event has permanently raised awareness of confiscation as a catastrophic, correlated exposure. EST
Key Milestones
1963

Tokyo Convention

First international legal framework for offences on board aircraft in flight. Establishes state jurisdiction over hi-jacking offences and aircraft commander authority to restrain offenders.

1970

Dawson's Field — PFLP

PFLP hijacks four aircraft simultaneously; three destroyed on the ground in Jordan. Landmark aviation war loss; first major multi-aircraft coordinated war peril event for the insurance market.

1970

Hague Convention

Convention for the Suppression of Unlawful Seizure of Aircraft — establishes international criminal law framework for hi-jacking and obligates signatory states to prosecute or extradite offenders.

1976

Entebbe Crisis

Air France aircraft hijacked to Uganda; state-supported hi-jacking raises questions about government complicity as an aviation war peril. Israeli rescue operation damages aircraft at Entebbe.

1988

Lockerbie — Pan Am 103

Bomb destroys Boeing 747 over Lockerbie; 270 killed. $300m+ insured loss. Libya attributed. Defines aviation terrorism as a hull war and liability war exposure at catastrophic scale.

1990s

AVN 48B Codification

War, Hi-jacking and Other Perils Exclusion Clause becomes the universal standard in aviation all-risks policies globally — creating the structural demand for the separate aviation war (RX) policy.

2001

9/11 — $3.5bn War Loss

Four aircraft used as weapons; hull war and liability war claims exceed $3.5bn. 7-day cancellation of terrorism cover exercised immediately; TRIA required as government backstop. Market restructured permanently.

2014

MH17 — Ukraine Shoot-Down

Boeing 777 shot down over eastern Ukraine by Russian military Buk missile; 298 killed. ~$180m insured loss. Galvanises conflict zone overfly risk assessment across the industry.

2021

Ryanair FR4978 — Belarus

Belarusian MiG-29 forces commercial aircraft to land in Minsk; dissident arrested. State-directed aircraft interference tests hi-jacking vs confiscation coverage boundary under RX policies.

2022

Russia — 900 Aircraft Confiscated

~$10–13bn of leased aircraft stranded in Russia following invasion of Ukraine. Largest confiscation event in aviation history. Claims overwhelm war market; litigation ongoing across multiple jurisdictions as of 2026.
