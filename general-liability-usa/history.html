Era 1: Common Law Foundations and Industrial Liability (Pre-1940)

Trespass, Negligence, and the Rise of Products Liability

US liability law traces its origins to English common law negligence, but diverged markedly as American courts expanded liability doctrines to serve the needs of an industrialising economy. MacPherson v Buick Motor Co (1916, NY Court of Appeals — Judge Cardozo) was the foundational US products liability case: it held that a manufacturer could be liable to the ultimate consumer even without privity of contract, laying the groundwork for modern products liability. VER

Employers' liability — the predecessor to workers' compensation — was among the earliest systematic commercial liability insurance in the US. General accident and liability policies emerged in the late 19th century, primarily covering premises liability. By the early 20th century, manufacturers and retailers sought broader coverage against bodily injury claims from customers and third parties. EST

Era 2: The CGL Era and Strict Liability Expansion (1940–1970)

Strict Liability, Products Liability Expansion, and Surplus Lines Origins

1944SEC v NRMA — US Supreme Court rules that insurance is "commerce" subject to federal regulation under the Commerce Clause; Congress passes the McCarran-Ferguson Act 1945 returning primary insurance regulation to the states. The state-by-state regulatory structure of the US market is cemented. VER
1963Greenman v Yuba Power Products (California Supreme Court — Justice Traynor) — establishes strict liability for products: a manufacturer can be liable for a defective product even without proof of negligence. Adopted across most US states by the 1970s; fundamentally expands products liability exposure. VER
1966ISO introduces the modern standardised CGL policy form, separating premises/operations coverage from products/completed operations; introduces the occurrence trigger definition. Lloyd's and London market carriers begin significant US GL reinsurance. VER
1969Restatement (Second) of Torts §402A — the most influential US products liability standard; adopted widely by state courts; establishes defective product liability in three categories: manufacturing defect, design defect, failure to warn. VER

Surplus lines regulation also developed in this era: California's 1945 Insurance Code established the "diligent search" requirement before non-admitted placement, a concept that spread across all states. Lloyd's was among the first non-admitted carriers systematically writing US surplus lines, primarily for unusual manufacturers, oil companies, and high-hazard risks refused by admitted markets. EST

Era 3: The Asbestos Crisis and Market Disruption (1970–1990)

The Defining Catastrophe of US GL — Asbestos Litigation and Its Aftermath

1973Borel v Fibreboard Paper Products Corp (5th Cir.) — first major asbestos personal injury verdict; establishes the "failure to warn" liability of asbestos manufacturers; triggers the avalanche of asbestos litigation that would become the costliest tort in US history. VER
1977Asbestos claims begin reaching primary CGL and umbrella carriers in volume. The "continuous trigger" vs "exposure trigger" vs "manifestation trigger" debate in coverage litigation begins — which policies across multiple years respond to a disease with a latency period of 20–40 years? VER
1982Johns-Manville files Chapter 11 bankruptcy — the first major asbestos defendant to seek bankruptcy protection; establishes the model of asbestos bankruptcy trust (524(g) trust) that would be replicated by 100+ asbestos defendants. Lloyd's London market absorbs hundreds of millions in asbestos reinsurance losses. VER
1985Pollution exclusion "absolute" language — ISO introduces the absolute pollution exclusion in the CGL form, replacing the qualified pollution exclusion; driven partly by asbestos losses but also Superfund/environmental cleanup litigation. Begins decades of coverage litigation over scope of "pollution." VER
1986ISO revises CGL form to occurrence basis with split products/completed operations aggregate — standardises the "per-occurrence" limit and separate PCOH aggregate that remains the US CGL structure today. VER
1988Lloyd's US operations crisis: asbestos, pollution, and long-tail liability losses threaten Lloyd's solvency; reinsurance-to-close failures on multiple syndicates; "LMX spiral" exposed. Lloyd's global market reform process begins, eventually leading to Equitas (1996). VER

Asbestos litigation ultimately cost insurers an estimated $70–100bn in the United States alone. VER The market effect was profound: carriers introduced occurrence-year reinsurance programmes, extended reporting periods for long-tail risks became standard, and the absolute pollution exclusion became universal. The lesson — that GL policies could face catastrophic long-tail losses decades after policy issuance — permanently changed US GL pricing and reserving.

Era 4: Reform, Tobacco, and the Modern Tort System (1990–2005)

State Tort Reform, Tobacco MSA, and Emerging Mass Torts

1994Liebeck v McDonald's ("Hot Coffee" case) — $2.86m initial verdict (reduced to $640k); galvanises public debate on tort reform; becomes symbolic of "litigation lottery" but also illustrates that GL jury awards can far exceed expected damages in consumer product cases. VER
1994Contract with America / state tort reform wave — Republicans push for federal tort reform; multiple states introduce damages caps (economic loss only, punitive cap = 2× compensatory), venue reform, and expert testimony standards. Federal tort reform fails; state-level outcomes uneven. VER
1998Tobacco MSA (Master Settlement Agreement) — US tobacco manufacturers settle with all 50 state AGs for $206bn over 25 years. The largest private litigation settlement in US history. Establishes the template for state AG-driven mass tort resolution outside traditional class action — replicated for opioids, PFAS, and other state-level GL claims. VER
2002Federal Terrorism Risk Insurance Act (TRIA) — excludes terrorism from standard GL; creates federal backstop; Lloyd's and E&S market begin writing standalone terrorism GL as a separate product (later T-codes in Lloyd's). VER
2003Silica MDL — thousands of silicosis claims consolidated in Texas; federal judge Jack B. Weinstein finds mass diagnosis fraud; severely limits silica litigation. Demonstrates that mass tort aggregate exposures are not always fully realised. VER

The period also saw significant pharmaceutical mass torts: Vioxx ($4.85bn Merck settlement, 2007), hormone replacement therapy litigation (Wyeth/Pfizer, ~$1bn), and Fen-Phen diet drug (Wyeth, ~$21bn). Each demonstrated that products liability could generate systemic insurer losses from a single product line, driving GL underwriters to scrutinise pharmaceutical, medical device, and supplement manufacturers far more intensively. VER

Era 5: Financial Crisis, Construction Defect, and E&S Growth (2005–2018)

Construction Defect Boom, Post-GFC Soft Market, and Emerging PFAS/Cannabis

2005Katrina GL losses — Hurricane Katrina generates property and casualty losses; GL claims from contractor performance disputes, contractor negligence, and mould/pollution issues; E&S market provides specialty GL capacity post-Katrina where admitted market retreats. VER
2010Deepwater Horizon explosion (April 2010) — 11 killed; $65bn total cost (BP); largest offshore GL/pollution loss in history. Tests occurrence vs claims-made for ongoing pollution cleanup; establishes contractor GL liability for catastrophic offshore events. VER
2012Colorado and Washington legalise recreational cannabis — creates a new E&S GL market for cannabis operators (denied admitted coverage in most states due to federal illegality); Lloyd's and E&S market expand US commercial GL product for cannabis sector. VER
2015Construction defect boom — post-GFC construction recovery drives construction GL claim volumes; Chinese drywall litigation, inadequate fire suppression, and structural design errors generate major GL and umbrella losses in Florida, Nevada, and California construction markets. EST
2017Minnesota Department of Health v 3M — first major PFAS litigation seeking state remediation costs from a manufacturer; establishes the litigation template for the coming PFAS mass tort wave. VER

The E&S market grew significantly in this period: admitted markets tightened underwriting standards post-GFC, creating capacity gaps that wholesale brokers filled by accessing Lloyd's and US surplus lines markets. Total US E&S market GWP approximately doubled from ~$35bn to ~$65bn between 2010 and 2018. EST

Era 6: Social Inflation, Mass Torts, and Nuclear Verdicts (2018–Present)

The Defining Structural Challenge: Social Inflation, TPLF, PFAS, Opioids, and Talc

2018Roundup / Monsanto — first jury verdict ($289m, later reduced) holding Roundup (glyphosate) caused non-Hodgkin lymphoma; Bayer acquires Monsanto; faces 160,000+ claims; tentative settlement ~$11.6bn in 2020. Largest pesticide liability settlement; tests GL coverage for latent disease. VER
2019Opioid MDL — AmerisourceBergen, McKesson, Cardinal Health, and Johnson & Johnson global settlement ~$26bn; state AG-driven model (tobacco MSA template); tests "public nuisance" theory as GL coverage trigger. Purdue Pharma bankruptcy plan includes ~$6bn to Sackler family; creates asbestos-trust precedent for future mass tort defendants. VER
2021Boy Scouts of America (BSA) sexual abuse bankruptcy — $2.46bn settlement; largest SAM liability resolution; establishes 524(g) trust for childhood sexual abuse. SAM GL coverage in US becomes the defining emerging risk for UA/US markets. VER
20223M PFAS settlement ($10.3bn, 2023 finalisation) — largest PFAS settlement; public water system contamination; tests GL "occurrence" triggers for long-term contamination with continuous exposure. DuPont/Chemours PFAS global settlement $1.185bn also 2021. VER
2023J&J talc — LTL Management (J&J subsidiary) bankruptcy plan $8.9bn; talc-related mesothelioma and ovarian cancer claims. Uses "Texas Two-Step" divisive merger bankruptcy strategy — third-party litigation funding industry actively opposing. Tests the limits of mass tort resolution through corporate restructuring. VER
2024Nuclear verdict frequency at record levels — Swiss Re Institute reports US nuclear verdicts ($10m+) increased 27% 2020–2023 vs prior 3-year period. Average nuclear verdict $35m+. Third-party litigation funding estimated at $13bn+ US market, directly correlating with verdict inflation. VER

The current US GL market (2024–2026) is defined by social inflation as a systemic, structural cost driver — not a temporary cyclical development. Lloyd's and E&S underwriters are responding with rate increases, sub-limits for emerging exposures (PFAS, SAM, opioids), and explicit social inflation loading in pricing models. The US GL market is at an inflection point: admitted and non-admitted markets must continue hardening to remain profitable against a backdrop of structurally rising loss trends. EST

The Asbestos Lesson and PFAS Parallel: The asbestos litigation crisis (1970s–1990s) cost US GL carriers $70–100bn from policies written decades earlier, because no one appreciated the long-tail nature of latent disease claims at the time of underwriting. Today's equivalent is PFAS ("forever chemicals"): widespread environmental contamination, decades of exposure, unknown long-term disease latency, and a litigation template being actively developed by state AGs and plaintiff attorneys. Underwriters writing GL for manufacturers, chemical companies, military contractors, and public water utilities face PFAS exposure that may not crystallise into claims for another 10–20 years. The asbestos lesson demands that PFAS be identified, sub-limited, or excluded today — not after the claims arrive. EST
