History & Origins

From the coffee houses of Cornhill to the age of catastrophe models — the story of how commercial property insurance evolved through fire, earthquake, hurricane and pandemic to become the world's largest specialty insurance class.

Overview History Timeline Loss Database Underwriting Rates & ROE Risk Mitigation Global Programme References Rater Programme Builder

Commercial property insurance has a longer history than almost any other financial product. Its origins lie in the aftermath of catastrophic urban fires, its modern architecture was forged by the 1906 San Francisco earthquake, and its current complexity — cat models, cyber exclusions, climate scenarios — reflects five centuries of adaptation to an ever-changing risk landscape.

Era 1 · 1666–1720

Birth of Fire Insurance — The Great Fire of London

The Great Fire of London (2–5 September 1666) destroyed 13,200 houses and 87 churches across 373 acres of the City. VER No insurance existed; the losses were uncompensated. In 1667, Nicholas Barbon founded the Insurance Office for Houses — the world's first fire insurance company — offering cover on London properties in return for a fixed annual premium. Competitors followed rapidly: the Friendly Society (1684), Hand-in-Hand (1696) and the Sun Fire Office (1710), still operating today as part of RSA. VER

  • Early policies covered only fire — no storm, flood or theft; coverage was strictly material damage
  • Fire marks — metal plaques affixed to buildings — identified which company insured the property so private fire brigades would attend
  • Lloyd's coffee house opened in Tower Street in 1688, soon attracting marine underwriters; property business developed alongside over the next century
  • Fire of Hamburg 1842 (destroyed ~4,200 buildings): demonstrated cross-border aggregation risk and drove development of European fire insurance markets VER
Era 2 · 1720–1906

Industrial Growth — The Victorian Property Market

The industrial revolution created entirely new property risks: factories, warehouses, mills and railways on a scale never seen before. Steam-powered machinery introduced boiler explosion as a catastrophic new peril; large mills burning to the ground could wipe out an insurer's entire capital. The period saw systematic development of fire survey techniques, fire brigades (absorbed into municipal control from 1866 in the UK), and the first real actuarial analysis of fire statistics. EST

  • Royal Exchange Assurance (est. 1720) and London Assurance received Royal Charters as the first joint-stock insurance companies, restricting Lloyd's to individual underwriting
  • Manchester Fire Offices Committee (1858) — forerunner of tariff market; agreed rates for cotton mills to prevent destructive price competition VER
  • Factory Acts 1833–1878 drove awareness of industrial hazard; early property surveys introduced occupancy classification
  • Fire Insurance Tariff Association (UK, 1868): created the tariff system fixing minimum rates by construction class — dominated UK market until 1985 VER
  • Chicago Great Fire (1871): destroyed $200m of property; multiple US insurers became insolvent, triggering US state regulation of insurer solvency VER
Era 3 · 1906–1970

San Francisco — The Birth of Catastrophe Reinsurance

The San Francisco earthquake and fire of 18 April 1906 killed ~3,000 people and destroyed 28,000 buildings — total insured loss approximately $250m (1906) or ~$7bn in 2024 values. VER Several insurers refused to pay earthquake claims, citing fire-following-earthquake as the true peril under their policies. Others paid in full — including Lloyd's, whose prompt settlement (Cuthbert Heath famously instructed his agents to "pay all of our policyholders in full irrespective of the terms of their policies") cemented Lloyd's reputation for fair dealing and gave it enduring dominance in the US catastrophe market. VER

  • The event demonstrated that earthquakes cause fires on a massive scale — "fire following earthquake" became a distinct coverage item requiring specific underwriting
  • First generation of catastrophe reinsurance treaties developed in the US and London market post-1906
  • Lloyd's gained a permanent foothold in US surplus lines business that has never been relinquished
  • Palm Beach hurricane 1926 and Miami hurricane 1926 triggered first US wind-only property losses; Florida market established
  • UK tariff market continued to dominate domestic fire insurance; Lloyd's focused on US, marine and specialty risks
  • WWII bomb damage: first use of government reinsurance pools (Pool Re predecessor concept) for war perils
Era 4 · 1970–2001

Deregulation, Catastrophe Models and the London Market Spiral

The UK property tariff market was abolished in 1985, unleashing competitive pricing and driving significant rate reductions through the late 1980s. The London Market Spiral — a catastrophic reinsurance pricing distortion — reached its climax around the Piper Alpha disaster (1988) and the great storm of 1987/North Sea windstorm losses, eventually contributing to a near-systemic failure that required Lloyd's reconstruction in 1996 (Equitas). VER

  • Hurricane Hugo (1989, $10bn) and Oakland firestorm (1991, $1.7bn) began the modern era of large US cat losses
  • Hurricane Andrew (1992): $27bn insured loss — far exceeded any cat model estimate; 11 US insurers became insolvent; AIR Worldwide's models were recalibrated; the event launched modern probabilistic cat modelling VER
  • Northridge earthquake (1994): $44bn total loss, $15.3bn insured — State Farm paid more in claims than it had collected in California in its entire history; triggered the California Earthquake Authority (CEA) VER
  • European storms Lothar and Martin (1999): €13bn insured — demonstrated need for European windstorm cat modelling VER
  • ISR (Industrial Special Risks) wording: developed in Australia 1970s; adopted widely in Lloyd's market as the benchmark all-risks industrial property form
  • COPE data standardisation: Lloyd's Market Association (LMA) developed COPE data requirements for large commercial risks; RMS founded 1988; AIR Worldwide 1987 VER
Era 5 · 2001–2024

9/11, Katrina, COVID BI and Climate Change

The September 11 attacks (2001) caused an insured loss of ~$47bn — the largest man-made insured loss in history at that time. VER Property losses alone exceeded $11bn. The event triggered instant market hardening, the creation of the Terrorism Risk Insurance Act (TRIA) in the US and Pool Re's extension to non-property terrorism. It demonstrated that property and liability losses could aggregate simultaneously on an unprecedented scale.

  • Hurricane Katrina (2005): $85bn total, $36bn insured — largest-ever US insured event at the time; failures in NFIP, levee design and claims handling sparked regulatory overhaul VER
  • Thailand floods (2011): $43bn total loss, ~$12bn insured — exposed supply-chain BI aggregation; Japanese auto and electronics manufacturers suffered $20bn+ BI losses from flooded industrial zones in Chao Phraya basin VER
  • Hurricane Harvey (2017): $90bn total, $30bn insured — record rainfall event; NFIP strained; massive uninsured losses in Houston
  • COVID-19 BI test case: FCA v Arch Insurance (UKSC 2021) — Supreme Court ruled in favour of policyholders on many disease and hybrid wordings; ~£1.2bn UK industry payout; market rewrote all BI wordings to exclude communicable disease VER
  • Hurricane Ian (2022): $60bn insured — second-largest ever US event; Florida market crisis; Citizens Insurance exposure; AOB assignment of benefits litigation history
  • Turkey-Syria earthquake (February 2023): 50,000+ deaths; $7bn insured vs $90bn total — massive insurance gap in emerging markets VER
  • 2023 record: $118bn insured nat cat losses globally — third consecutive year above $100bn; severe convective storm (SCS) emerged as leading peril in US for attritional losses VER

The Long Arc: Key Structural Shifts in Commercial Property 1666–2024

  • Named perils → All risks: Victorian fire-only policies evolved to all-risks ISR forms by the 1970s; all-risks remains the dominant large-commercial basis
  • Material damage → BI: BI losses now frequently exceed MD on large losses; COVID forced complete re-examination of what constitutes a covered BI trigger
  • Manual survey → Cat models: Human fire survey gave way to RMS/AIR probabilistic models; Lloyd's RDS (Realistic Disaster Scenarios) framework mandated from 2001
  • Tariff → Free market: UK tariff abolished 1985; intense competition followed, contributing to Spiral losses; Lloyd's reconstruction 1996 cleared the decks
  • Physical perils → Intangible risks: Cyber physical damage (NotPetya 2017 — Mondelez v Zurich, settled 2022), pandemic BI, PFAS contamination are reshaping what counts as "property damage"
  • Climate change: Increasing frequency and severity of nat cat — especially secondary perils (SCS, wildfire, flood) — driving systemic repricing of cat-exposed risks globally
