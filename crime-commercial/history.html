Era History
Pre-1900 — Origins of Fidelity Bonding

Guaranteeing Trusted Employees: Fidelity Bonds Emerge

Commercial fidelity insurance originated from the practice of personal surety — a guarantor who would personally vouch for an employee's honesty. This was standard in finance, banking and trade from the medieval period. Lloyd's coffeehouse merchants provided early ad hoc crime cover from the 17th century, though fidelity as a formal product developed later.

The first formal fidelity guarantee companies appeared in Britain in the 1840s. The Guarantee Society (1840) and Guarantee and Accident Society (1842) pioneered corporate fidelity bonds, allowing employers to insure against employee theft without relying on personal sureties. This was revolutionary: it transferred the risk of dishonest employees from the social network of guarantors to the insurance market. VER

In the US, the American Surety Company (1884) and US Fidelity and Guaranty Company (1896) brought fidelity bonding to the rapidly industrialising American economy. Railway companies, banks and large retailers were early adopters, driven by the risks of managing large cashier and teller workforces at distance.

1900–1945 — Industrialisation and Standardisation

Commercial Fidelity Matures; Blanket Bonds Introduced

The growth of large industrial corporations — department stores, manufacturing conglomerates, utilities — created demand for blanket bonds covering all employees without individual schedules. The Commercial Blanket Bond (CBB) allowed employers to cover unknown employees on a blanket basis up to a single limit, a significant practical advance over position or name schedule bonds. VER

The 1929 Wall Street Crash and Great Depression exposed large-scale embezzlement across corporates and financial institutions. Many corporations discovered that trusted long-serving employees had used the chaotic environment to conceal substantial thefts. Insurers responded by tightening underwriting and developing clearer definitions of "employee" and "dishonest act."

During World War II, wartime production environments — with rapid workforce expansion, reduced supervision and strained internal controls — drove elevated fidelity claims. Post-war, the returning of normalcy to corporate controls brought a period of relative calm, but the lesson that internal control breakdowns enable fraud was well established.

1945–1980 — Computer Age Arrives; Forgery Fraud Evolves

EDP Crime Emerges; ISO Standardises Commercial Crime Forms

The arrival of computers in business operations created new fraud vectors not contemplated in original fidelity wordings. Early EDP (electronic data processing) crime took the form of employee manipulation of payroll systems, inventory records and accounts payable — exploiting the complexity and opacity of the new technology to conceal theft. EST

By the 1960s, "salami slicing" — the diversion of small rounding amounts from thousands of computer transactions — had emerged as a specifically computer-enabled fraud technique. Cheque kiting, float exploitation and computerised payroll padding were all identified and began appearing in loss reports.

The Insurance Services Office (ISO) standardised commercial crime forms through the 1960s–70s, creating the Commercial Blanket Bond and the Blanket Crime Policy that established the broad framework still in use today. The discovery basis trigger became the standard for commercial crime, replacing the older occurrence basis that had made it extremely difficult to attribute long-running embezzlements to specific policy years. VER

1980–2000 — Wire Fraud, RICO and the BEC Precursor

Electronic Funds Transfer Fraud; RICO Developments; Insider Trading

The growth of electronic funds transfer (EFT) systems through the 1980s created a major new crime vector. Fraudulent SWIFT and telex payment instructions, telephone fraud directing wire transfers, and early computer intrusion cases all tested the boundaries of commercial crime coverage. Underwriters debated whether computer-directed wire fraud was "computer crime" (covered) or simply theft by deception (not covered). VER

The US RICO (Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act 1970) had profound implications: large-scale employee fraud could now be prosecuted as organised crime, with treble damages and restitution orders. This made subrogation recovery more viable for insurers but also signalled the industrialisation of internal fraud.

The 1980s savings & loan crisis exposed massive insider fraud in commercial real estate lending — not strictly a BC claim (FIs used BB policies) but the risk management lessons around CEO-level fraud and board oversight directly influenced commercial crime underwriting standards for corporates. The era ended with the first internet-enabled frauds beginning to appear.

2000–2015 — Corporate Scandals; Social Engineering Emerges

Enron, WorldCom, and the Rise of BEC

The early 2000s corporate fraud scandals — Enron, WorldCom, Tyco, HealthSouth — were predominantly accounting fraud and securities fraud rather than commercial crime insurance claims. However, they dramatically raised awareness of the risk of senior management fraud ("above the bond" risk) and drove significant investment in internal controls, audit committee oversight and whistleblower programmes driven by the Sarbanes-Oxley Act 2002. VER

The ACFE (Association of Certified Fraud Examiners) began publishing its landmark Report to the Nations from 2002, providing the first systematic global data on occupational fraud. These reports became the definitive reference for commercial crime underwriters, establishing that a typical fraud lasts 14–18 months before detection and that small businesses (<100 employees) suffer proportionately higher losses than large corporates. EST

Business Email Compromise (BEC) fraud emerged around 2013 as a major commercial crime peril. The FBI first tracked it formally in 2015. Unlike earlier EFT fraud, BEC exploited human psychology rather than technical vulnerabilities — a fraudulent email impersonating the CEO or a known vendor convinces an accounts payable employee to make an urgent wire transfer. No hacking required; the employee acts voluntarily. Coverage disputes erupted over whether BEC losses triggered the "computer fraud" coverage or fell in a policy gap. EST

2015–Present — BEC Dominance; Deepfake Fraud; Lloyd's BC Market

BEC Becomes the Dominant Peril; AI-Enabled Fraud Emerges

BEC losses to US businesses reported to the FBI IC3 grew from $215m (2015) to $2.7bn (2022) — the single largest cybercrime category by financial loss. Courts grappled with whether commercial crime policies responded: the 2018 Medidata v Federal Insurance (2nd Circuit) and 2019 American Tooling Center v Travelers (6th Circuit) decisions reached opposite conclusions on nearly identical facts, creating a coverage fault line that persists today. VER

Social engineering endorsements became standard market additions from approximately 2016, typically as sublimit add-ons ($100k–$1m) rather than full policy limit extensions. The London market developed bespoke BEC/funds transfer fraud (FTF) coverage through Lloyd's syndicates that offered more generous sub-limits than admitted markets, driving significant BC premium growth. VER

By 2023–24, AI-generated deepfake audio and video fraud had emerged as the next frontier: in a high-profile 2024 case, a Hong Kong finance employee was deceived by a deepfake video call impersonating the company's CFO into transferring $25m. Commercial crime policies are being reviewed to confirm — or add — coverage for AI-enabled social engineering. The BC market is at an inflection point as the technology arms race between fraudsters and corporates accelerates.

Lloyd's BC Market Context: Lloyd's syndicates write commercial crime as a component of broader financial lines portfolios. The Lloyd's BC market is estimated at $300–500m GWP, primarily excess and surplus lines placement for US and international corporates with complex exposures. London market syndicates are known for flexibility on social engineering sublimits and multinational programme structure — areas where admitted markets are more restrictive. Crime aggregation (accumulation of BEC losses across large employer groups) is a growing portfolio management concern. EST
Key Milestones
1840

Guarantee Society Founded

First corporate fidelity guarantee company established in London, replacing personal surety with insurance-backed employee guarantees.

1896

US Fidelity & Guaranty Co.

USF&G founded, becoming one of America's leading fidelity bond providers for banks, retailers and manufacturers.

1929

Wall Street Crash Exposes Fraud

Great Depression reveals widespread corporate embezzlement; discovery basis trigger standardised to address long-running theft claims.

1974

ERISA Fidelity Bond Requirement

ERISA §412 mandates fidelity bonding for all persons handling employee benefit plan assets — creates a compulsory sub-market within commercial crime.

1986

ISO Commercial Crime Policy

Insurance Services Office publishes the standardised Commercial Crime Policy (CCP), unifying coverage across employee dishonesty, forgery, computer fraud and money & securities.

2002

Sarbanes-Oxley Act

SOX §302/404 mandates CEO/CFO certification of internal controls and independent audit committee oversight, directly reducing senior management fraud risk in public companies.

2002

ACFE Report to the Nations

First global occupational fraud study: median loss $90k, median duration 18 months. Becomes the definitive underwriting reference for commercial crime.

2013

BEC Emerges

FBI begins tracking Business Email Compromise as a distinct fraud category. Early cases involve supplier impersonation and CEO fraud directing urgent wire transfers.

2018

Medidata v Federal Insurance

2nd Circuit confirms computer fraud coverage responds to BEC. Creates circuit split with 6th Circuit's American Tooling Center decision (2019) — coverage uncertainty persists.

2024

Deepfake CFO Fraud — $25m Loss

Hong Kong employee transfers $25m after AI deepfake video call impersonating company CFO. Signals new era of AI-enabled commercial crime. Coverage reviews underway market-wide.
