Module 01 — History & Origins

From the first bankers blanket bond in 1916 to BEC, deepfake fraud and crypto crime in 2024

Overview History Timeline Loss Database Underwriting Rates & ROE Risk Mitigation Global Programme References Rater

Financial institution crime insurance has its roots in the oldest concerns of banking: that the people entrusted to handle other people's money might steal it. From medieval goldsmiths to modern investment banks, the problem of employee dishonesty has been a constant feature of financial services, driving a parallel market of fidelity guarantees, surety bonds, and — eventually — the modern composite Lloyd's BB policy. The history of the market is also the history of fraud itself, shaped by each new technology and each new opportunity for dishonesty that the financial system creates.

Era 1 · 1900–1950

Origins — The Bankers Blanket Bond & Fidelity Guarantee

Before the modern composite crime policy existed, banks managed fidelity exposure through two separate mechanisms: individual fidelity bonds (which named each employee and required separate underwriting) and employer's liability actions against dishonest staff. Both were cumbersome. Named-schedule bonds were expensive to administer as staff numbers grew; legal action against dishonest employees was slow and rarely recovered full losses.

The American Bankers Association (ABA) worked with the surety industry in the early twentieth century to develop a standardised composite solution. The result was the Bankers Blanket Bond (BBB), formally introduced in its modern composite form in 1916. The bond "blanketed" all employees without naming them and consolidated the principal crime exposures into a single instrument: employee dishonesty, on-premises loss, in-transit loss, forgery, and securities. VER

The Lloyd's market entered the FI crime space in the 1920s and 1930s, initially as a reinsurer of American surety companies and later as a direct underwriter through specialist syndicates. The London market's flexibility — particularly its ability to write unusual or large risks on a subscription basis — made it attractive for large US and international banks seeking capacity beyond the domestic surety market.

  • 1916: American Bankers Association Bankers Blanket Bond — standardised composite FI crime policy VER
  • 1920s: Lloyd's syndicates begin writing FI fidelity as reinsurers of US surety companies
  • 1933: US Banking Act (Glass-Steagall) separates commercial and investment banking — creates distinct FI risk profiles for each sector
  • 1930s: Great Depression exposes widespread bank embezzlement; fidelity bond claims surge; underwriters tighten terms
  • 1940: US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) begins requiring fidelity bonds for registered investment companies under the Investment Company Act 1940 VER
  • Post-WWII: UK clearing banks begin adopting blanket bond-style cover through Lloyd's; previously reliant on staff guarantee societies
Lloyd's Advantage in FI Crime: Unlike domestic US surety companies bound by state regulation and rate filings, Lloyd's syndicates could write bespoke terms, offer higher limits, and respond faster to emerging risks. This flexibility allowed Lloyd's to dominate the large and complex end of the global FI crime market from the 1950s onwards.
Era 2 · 1950–1985

Computerisation & the First Electronic Crime Exposures

The post-war decades saw the rapid computerisation of banking. The introduction of mainframe computers to process transactions, the early SWIFT network (launched 1973), and the growth of credit card processing created entirely new categories of financial crime that the original BBB wording did not contemplate. VER

The first significant wave of computer-related bank fraud emerged in the 1960s and 1970s: salami slicing (routing fractions of cents from many transactions to a fraudster account), round-down fraud, and manipulation of batch processing to create phantom credits. These acts were committed by bank employees with privileged access to systems — precisely the kind of loss covered by the employee dishonesty insuring agreement, but which the original wording had not anticipated.

Lloyd's underwriters and the American surety market responded by adding specific computer systems fraud endorsements to the BBB in the 1970s and early 1980s. These were initially sub-limited and narrowly worded, covering only losses arising from fraudulent instructions entered directly into the insured's own computers — a definition that would prove inadequate as networks became interconnected.

  • 1950s: IBM mainframe adoption by major US banks; first batch-processing frauds by insider employees
  • 1973: SWIFT network launched — interbank messaging begins its journey toward becoming the primary global payments backbone VER
  • 1974: CHIPS (Clearing House Interbank Payments System) operational — large-value US dollar clearing; new fraud vector
  • 1977: First documented significant computer fraud case in banking — Rifkin/Security Pacific ($10.2m wire fraud via impersonation) VER
  • 1979: Stan Rifkin convicted — underwriters begin drafting computer crime endorsements
  • Early 1980s: Computer crime endorsements added to BBB wordings; initially sub-limited to $1–5m EST
  • 1983: FINRA predecessor (NASD) formalises fidelity bond requirements for broker-dealers — minimum $25,000 VER
Era 3 · 1985–2000

Rogue Traders, S&L Crisis & Market Stress

The 1980s and 1990s were characterised by two dominant themes: the systemic failure of the US Savings & Loan (S&L) industry — in which endemic fraud played a significant role — and the emergence of the rogue trader as a new archetype of FI crime. Both would reshape the BB market.

The S&L crisis (1986–1995) resulted in the failure of over 1,000 US thrift institutions at a cost to the US government of approximately $160bn. The FDIC's investigation found that insider abuse and outright fraud was a material factor in the failure of a significant proportion of failed institutions. Lloyd's and the US surety market faced substantial fidelity claims as a result. Underwriters responded with tighter controls requirements, lower limits for troubled institutions, and more aggressive subrogation. VER

The rogue trader era began with the collapse of Barings Bank in 1995. Nick Leeson, a derivatives trader in Singapore, accumulated £827m in unauthorised losses through concealed trading positions in a hidden account (88888), bringing down Britain's oldest merchant bank. The loss exceeded Barings' entire equity capital. Lloyd's FI crime underwriters paid substantial claims, though coverage disputes centred on whether Leeson's acts constituted manifest dishonesty (as opposed to reckless trading) under the employee dishonesty insuring agreement. VER

  • 1984–1989: US S&L crisis — fraud identified in significant proportion of failed institutions; fidelity bond claims surge VER
  • 1987: BCCI fraud begins to unfold — $13bn fraud across 70 countries; complex multinational FI crime coverage issues VER
  • 1991: BCCI collapse — Lloyd's market faces complex multi-jurisdictional FI crime claims
  • 1994: Daiwa Bank — Toshihide Iguchi ($1.1bn unauthorised bond trading, New York) VER
  • 1995: Barings Bank collapse — Nick Leeson, £827m loss; landmark rogue trader event for Lloyd's BB market VER
  • 1995: Sumitomo copper scandal — Yasuo Hamanaka, $2.6bn unauthorised copper futures trading VER
  • 1996: Allied Irish Banks — John Rusnak, $691m forex trading fraud (discovered 2002) VER
  • Late 1990s: Internet banking launched; underwriters begin to consider e-commerce crime exposures
Post-Barings market response: Underwriters introduced more rigorous questions on trading controls, position limits, and reconciliation procedures. Many Lloyd's BB wordings were amended to clarify that rogue trading losses were covered only where manifest dishonest intent to benefit personally could be demonstrated — a response to arguments that unauthorised but not self-enriching trading was covered.
Era 4 · 2000–2015

Internet Era, Cyber-Enabled Fraud & Major Rogue Trader Events

The 2000s brought a second wave of rogue trader events alongside the rapid expansion of internet-enabled bank fraud. The global financial crisis of 2008 added a new dimension: market stress exposing frauds that had been concealed during the boom years, most notably the $65bn Madoff Ponzi scheme. VER

Jérôme Kerviel at Société Générale accumulated €49.9bn in unauthorised positions in European equity index futures, generating a loss of €4.9bn when the positions were unwound in January 2008 — the largest single trading fraud in history at that date and a €7.1bn loss when including related hedging costs. VER The SocGen loss drove a significant reassessment of controls requirements in BB underwriting: underwriters began requiring detailed evidence of position limits, trader monitoring systems, and management information flows before binding large FI crime limits.

Simultaneously, online banking fraud grew rapidly. Phishing attacks, malware targeting banking credentials, and Man-in-the-Browser (MitB) attacks were draining millions from retail and SME banking customers. Underwriters debated whether losses from online banking fraud were covered under computer crime insuring agreements — coverage disputes that would foreshadow the later BEC coverage battles.

  • 2001: 9/11 — operational resilience of FIs tested; underwriters add business continuity controls questions
  • 2002: Allied Irish Banks/Rusnak loss discovered — $691m forex fraud spanning 5 years VER
  • 2003: First major phishing attacks on banking credentials; early MitB malware EST
  • 2008: SocGen/Kerviel — €4.9bn (€7.1bn total) rogue trading loss; largest single FI crime event to date VER
  • 2008: Madoff Ponzi scheme — $65bn investor fraud; fidelity/crime coverage questions for feeder fund managers VER
  • 2011: UBS / Kweku Adoboli — $2.3bn unauthorised trading, Delta One desk Geneva VER
  • 2012: JPMorgan "London Whale" — Bruno Iksil, $6.2bn credit derivatives trading loss VER
  • 2013: LIBOR rigging scandal — multiple global banks; regulatory fines exceed $9bn globally VER
  • 2014: BEC (Business Email Compromise) first formally identified by FBI; early FI victims include payment departments at banks VER
  • 2015: FDIC survey shows 10% of US community banks experienced employee fraud in prior 3 years EST
Era 5 · 2015–2024

Cyber-Enabled Crime, BEC, Deepfakes & Crypto

The period from 2015 to 2024 has been defined by a fundamental shift in the nature of FI crime: the transition from primarily insider threats (employee dishonesty, rogue trading) to hybrid cyber-enabled crime in which external actors exploit technology to impersonate insiders, breach systems, or manipulate authorised staff into making fraudulent payments.

The Bangladesh Bank SWIFT heist of February 2016 was the watershed event. North Korean hackers (attributed to the Lazarus Group) compromised Bangladesh Bank's SWIFT credentials and submitted fraudulent payment orders totalling nearly $1bn to the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. $81m was successfully transferred before the fraud was detected. VER The event prompted SWIFT to launch the Customer Security Programme (CSP), requiring member institutions to meet mandatory security controls. It also forced Lloyd's BB underwriters to address a critical coverage gap: many computer crime insuring agreements specified that fraudulent instructions must be entered directly into the insured's computer — but the SWIFT instructions were sent through the legitimate SWIFT interface.

Business Email Compromise (BEC) became the dominant FI crime vector from 2016–2024. FBI IC3 data shows BEC losses to US victims exceeding $50bn in aggregate from 2013–2023. VER Financial institution treasury and payments departments became prime targets. Coverage battles erupted between insureds and underwriters over whether BEC losses triggered the computer crime insuring agreement (requires unauthorised computer access) or the social engineering insuring agreement (requires specific sub-limit endorsement). Courts in multiple jurisdictions issued conflicting rulings.

  • 2016: Bangladesh Bank SWIFT heist — $81m stolen; Lazarus Group attributed; SWIFT CSP launched VER
  • 2016: First court rulings on BEC and computer crime coverage — Medidata v Federal Insurance; courts split VER
  • 2016–2018: Series of SWIFT-enabled bank heists across Asia, Africa, Latin America; aggregate losses ~$200m+ EST
  • 2017: WannaCry/NotPetya ransomware — FI operational disruption; crime vs. cyber policy boundary debated
  • 2019: First "deepfake audio" BEC fraud — UK energy company CEO voice impersonation; €220,000 wired VER
  • 2020: COVID-19 pandemic — mass remote working; surge in BEC and internal fraud; FI crime losses up ~30% EST
  • 2021: Ransomware attacks on Colonial Pipeline, JBS — underwriters draw war/cyber-crime boundary for FI cover
  • 2022: FTX collapse — $8bn customer funds misappropriated; crypto exchange fidelity/crime coverage questions VER
  • 2023: MGM/Caesars social engineering attacks — vishing (voice phishing) used; FI sector on alert VER
  • 2024: Deepfake video BEC — Hong Kong finance worker transfers $25m after video call with synthetic CFO VER
  • 2024: UK PSR mandatory APP fraud reimbursement — payment firms face new regulatory crime-adjacent liability
2024 State of Play: The Lloyd's BB market in 2024 faces a tripartite challenge: (1) defining coverage boundaries between computer crime, social engineering, and cyber liability policies; (2) underwriting the rapid growth of fintech and crypto exchange clients; (3) managing accumulation risk as BEC fraud becomes near-ubiquitous across all FI segments. Premium rates hardened significantly 2019–2022 (+40–80% across the FI crime market EST), with some moderation from 2023 as new capacity entered.
