Landmark Cases — Insurance Significance

The following cases have materially shaped Lloyd's BB underwriting practice, policy interpretation, and coverage design for FI crime risks. VER

Barings Bank / Nicholas Leeson — Singapore 1995

Loss: £827 million. Leeson, a rogue trader based in Singapore, concealed losses in an error account (account 88888) via fictitious trades. Lack of segregation of duties — Leeson ran both the trading desk and the back office — was the critical control failure. The collapse of Barings (founded 1762) triggered a Lloyd's market review of FI crime underwriting standards and mandatory segregation of duties requirements for BB cover.

Insurance outcome: Insurers disputed coverage on grounds that losses arose from employee dishonesty intended to benefit the bank (meeting its targets) rather than Leeson personally at the time losses were initiated. Subsequent legal proceedings and settlement — exact insurance recovery amount not public. Established that ongoing concealment of losses (not initial dishonest intent) can satisfy the manifest intent test. VER

Société Générale / Jérôme Kerviel — Paris 2008

Loss: €7.1 billion (largest single rogue trader loss in history to that date). Kerviel, a junior trader, built up unauthorised long positions of €50bn nominal value in European equity futures using fictitious hedges entered in the bank's own systems. SocGen's BB insurers (led by Lloyd's) faced a claim. Key coverage question: did Kerviel's acts have manifest intent to cause loss to SocGen? Kerviel's defence was that management knew — this would undermine the dishonesty element.

Insurance outcome: SocGen settled with insurers for reportedly around €100–150m (vs. €7.1bn loss), reflecting coverage limitations on the dishonesty trigger and the deductible structure. The case prompted Lloyd's syndicates to tighten manifest intent and financial benefit definitions in BB policy wordings. EST

Bernard Madoff Ponzi Scheme — New York 2008

Loss: $64.8 billion (stated investor account values); actual cash losses estimated ~$17bn. Madoff operated the largest Ponzi scheme in history through his BMIS broker-dealer. Feeder funds and institutional investors (hedge funds, family offices, charities, banks) placed assets. The question for BB insurers of feeder funds: did the fund's own employees act dishonestly, or were funds passively invested (no employee element)?

Insurance outcome: Most feeder funds did not recover under BB — the dishonesty was Madoff's, not the fund's own employees. D&O claims against feeder fund directors and managers were more productive. Established the critical distinction: BB covers employee dishonesty of the insured entity — if the fraud is perpetrated by an external third party, BB does not respond. VER

Medidata Solutions Inc v Federal Insurance Co — 2nd Circuit 2018

Loss: ~$4.8 million. BEC attack: fraudster spoofed emails from Medidata's President to finance employees, directing wire transfers. Federal Insurance (Chubb) denied coverage under the computer fraud clause, arguing no "computer violation" as employees voluntarily authorised the transfers. District court found for insurer; Second Circuit reversed.

Ruling (2nd Cir. 2018): The fraudulent emails were transmitted via computer, which used Medidata's computer systems; this was sufficient to trigger the computer fraud clause even though employees voluntarily acted on the instructions. Significantly: established that BEC losses can be covered under existing computer crime clauses in BB/crime policies without requiring a specific social engineering endorsement — directly influenced Lloyd's BB wordings post-2018. VER

Bangladesh Bank SWIFT Heist — 2016

Loss: $81 million (of $951m attempted). Attackers (attributed to Lazarus Group, DPRK) compromised Bangladesh Bank's SWIFT terminal via malware planted on the bank's network; sent 35 fraudulent SWIFT payment instructions to the Federal Reserve Bank of New York; $81m transferred to Philippines before stopped by a spelling error. Bangladesh Bank's BB insurer faced a computer crime claim.

Insurance significance: Established that SWIFT compromise falls within computer crime cover where the system access was unauthorised; SWIFT CSP was not yet mandatory in 2016 — the heist directly caused SWIFT to introduce the Customer Security Programme (CSP) and Lloyd's underwriters to require SWIFT CSP attestation as a condition of computer crime cover from 2017 onwards. VER

UBS / Kweku Adoboli — London 2011

Loss: $2.3 billion. Adoboli, a trader in UBS's Delta One desk in London, concealed fictional hedging trades via the same system vulnerabilities exposed in the Leeson case — fictitious forward-settling trades that disguised real risk positions. Sentenced to seven years' imprisonment.

Insurance outcome: UBS held substantial internal limit; BB recovery reported but not public. Key underwriting lesson: rogue trader losses in Delta One / synthetic ETF desks are a systematic risk category; underwriters now require specific disclosures on Delta One desk controls, position reconciliation frequency, and independent risk oversight for institutions with large equity derivatives books. EST

Material Data Gaps

The following data gaps represent areas where public information is insufficient to support verified figures. Future versions of this hub will update these sections as information becomes available. GAP

GAP 1 — Lloyd's BB Crime GWP Granularity

Lloyd's publishes aggregate GWP by broad class (Property, Casualty, Specialty, Marine, etc.) but does not publish a specific breakdown isolating Financial Institutions Crime / Fidelity (BB) from broader crime or financial lines. The ~$3.5bn global market estimate used in this hub is a composite broker estimate. A definitive public Lloyd's BB GWP figure does not exist. This gap affects the rates analysis module accuracy.

GAP 2 — BB Insurance Recovery Data on Major Rogue Trader Losses

Actual insurance recovery amounts paid under BB policies for major rogue trader losses (Barings, SocGen, UBS/Adoboli, London Whale) are either confidential settlement figures or undisclosed. Public reporting covers judicial proceedings but not insurance settlements. The coverage disputes and settlement outcomes referenced in the landmark cases section are based on partial press reporting and academic literature, not disclosed insurer accounts.

GAP 3 — Lloyd's BB Loss Ratio Time Series

Lloyd's does not publish class-specific loss ratios for the BB sub-class. The rates analysis module loss ratio series (2010–2024) is constructed from aggregate Lloyd's results, broker market intelligence, and ACFE loss data. The ±10–15 percentage point uncertainty band on the estimated loss ratios is material for underwriting calibration purposes. Syndicate-level actual loss ratios are available to Lloyd's managing agents but not published.

GAP 4 — BEC / Social Engineering Insurance Claim Population

While the FBI IC3 reports aggregate BEC losses ($2.9bn US 2023), this covers all industries and does not isolate FI-sector BB insurance claims. The proportion of BEC losses that result in insured recovery vs. uninsured loss is unknown from public data. Coalition and Beazley cyber claims reports give partial data for cyber policies but do not cover BB crime policies. A robust BEC claim frequency/severity study for the FI BB market does not exist in the public domain.

GAP 5 — DORA Impact on FI Crime Insurance Pricing

The EU Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) came into force January 2025. The impact on Lloyd's BB underwriting for EU-regulated financial institutions — specifically whether DORA compliance reduces computer crime loading, alters SWIFT CSP requirements, or creates new insurable obligations — is not yet established by market practice or published underwriting guidance. This hub will be updated as the DORA impact on BB pricing becomes clearer through the 2025–2026 renewal cycle.
