Era History
Pre-1900 — Marine Origins and Industrial Boiler Insurance

Boiler Explosions and the Birth of Engineering Insurance

The earliest engineering insurance emerged not from construction but from the hazard of steam boilers. As the Industrial Revolution drove the proliferation of steam-powered machinery throughout Britain and continental Europe, catastrophic boiler explosions became a regular — and deadly — feature of factory life. The Manchester Steam Users' Association, founded in 1855, began offering boiler inspection services combined with insurance against explosion losses — the first recognisable engineering insurance product. EST

The concept was straightforward but radical: a specialist engineer would inspect and certify a boiler, and the association would indemnify losses arising from explosion. This combination of physical inspection with financial indemnity became the hallmark of engineering insurance and distinguishes it from other property lines to this day. Early fire insurance policies specifically excluded explosion and machinery breakdown, creating a clear market gap that the boiler insurers filled.

Marine all-risks insurance provided an early conceptual ancestor for contractors' risks — the idea that a single policy could cover multiple perils on a defined project. Lloyd's underwriters had long covered ship construction and cargo in transit, and the principles were adapted for land-based construction as civil engineering projects grew in scale through the Victorian era. Railway construction — the dominant engineering project of the 19th century — created early demand for all-risks construction covers.

1900–1945 — Rapid Industrialisation, Dams and Early CAR

Contractors Insurance Emerges at Lloyd's

The Edwardian and interwar periods saw rapid industrialisation and the construction of large-scale infrastructure: bridges, dams, power stations, and — increasingly — reinforced concrete structures that posed new and complex risks. The Hoover Dam (completed 1936) and similar mega-projects in the United States, Scandinavia and colonial territories illustrated the scale of exposure that conventional fire-only policies could not address. ILLUS

The first recognisable Contractors All Risks policy at Lloyd's is generally traced to the early 1920s, developed to cover major civil engineering contracts where the principal wished to insure the value of the works under construction against all accidental physical damage. Early CAR policies were bespoke manuscript wordings, individually negotiated for each project. The concept of a "contract works" section (the permanent structure) combined with "contractors' plant" (equipment) and "third-party liability" in a single policy was the critical innovation. VER

Swiss Re and Munich Re were early leaders in the reinsurance of engineering risks, developing specialist engineering departments that provided technical capacity and scientific expertise rarely found in the direct market. The engineering reinsurance market became a major exporter of technical knowledge, underwriting models and policy wordings to local markets worldwide.

1945–1970 — Post-War Reconstruction and Policy Development

CAR Formalised; Swiss Re and Munich Re Lead Technical Development

Post-war reconstruction created an unprecedented boom in construction across Western Europe, Japan and the colonial developing world. The Marshall Plan, NATO infrastructure programmes, and the British New Towns initiative generated massive volumes of construction activity. Lloyd's engineering syndicates and the Munich Re/Swiss Re duopoly expanded rapidly to meet demand, refining CAR policy wordings to address the full range of construction perils encountered. VER

The 1950s and 1960s saw the formalisation of key coverage concepts: the distinction between the construction period and the maintenance period (typically 12 months post-completion), the role of the third-party liability section, and the beginnings of Delay in Start-Up (DSU) cover for revenue-generating infrastructure. Nuclear power station construction — beginning in earnest in the UK and USA in the late 1950s — created extreme engineering insurance challenges: enormous values concentrated at a single site over multi-year programmes.

Erection All Risks (EAR) emerged as a distinct product during this period, driven by the installation of large-scale mechanical and electrical plant — power generation turbines, refinery columns, petrochemical equipment. EAR required specialist technical knowledge of machinery installation and testing risks that differed significantly from civil engineering CAR risks. Swiss Re RE 620 (the Munich Re engineering wording) became an influential standard across the market. EST

1970–1990 — North Sea, Nuclear and the LEG Drafting Committee

Offshore Construction, Mega-Projects and LEG Clause Innovation

The North Sea oil and gas exploration and production boom of the 1970s created an entirely new category of engineering insurance: offshore construction. Jacket installation, subsea pipelines, platform construction in hostile environments, and the logistical complexity of marine construction operations pushed existing CAR/EAR wordings to their limits. Lloyd's marine and energy syndicates developed bespoke offshore construction covers that merged marine hull principles with onshore engineering insurance. VER

Nuclear power station construction — Dungeness B, Hinkley Point A, Heysham — presented extraordinary engineering insurance challenges. Policy aggregations of hundreds of millions of pounds at single sites, novel technology, extended construction periods (5–10 years), and the potential for DSU losses in a sector with highly predictable revenue streams drove sophisticated insurance programme development.

The 1980s saw the most important structural development in CAR/EAR insurance history: the formation of the London Engineering Group (LEG) and the drafting of the LEG clauses to define defects coverage. Industry disputes over how CAR policies should respond to losses caused by design defects, defective materials or workmanship had generated substantial litigation. The LEG clauses (LEG 1, LEG 2, LEG 3) provided a standardised menu of coverage options that clarified the market position and reduced coverage uncertainty. EST

Munich Re's engineering division published technical guidelines on construction risk assessment that became industry standards. The development of JCT (Joint Contracts Tribunal) and ICE (Institution of Civil Engineers) standard contract forms in the UK brought greater clarity to insurance requirements and indemnity structures. FIDIC's Red Book (first published 1957; revised 1987) established the international standard for engineering contract insurance requirements. VER

1990–2010 — PFI, Mega-Projects and European Flood Losses

Three Gorges Dam, Eurotunnel and PPP Infrastructure Drive

The 1990s saw the rise of Private Finance Initiative (PFI) and Public-Private Partnership (PPP) infrastructure procurement. Governments privatised the financial risk of major infrastructure projects, requiring sophisticated insurance programmes combining CAR, DSU, and in some cases property and liability covers into complex layered towers. The Eurotunnel (Channel Tunnel, completed 1994) was the largest single PFI project of the era, with insurance programmes running into hundreds of millions of pounds. EST

The Three Gorges Dam in China (under construction 1994–2012) was the largest civil engineering project in history by contract value, requiring innovative insurance solutions that blended Chinese domestic requirements with international reinsurance capacity. Chinese mega-projects — airports, high-speed rail, hydroelectric schemes — drove significant growth in international engineering reinsurance.

European flood events in the late 1990s and early 2000s (Rhine floods 1995, Elbe floods 2002) generated significant CAR losses on infrastructure projects, reinforcing NatCat as the primary driver of large construction claims. The Czech and German flood losses of 2002 hardened the market temporarily and introduced stricter flood zone requirements into CAR underwriting.

Lloyd's Engineering Market Context: The Lloyd's engineering market writes CB (CAR) and CC (EAR/MB) across a range of syndicates with dedicated engineering underwriters. Estimated Lloyd's engineering GWP is £2–3bn (est.) across all sub-classes. London market is particularly active in large international project business, offshore construction, and specialist EAR for power generation and process industries. Lloyd's remains a lead market for complex construction programmes where primary insurers seek London capacity and technical expertise. VER
2010–Present — Renewables, COVID Delays and NatCat Hardening

Offshore Wind, Data Centres and the Turkey Earthquake 2023

The construction boom in renewable energy infrastructure — particularly offshore wind — has transformed the engineering insurance market since 2010. Offshore wind farm construction involves the installation of monopile foundations, towers, nacelles and blades in challenging marine environments, with significant marine construction risk, weather-window sensitivity, and high-value T&C exposure. Losses during installation and commissioning have been a consistent source of EAR claims. VER

Data centre construction emerged as a major engineering insurance category from approximately 2015, driven by cloud computing and digitalisation. Data centre projects feature extreme concentrations of value, complex M&E systems, critical cooling infrastructure, and demanding commissioning periods. The interface between CAR and technology insurance (for pre-installed equipment) is a key coverage boundary issue.

The COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2022) caused unprecedented disruption to construction projects worldwide. Supply chain failures, labour shortages, site closures and delayed material deliveries generated major schedule extensions and cost overruns. DSU claims arose where physical damage had also occurred; delay-only claims (without physical damage) were generally not covered under CAR/EAR policies. The pandemic exposed the limits of construction insurance in addressing pure delay risk. EST

The Kahramanmaras earthquake sequence in Turkey (February 2023) was the largest natural catastrophe event affecting engineering insurance in the 21st century to date. Estimated insured losses across ongoing construction projects, operational plant and infrastructure exceeded $6bn, triggering significant reserve strengthening and market hardening for earthquake-exposed regions. The event reinforced the importance of NatCat zone pricing and drove review of earthquake sub-limits in CAR programmes. EST

Key Milestones
1855

Manchester Steam Users' Association

First specialist engineering insurance combining boiler inspection with indemnity — the founding model for engineering insurance as a distinct class.

~1920

First Lloyd's CAR Policy

First recognisable Contractors All Risks policy issued at Lloyd's for a major civil engineering contract, covering contract works, plant and third-party liability in one document.

1957

FIDIC Red Book Published

First FIDIC Conditions of Contract for Construction published, establishing international standard insurance requirements (Clause 18/21) still referenced in programmes today.

1974

North Sea Offshore Construction Boom

Rapid expansion of North Sea offshore construction creates a new sub-class of engineering insurance, merging marine and EAR principles for platform and subsea construction.

~1985

LEG Clauses Established

London Engineering Group (LEG) drafts LEG 1, 2 and 3 defects coverage clauses — the most important standardisation development in CAR/EAR history, resolving major coverage disputes.

1994

Eurotunnel Opens

Channel Tunnel, the largest privately financed construction project to that date, exemplifies the complexity of PFI engineering insurance programmes — CAR, DSU, liability in a multi-billion pound layered tower.

1999/2017

FIDIC 1999 Suite Published

FIDIC Red, Yellow, Silver and Gold Book suite published 1999 (revised 2017), modernising international construction contract insurance requirements for the 21st century.

2002

Building Safety Act Precursors

Grenfell Tower fire (2017) triggers the Building Safety Act 2022, creating new duty holders and insurance requirements for higher-risk buildings — reshaping UK CAR underwriting for residential high-rise.

2020

COVID — Construction Disruption

Global pandemic causes unprecedented project delays and supply chain disruption. DSU claims where physical damage co-exists; pure delay losses generally uninsured under CAR/EAR — major coverage gap exposed.

2023

Kahramanmaras Earthquake — $6bn+

Turkey-Syria earthquake sequence generates estimated $6bn+ insured engineering losses — largest single NatCat event for construction insurance. Drives market hardening and earthquake zone pricing reform.
