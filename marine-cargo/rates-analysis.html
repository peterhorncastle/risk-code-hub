Marine cargo rates are driven by a combination of attritional loss experience, large single events, reinsurance cost, and competitive pressure from both Lloyd's and non-Lloyd's markets. The class entered a prolonged soft cycle post-2000, bottomed out around 2017–2019 (when many London market syndicates were writing at loss ratios of 80%+), and has since undergone a measured hardening driven by large events (Tianjin, Hanjin, vessel fires, COVID accumulation). War risk — historically priced separately and cyclically — has spiked sharply since 2023 due to the Red Sea crisis.

Market Cycle Narrative

2010–2015 · Soft Market

Post-Financial Crisis Softening

Abundant capacity, growing trade volumes, low large-loss experience drove rates down 20–35% from 2010 peaks. Competition from emerging Asian and European markets intensified. Lloyd's Performance Management began flagging marine cargo as a loss-making line by 2013–14.

2015–2016 · Shock Events

Tianjin & Hanjin Corrections

Tianjin port explosions ($3.3bn, Aug 2015) and Hanjin bankruptcy ($500m+ cargo claims, Sep 2016) exposed systemic underpricing. Brief rate hardening: +10–20% on commodity and containerised accounts. Reinsurers adjusted terms. Insufficient sustained correction — competition returned within 12 months.

2017–2019 · Trough

Loss Ratio Crisis

NotPetya (2017, $300m Maersk) and Maersk Honam fire (2018, $600m+) hit a market already running 75–85% loss ratios. Lloyd's Performance Management's Decile 10 review (2018–19) forced several marine cargo syndicates to exit or dramatically reduce capacity. Catalyst for genuine market discipline.

2020–2022 · Hardening

COVID Accumulation & Rate Recovery

COVID port congestion (2020–21), Felicity Ace ($500m, 2022), Ever Given GA ($900m fund, 2021) drove sustained hardening. Containerised cargo rates up 20–40%; vehicle carrier rates up 30–60%. Automatic limits reduced; deductibles increased across many renewal accounts.

2023–2024 · War Risk Spike

Red Sea Crisis & Route Disruption

Houthi attacks from Oct 2023 triggered the sharpest war risk rate movement since the 1991 Gulf War. Red Sea transit war risk: 0.07% → 0.5–1.0%. Most cargo now routing Cape of Good Hope — longer transit, higher freight costs, but no war risk surcharge. Total premium effect on annual accounts: +5–15% on affected trades.

ROE Scenarios ILLUS

Bull Scenario
+14–22%

Benign large-loss year; no vessel fire event; Red Sea war risk written profitably; loss ratio 50–55%; investment return tailwind. Rate discipline maintained across market.

Base Scenario
+6–12%

Moderate attritional frequency; one or two mid-size events (GA or vessel damage); loss ratio 62–68%; stable rates; adequate technical margin on most segments.

Bear Scenario
−15–35%

ULCV fire/sinking event ($500m–$1bn insured cargo); major port accumulation loss; Houthi attacks produce war risk claims; Red Sea cargo losses spike; combined loss ratio 100–130%.

Bear scenario trigger events in the current market (2024) are dominated by: (1) a container fire/sinking on a 20,000+ TEU vessel traversing a major ocean route; (2) a port/terminal explosion on the scale of Tianjin or larger (Jebel Ali, Rotterdam, Singapore are the key accumulation sites); (3) sustained Houthi attacks producing direct war risk cargo losses rather than just route diversion costs. Any of these events, individually, has the potential to produce the worst marine cargo loss year in modern Lloyd's history given current accumulation levels. EST
