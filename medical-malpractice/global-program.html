International Healthcare Programme — Illustrative 5-Layer Tower

For a large private hospital group or medical NGO operating in multiple jurisdictions, a layered programme structure is required. The diagram below shows a typical structure from bottom (retained) to top (reinsurance). EST

Layer 5 — XL / Aggregate Reinsurance

Excess-of-loss reinsurance protecting the programme against catastrophic obstetric or multi-claimant events. Aggregate stop-loss for policy years with unexpectedly high claim frequency across international operations. Placed via Lloyd's reinsurance market.

£25m xs £15m aggregate

Layer 4 — Lloyd's International Excess MedMal

Lloyd's syndicate capacity providing the upper excess layer above primary local admitted policies. Claims-made basis; covers all international jurisdictions via a master policy / difference-in-conditions structure. Key for US and Middle East excess layers where admitted market capacity is restricted.

£10m xs £5m per claim

Layer 3 — Primary Local Admitted MedMal (Per Country)

Locally admitted medical malpractice policies in each operating jurisdiction (UK, UAE, Singapore, India etc.). Must comply with local regulatory requirements — e.g. DHA/MOH requirements in UAE; IRDAI requirements in India. Locally admitted policies ensure regulatory compliance and local claims handling. Where local market capacity is limited, fronting arrangements are used.

£5m limit per claim (local)

Layer 2 — GMC / Professional Defence Organisation

Individual practitioners in the group may maintain MDU, MPS or MDDUS membership for personal professional defence costs, GMC fitness-to-practise defence, and regulatory investigation support. Complements (does not replace) the institutional MedMal policy.

MDU/MPS per practitioner

Layer 1 — Self-Insured Retention (SIR)

The insured retains the first layer of each claim — typically £50,000–£500,000 per claim depending on group size. The SIR aligns the insured's incentives with risk reduction. Larger groups with strong governance may retain up to £1m per claim, reducing premium and incentivising investment in clinical risk management.

SIR: £50k–£500k per claim

Jurisdiction Profiles

United Kingdom

NHS: Clinical Negligence Scheme for Trusts (CNST) administered by NHS Resolution covers NHS trusts. Commercial Lloyd's market covers private hospitals, independent practitioners, private GPs, aesthetic clinics and nursing homes.

Standard of care: Bolam/Bolitho for clinical decisions; Montgomery for consent.

Key regulator: GMC (doctors), NMC (nurses), GDC (dentists), CQC (healthcare organisations). VER VER

United States

Structure: State-regulated; admitted market dominates. Lloyd's participates in excess layers and specialist niches (telemedicine, aesthetics, concierge medicine). Contingency fee plaintiff bar, jury trials and discovery obligations make US MedMal 10–15× UK in equivalent claim values.

Defensive medicine: Cultural norm in US — estimated $100bn+ annually in unnecessary tests/procedures driven by litigation fear.

Key challenge: Physician shortage in high-risk specialities (obstetrics, neurosurgery) due to insurance costs. EST

Australia

Structure: APRA-regulated admitted market; Civil Liability Acts in each state cap general damages and apply threshold tests for non-economic loss.

Key bodies: Medical Indemnity Protection Society (MIPS), Avant, MDA National dominate the market. Lloyd's participates for complex/excess layers.

Emerging: Surgical mesh and breast implant class actions; cosmetic surgery regulation tightening. VER

Middle East / UAE

Structure: DHA (Dubai) and MOH (Abu Dhabi) mandate MedMal indemnity for all licensed practitioners. Conventional MedMal or Takaful (Islamic insurance) both available.

Claims: Significantly lower severity than UK/US due to blood money (Diya) system for death, and lower local court damages. However, reputation risk and expatriate patient claims (potentially subject to home jurisdiction law under Rome I/II) can be complex.

Growth: Saudi Arabia Vision 2030 driving private hospital expansion — significant GH growth opportunity. VER

India

Structure: Consumer Protection Act 2019 (CPA 2019) provides the primary framework for patient claims against private healthcare providers. Patients file complaints to District/State/National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions. Claims are substantially lower value than UK but growing rapidly.

Regulatory: Medical Council of India being reformed to National Medical Commission (NMC). Mandatory MedMal insurance proposed but not yet implemented nationally.

Opportunity: India's private healthcare sector is one of the fastest growing globally — significant Lloyd's GH/GM opportunity for international private hospital chains. VER

Sub-Saharan Africa

Structure: Limited local MedMal insurance capacity in most markets. South Africa most developed — SAMLA (South African Medical & Legal Association) active; local admitted capacity exists.

Fronting: International programmes covering African operations typically require fronting arrangements with local admitted carriers (BRINS in Nigeria, Kenya Re, ZEP-RE for east/southern Africa).

NGO/aid: Medical NGOs (MSF, IRC) operating in Africa require specialist MedMal cover — typically placed through Lloyd's with broad jurisdictional scope and low-value/high-frequency claims profile. VER

Case Study — Medical Tourism Cross-Border Liability

UK Aesthetic Surgery Clinic — French Patient Post-Procedure Complications (Illustrative)

Scenario: A London-based aesthetic surgery clinic (GT code) performs a rhinoplasty on a French national patient who travels to the UK specifically for the procedure, having found the clinic online. Three months post-procedure, the patient (now back in France) experiences complications — a secondary procedure is required in France. The patient instructs a French lawyer and claims damages.

Cross-border liability determination: Under EU Regulation (EC) No 864/2007 (Rome II), the law applicable to a non-contractual obligation (tort/delict, including medical negligence) is the law of the country where the damage occurred. If the damage is a continuing harm first arising in France (where the patient lives and is treated for complications), French law may govern. However, the act of negligence occurred in the UK — Rome II Article 4(1) may point to UK law if damage was sustained in the UK at the time of surgery. VER

EU Cross-Border Healthcare Directive 2011/24/EU: Patients exercising rights to cross-border healthcare within the EU are entitled to reimbursement up to the level of the equivalent treatment in their home member state. The Directive does not directly address MedMal liability law, but it establishes that patients seeking treatment abroad retain consumer and patient rights. Post-Brexit, the Directive no longer applies to UK providers; however Rome II remains in force in the UK via the Retained EU Law framework. VER

Insurance implications: The UK GT policy (claims-made basis) should respond to a claim made in the UK against the UK clinic, even for a foreign national patient. Key questions: (a) does the policy provide worldwide coverage (including claims brought in France)? (b) is there a jurisdiction clause restricting coverage to UK/EU courts? (c) is the French patient's damage a UK claim or a French claim? Underwriters writing aesthetics GT risks with EU patient flows must ensure the policy wording provides adequate multinational coverage and clarifies jurisdiction for cross-border claims. EST

Resolution: Most international aesthetic clinic GT policies include worldwide liability cover (excluding USA/Canada). The claim would typically be handled under the UK policy with the UK solicitor-panel managing the defence — including instruction of French law experts where the applicable law is disputed. Rome II arbitrage (choosing the more favourable jurisdiction) is a plaintiff strategy that underwriters of medical tourism risks must anticipate.

EU Cross-Border Healthcare Directive 2011/24/EU — Key Points for MedMal Underwriters: Patients in EU member states are entitled to seek healthcare in another EU member state and receive reimbursement up to the equivalent cost in their home state. Member states must ensure cross-border healthcare providers are registered, insured and subject to the same liability standards as domestic providers. The Directive requires member states to ensure access to liability insurance for healthcare providers treating EU cross-border patients. Post-Brexit, UK providers no longer benefit from or are subject to the Directive, but EU patients coming to the UK may still rely on Rome I (contractual) and Rome II (tortious) private international law frameworks to determine applicable law and jurisdiction. Underwriters writing UK private hospitals treating significant EU patient volumes (particularly UK-EU medical tourism in aesthetics, fertility and specialist surgery) should ensure their programme explicitly addresses cross-border jurisdiction scenarios.
