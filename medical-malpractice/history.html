Era History
Pre-1858 — Pre-Regulation Era

Barber-Surgeons, Common Law and the Absence of Formal Licensing

Before the Medical Act 1858, the practice of medicine in the United Kingdom was almost entirely unregulated. Medical practitioners ranged from university-educated physicians (admitted by royal colleges) to barber-surgeons who combined shaving with minor surgery, apothecaries who prescribed as well as dispensed medicines, and a vast informal market of herbalists, bonesetters and quacks. There was no unified register, no minimum competency standard, and no formal licensing requirement. [L4]

Legal recourse for medical injury existed through common law negligence: a patient harmed by a practitioner's negligent act could in principle sue for damages. However, the practical barriers were enormous — medical expertise was scarce, courts were reluctant to second-guess practitioners, and litigation was expensive and socially stigmatised. The concept of a duty of care between doctor and patient was recognised in principle but seldom litigated successfully. The doctrine that a professional must exercise reasonable care and skill was developing through general contract and tort law but had not yet been applied specifically to medicine.

Insurance for medical liability was essentially non-existent in this era: practitioners relied on personal financial assets and professional reputation. The few cases that reached the courts established early precedents for professional liability that would later develop into the modern law of clinical negligence.

1858–1948 — Regulation, Duty of Care and the Road to the NHS

GMC Established; Donoghue v Stevenson; NHS Founded

The Medical Act 1858 was transformative: it established the General Medical Council (GMC) as the regulatory body for medicine, created a national register of qualified medical practitioners, and — crucially — made it a criminal offence for an unregistered person to claim to be a registered doctor. For the first time, there was a formal definition of who constituted a qualified practitioner and a regulatory body with power to remove practitioners from the register for professional misconduct. [L4]

The landmark tort case of Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562 established the modern law of negligence and the neighbour principle — that one owes a duty of care to those who might foreseeably be harmed by one's actions. Although a products liability case (a decomposed snail in a bottle of ginger beer), its articulation of the duty of care concept proved foundational for all professional liability, including medicine. The doctor-patient relationship was clearly a proximate relationship where duty of care could arise. [L1]

The medical defence organisations — the Medical Defence Union (MDU, founded 1885) and Medical Protection Society (MPS, founded 1892) — emerged in the Victorian era as mutual organisations to defend practitioners against legal claims. They provided legal assistance rather than insurance indemnity in the modern sense, but established the principle that medical practitioners needed collective mutual protection against litigation. The National Health Service Act 1946 and the founding of the NHS in 1948 fundamentally changed the landscape: medicine became a public service, with the Crown (via employing NHS bodies) potentially sharing liability for the acts of NHS-employed practitioners.

1948–1980 — Bolam, NHS Indemnity and the Mutual Sector

The Bolam Test Established; MDU/MPS Defend the NHS Era

The Bolam test, established in Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee [1957] 1 WLR 582, defined the standard of care in medical negligence for the next half-century. The test holds that a doctor is not negligent if they act in accordance with a practice accepted by a responsible body of medical opinion, even if other practitioners would have acted differently. This "body of professional opinion" standard set a high bar for claimants and contributed to the relatively low volume of successful clinical negligence claims through the 1960s and 1970s. [L1]

In the early NHS, liability was handled informally: NHS employing bodies (hospitals, hospital management committees) carried self-insured risk for their employed staff. Individual NHS doctors also maintained MDU or MPS membership for personal protection, particularly for private practice. The split between NHS-funded indemnity and personal defence organisation membership created a complex and poorly defined liability allocation that would only be clarified in 1990.

This era saw relatively modest levels of clinical negligence litigation, reflecting the paternalistic culture of medicine and patients' reluctance to challenge their doctors. However, high-profile pharmaceutical disasters — notably thalidomide (1958–1962), which caused severe birth defects in approximately 10,000 children worldwide — demonstrated the catastrophic potential of medical product liability and drove significant legal and regulatory reform, including the Thalidomide Settlement of 1973. [I1]

1980–2000 — Crown Indemnity, Litigation Explosion and the NHSLA Precursor

NHS Trusts Assume Liability; US Tort Crisis Influences UK; NHSLA Established

The introduction of Crown Indemnity in 1990 was a pivotal moment in UK healthcare liability. NHS Circular HC(89)34 directed that NHS employing bodies would indemnify their employed practitioners for negligent acts carried out in the course of NHS duties. This transferred the primary liability exposure from individual practitioners and their defence organisations to NHS trusts and health authorities — a fundamental restructuring of the risk allocation. The MDU and MPS remained important for private practice and non-NHS activities, but their role in NHS clinical negligence was substantially reduced. [I1]

Across the Atlantic, the United States was experiencing a severe medical malpractice crisis in the 1970s and 1980s: escalating jury awards, a proliferation of plaintiff attorneys, and dramatic premium increases drove many obstetricians, neurosurgeons and other high-risk specialists to abandon practice or relocate. US commercial medical malpractice insurers faced solvency crises. While UK litigation remained far more restrained due to the absence of jury trials, contingency fees and punitive damages, the US experience influenced UK policymakers and insurers to consider systemic reform.

The NHS Litigation Authority (NHSLA) was established in 1995 to manage and administer the Clinical Negligence Scheme for Trusts (CNST), which pools clinical negligence liabilities across participating NHS trusts on a risk-sharing basis. This collective scheme replaced ad hoc self-insurance by individual trusts and represented the first systematic approach to managing NHS-wide clinical negligence risk. By the end of the 1990s, the NHSLA was managing a growing caseload of claims that would escalate dramatically in the following decade. [I1]

2000–2015 — Public Inquiries, Montgomery and the NHSLA's Growing Burden

Bristol, Shipman, Mid Staffs — Systemic Failures Reshape Healthcare Liability

The first decade of the 21st century was defined by a series of devastating public inquiries into systemic NHS failures. The Bristol Royal Infirmary Inquiry (2001) found that the cardiac surgery unit had a significantly higher mortality rate for children's heart surgery than comparable centres, with institutional failures to act on warning signs for years. It triggered major reforms in medical regulation, clinical audit and patient safety culture. [I4]

The Shipman Inquiry (2004–2005) examined the murders of approximately 250 patients by GP Harold Shipman — the largest serial killing by a single perpetrator in UK history. It exposed failures in death certification, GP oversight and appraisal systems, driving major regulatory reform including compulsory revalidation of doctors (implemented 2012). Though not a clinical negligence case in the traditional sense, the inquiry shaped the regulatory framework within which MedMal risk is managed. [I1]

The Mid Staffordshire NHS Foundation Trust Public Inquiry (Francis Report 2013) found that between 400 and 1,200 patients may have died as a result of poor care at Stafford Hospital between 2005 and 2009, driven by a culture of cost-cutting, poor management and failure of oversight. The Francis Report's 290 recommendations fundamentally changed NHS governance and patient safety obligations. Liability claims arising from the Mid Staffs failures added significantly to NHS Resolution's caseload. [I8]

The Supreme Court decision in Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board [2015] UKSC 11 revolutionised informed consent in UK medical law. Overruling the previous Bolam-based approach to consent, the Court held that a doctor must take reasonable care to ensure that a patient is aware of any material risks involved in a proposed treatment, and of any reasonable alternatives. "Material" is defined by what a reasonable patient in the patient's position would want to know. This patient-centred consent standard replaced the doctor-centred Bolam approach for consent cases and has driven a significant increase in consent-related clinical negligence claims. [L2]

2015–Present — NHS Resolution, Ockenden and the £83bn Liability Crisis

Maternity Crisis, Long-Tail Escalation, COVID Delays and AI Diagnostics

The NHSLA was rebranded as NHS Resolution in 2017, reflecting a broader mandate encompassing dispute resolution, learning from claims data, and promoting a culture of early resolution. By 2022/23, NHS Resolution paid out £2.8bn in clinical negligence claims — a figure that continues to rise year on year. The total NHS clinical negligence provision (the present value of all estimated future claims) reached £83.4bn in 2023 — a figure that has prompted serious concern about the long-term financial sustainability of NHS indemnity. [I1][I2]

The Ockenden Report (2022) into maternity services at Shrewsbury and Telford NHS Trust found avoidable harm to more than 1,300 babies and 9 mothers over two decades. It identified persistent systemic failures in leadership, culture, staffing and governance. Obstetrics remains the single highest-cost class within NHS clinical negligence: cerebral palsy claims from adverse birth outcomes can reach £20–30m due to the lifetime care costs for a severely brain-damaged child. Maternity safety has become a national priority and a major driver of NHS Resolution's reform agenda. [I4]

COVID-19 (2020–22) generated a new category of clinical negligence claims: patients whose non-COVID conditions — notably cancer, cardiac disease and orthopaedic conditions — went undiagnosed or untreated during the pandemic. Delayed diagnosis and treatment claims are emerging across multiple specialities, with a long latency period as harm becomes apparent and patients bring claims. The actuarial community is working to quantify this COVID-delayed-care liability tail.

Looking forward, the emergence of AI in diagnostics — AI-assisted radiology, algorithmic sepsis prediction, AI-driven pathology — raises new questions about liability when an algorithm misdiagnoses or delays diagnosis. The regulatory framework (UKCA marking for medical devices, MHRA oversight of software as a medical device) is developing rapidly. Underwriters writing GH and GM codes must now consider AI diagnostics liability as a material emerging exposure.

Lloyd's MedMal Market Context: Lloyd's syndicates write Medical Malpractice principally as a private sector and international class. NHS liability is managed through NHS Resolution (CNST) outside the commercial market. Lloyd's GH/GM/GN/GO/GQ/GT business includes: private hospital groups (BMI, Spire, HCA), international healthcare organisations, private GPs and consultants, aesthetic clinic operators, overseas healthcare providers (Middle East, Asia), and medical NGOs. US MedMal is largely written by admitted domestic carriers; Lloyd's participates in excess layers and specialist niches. The Lloyd's MedMal market is estimated at £500m–£800m GWP across all codes (estimated, not publicly disclosed at sub-class level). [I10]
Key Milestones
1858

Medical Act 1858 — GMC Founded

General Medical Council established; national register created; unregistered practice regulated for the first time. Foundation of modern medical regulation.

1957

Bolam Test Established

Bolam v Friern HMC [1957] — standard of care defined as a responsible body of medical opinion. Remains central to clinical negligence law despite subsequent refinements.

1961

Thalidomide Disaster

Approximately 10,000 children worldwide born with severe limb deformities caused by thalidomide prescribed for morning sickness. Drives pharmaceutical liability reform internationally.

1990

Crown Indemnity — NHS Trusts Assume Liability

HC(89)34 shifts primary MedMal liability from individual practitioners to NHS employing bodies. MDU/MPS role in NHS practice reduced substantially.

1995

NHSLA / CNST Established

NHS Litigation Authority created to administer Clinical Negligence Scheme for Trusts — first systematic pooling of NHS clinical negligence liabilities.

1997

Bolitho Refinement

Bolitho v City & Hackney HA [1997] UKHL 46 — Bolam standard refined: court may reject a body of professional opinion if it cannot withstand logical analysis. Marginally strengthens claimants' position.

2001

Bristol Heart Surgery Inquiry

Bristol Royal Infirmary Inquiry reveals systemic paediatric cardiac surgery failures. Drives major reform in clinical audit, peer review and patient safety governance.

2004

Shipman Inquiry Published

Harold Shipman — 250+ patient murders over 23 years. Inquiry drives compulsory GP revalidation, reform of death certification and GMC fitness-to-practise procedures.

2015

Montgomery v Lanarkshire — Informed Consent

Supreme Court replaces Bolam consent standard with patient-centred test: material risk must be disclosed. Significant increase in consent-based clinical negligence claims follows.

2022

Ockenden Report — Maternity Crisis

Ockenden Review finds avoidable harm to 1,300+ babies and 9 mothers at Shrewsbury & Telford. NHS obstetrics the highest-cost single class in clinical negligence.

2023

NHS Provision Reaches £83.4bn

NHS Resolution's total clinical negligence provision reaches £83.4bn — raising serious questions about long-term NHS indemnity sustainability.
