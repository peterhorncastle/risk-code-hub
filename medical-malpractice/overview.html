GH — Medical Malpractice: Hospital

Covers NHS trusts, foundation trusts, private hospitals and independent treatment centres. High bed-count, multi-speciality exposure with significant obstetrics, surgery and emergency medicine risk. In the NHS, CNST (Clinical Negligence Scheme for Trusts) administered by NHS Resolution provides cover; private hospitals require commercial policies, often placed through Lloyd's for large limits. [I1]

GM — Medical Malpractice: General

General medical practitioners (GPs), primary care networks and GP partnerships. Historically defended by mutual defence organisations (MDU, MPS, MDDUS); since 2019 NHS England mandates state-backed indemnity for NHS GP services. Private GP services and locum GPs still require commercial cover. Average claim frequency per GP is lower than hospital specialists but long tail from delayed diagnosis claims. [I3]

GN — Medical Malpractice: Nursing

Nursing homes, care homes and residential care facilities. Distinct from hospital cover: lower acuity but high frequency of falls, pressure ulcer, medication error and end-of-life care claims. Care Quality Commission (CQC) oversight drives regulatory compliance risk. Staffing levels and training standards are key underwriting factors. [L5]

GO — Medical Malpractice: Other

Allied health professionals not captured by GH/GM/GN: dentists, physiotherapists, opticians, chiropractors, osteopaths, podiatrists, dietitians and occupational therapists. Generally lower claim severity than hospital specialities but broad frequency exposure across large practitioner populations. Dental negligence (failed treatment, extraction errors, nerve damage) is a high-frequency sub-class. [I9]

GQ — Healthcare Liability

Healthcare organisations: clinical commissioning groups (now Integrated Care Boards in England), health systems, private healthcare operators, clinical networks. Organisational rather than individual practitioner liability — covers failures of system design, commissioning decisions, care pathway failures. Interfaces with public liability and D&O for governance failures. [L5]

GT — Medical Malpractice: Treatment

Treatment liability for aesthetic and cosmetic medicine: aesthetic clinics, cosmetic surgery centres, laser and skin clinics, IV therapy providers, non-surgical cosmetic procedure providers. Fast-growing unregulated sector with high claim frequency from botched procedures. Absence of mandatory GMC registration for many aesthetic practitioners is a significant underwriting concern. [I10]

Coverage Taxonomy

Clinical Negligence

Core coverage: liability arising from a failure to meet the standard of care expected of a reasonably competent medical practitioner (Bolam/Bolitho standard). Covers misdiagnosis, surgical error, medication error, delayed treatment, and failure to refer. The dominant MedMal peril by claim frequency and severity. [L1]

Products Liability — Medical

Liability arising from prescription or use of a defective medicinal product, medical device or implant in the course of treatment. Interfaces with manufacturers' products liability but treating clinician or hospital may face concurrent liability. PIP breast implant and surgical mesh claims are illustrative examples. [L8]

Defence Costs & Legal Expenses

Costs of defending clinical negligence claims, including solicitor fees, expert witness costs, court costs and counsel fees. In complex cases (e.g. cerebral palsy, spinal injury) defence costs can equal or exceed the damages. Claims-made policies cover defence costs arising in the policy year. [I6]

Regulatory Investigations

Costs of responding to GMC (fitness to practise), CQC inspection, coroner's inquest and Healthcare Safety Investigation Branch (HSIB) investigations. Not always included as standard — regulatory defence cover is an important extension particularly for consultants and GP partners. [L4]

Retroactive / Claims-Made Basis

MedMal is almost universally written on a claims-made basis: the policy in force at the time the claim is made (not at the time of treatment) responds. Retroactive date defines the earliest date of alleged negligent act that is covered. Long retroactive dates substantially increase insurer exposure, particularly for obstetrics and surgery with long latency injuries. [I6]

Extended Reporting Period (Tail Cover)

On retirement, career break or change of insurer, practitioners require an Extended Reporting Period (ERP/tail cover) to ensure that claims arising from past treatments (made after the policy period) are covered. Typically provided for 1–6 years at additional premium. Essential for surgeons, obstetricians and any practitioner with long-latency exposures. [I6]

Claims-Made vs Occurrence — Critical Distinction for MedMal: Unlike most liability classes which can be written on an occurrence basis, Medical Malpractice is almost universally written on a claims-made basis. This means the policy in force at the time the claim is first made (not at the time of the negligent act or treatment) responds to the claim. A treatment carried out in 2019 by a GP who retires in 2024 and receives a claim letter in 2026 would require a claims-made policy in force in 2026, or a tail/ERP covering the period post-retirement. This makes Extended Reporting Period (tail cover) essential at career end, and retroactive date negotiation critical at policy inception. [I6]
Warning — Aesthetic & Cosmetic Procedures (GT): The UK aesthetic medicine sector (Botox, fillers, laser treatments, cosmetic surgery) is largely unregulated. Practitioners are not required to hold medical qualifications. This creates significant adverse selection risk for underwriters writing GT. The 2021 HEE/GMC Joint Review and proposed Aesthetics Regulation (UK) may tighten requirements but remains in development. High claim frequency, difficulty establishing standard of care, and absent consent documentation make aesthetics a challenging sub-class requiring significant rate loading and careful underwriting. [I10]
