Era 1: The Tort Law Foundations (Pre-1932)

Pre-1932 · Common Law Origins

Privity, Negligence and the Duty of Care

Before the twentieth century, liability for personal injury caused by defective products or negligent conduct was severely constrained by the doctrine of privity of contract — only the immediate contracting party could sue. Consumers injured by defective goods had no direct claim against the manufacturer; they could only pursue the retailer with whom they had contracted. Industrialisation created mass-production of consumer goods and a growing mismatch between legal rights and economic reality.

Early liability insurance in Britain developed informally in the late nineteenth century, initially as extensions to fire policies covering third-party damage from industrial activities. The Employers' Liability Act 1880 imposed statutory duties on employers for workplace injury — one of the earliest non-marine liability insurance triggers. Lloyd's syndicates began underwriting public liability on a case-by-case basis from the 1890s, adapting indemnity wordings from marine liability precedents.

  • 1880: Employers' Liability Act 1880 — statutory employer negligence liability; early driver of liability insurance demand
  • 1897: Workmen's Compensation Act 1897 — compulsory employer compensation regardless of fault; Lloyd's syndicates begin writing employers' liability
  • 1906: Workmen's Compensation Act 1906 extended coverage; modern EL insurance product emerges
  • Pre-1932: Rylands v Fletcher (1868) — strict liability for escape of dangerous things from land; foundational precedent for non-fault liability theory

Era 2: The Negligence Revolution — Donoghue v Stevenson (1932)

1932 · Foundational Precedent

The Snail in the Bottle — Modern Liability Insurance Is Born

The House of Lords decision in Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562 is the single most important case in the history of liability insurance. VER Mrs May Donoghue consumed ginger beer poured from an opaque bottle purchased by a friend at a café in Paisley, Scotland. The bottle contained a decomposed snail. She suffered gastroenteritis and nervous shock, but had no contract with the manufacturer (Stevenson) — only her friend had contracted with the café.

Lord Atkin's majority judgment established the modern neighbour principle: "You must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour." A manufacturer owed a duty of care to the ultimate consumer, regardless of privity of contract. The ruling created the legal foundation for products liability insurance — a manufacturer could now be sued directly by injured consumers. The non-marine liability market at Lloyd's expanded rapidly through the 1930s and 1940s as manufacturers sought coverage for this newly recognised exposure.

  • 1932: Donoghue v Stevenson — duty of care established; products liability exposure created
  • 1938: Increased liability underwriting at Lloyd's — public liability and products combined policies develop
  • 1940s: WWII industrial production; liability claims pause; insurers build reserves
  • 1950s: Post-war consumer boom; mass production of consumer goods; products liability exposure grows

Era 3: The Pharmaceutical and Industrial Catastrophe Era (1960–1985)

1960–1985 · Mass Casualty Events

Thalidomide, Asbestos and the Limits of Tort — The Non-US Market Learns Long-Tail

The period from 1960 to 1985 saw a series of catastrophic mass-casualty events involving defective products that fundamentally shaped non-US GL underwriting philosophy. These events revealed the latency problem: liability claims can take years or decades to emerge after the product is placed on market, long after the policy has expired and the premium has been spent.

  • 1961: Thalidomide — sedative drug prescribed to pregnant women causes birth defects in ~10,000 children worldwide (including ~2,000 in UK). VER German manufacturer Grünenthal and UK distributor Distillers faced claims. UK settlement (1973) eventually reached £28m — inadequate. Revealed the inadequacy of existing product liability law for pharmaceutical mass-tort events.
  • 1972: Employers' Liability (Compulsory Insurance) Act 1969 — UK EL becomes compulsory from 1972; separates EL from GL market
  • 1974: Flixborough disaster — chemical plant explosion, 28 fatalities, major public liability claim against operator. Accelerates industrial GL pricing
  • 1976: Seveso disaster (Italy) — dioxin release from ICMESA chemical plant; large public/environmental liability; precursor to Seveso Directives. VER
  • 1978: Herkules MF settlement — asbestos bodily injury claims begin reaching London market from non-US sources; early long-tail development
  • 1984: Bhopal, India — Union Carbide gas leak, ~15,000 deaths (est.), hundreds of thousands injured. EST Landmark industrial liability event: ultimate settlement (US$470m, 1989) widely regarded as wholly inadequate. VER Demonstrated both the scale of industrial liability exposure and the inadequacy of tort law for mass-casualty developing-world events.
  • 1985: EU Product Liability Directive (Council Directive 85/374/EEC) enacted — first harmonised strict liability regime for defective products across European Community. VER Removed the need to prove fault; producer strictly liable where product is defective and damage caused by defect. Development risk defence allowed by Member States.

Era 4: Harmonisation and the Global Products Liability Market (1985–2010)

1985–2010 · Regulatory Harmonisation

EU PLD, BSE, PIP and the Professionalisation of International Casualty

The EU Product Liability Directive (1985) transformed European products liability underwriting — strict liability meant that product liability insurance became a genuine commercial necessity rather than an optional extra for European manufacturers. Lloyd's non-US GL book grew substantially through this period as European industrial companies sought coverage for their Directive exposure and export risks worldwide.

  • 1987: Consumer Protection Act 1987 (UK) — implements EU PLD into English law; strict liability for defective products. VER
  • 1992: General Product Safety Directive (GPSD 92/59/EEC) — safety obligation for all consumer products placed on EU market; recalls and enforcement powers. Replaced 2001 by GPSD 2001/95/EC. VER
  • 1996: BSE/CJD crisis — UK beef products causing variant Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease; major food products liability event; EU beef export ban; Lloyd's food GL book affected. EST
  • 2001: Asbestos — Lloyd's Equitas run-off; asbestos-related liabilities (largely US but some non-US) crystallise; major reserve strengthening across the market. VER
  • 2006: EU REACH Regulation — Registration, Evaluation, Authorisation and Restriction of Chemicals; strict chemical safety information requirements; compliance failure creates product liability exposure. VER
  • 2007–2009: Global financial crisis — tort claim frequency dips; liability insurers benefit from reduced claims activity but face investment pressure

Era 5: Social Inflation, Regulatory Revolution and Digital Risk (2010–2024)

2010–2024 · Modern Era

PIP, Dieselgate, Grenfell, GPSR and the EU Representative Actions Revolution

The decade from 2010 to 2024 saw the non-US GL market face claims events of unprecedented scale and complexity — a French breast implant scandal affecting 400,000 women globally, a pan-European automotive emissions fraud, a cladding fire that destroyed 72 lives, and a fundamental rewrite of EU product safety law. The period also saw the first meaningful steps toward EU-style class action litigation.

  • 2011–2013: PIP breast implant scandal — Poly Implant Prothèse (PIP), France; industrial silicone (not medical grade) used in ~400,000 implants worldwide. EST French courts, EU regulatory response, insurer claims across Lloyd's non-US NR book. Major products liability event with long-tail development. VER
  • 2015: Volkswagen Dieselgate — defeat device software in VW, Audi, Porsche diesel engines manipulates emissions tests. VER European regulatory fines, civil litigation across Germany, UK, France; distinct from US class action ($25bn US settlement). European GL and D&O claims.
  • 2017: Grenfell Tower fire — 72 deaths; cladding system (Arconic ACM cladding) failed fire safety standards. VER Products liability and public liability claims against manufacturers, installers, local authority. Long-term liability development ongoing as of 2024.
  • 2019: EU Representative Actions Directive — enables consumer organisations to bring collective redress actions across EU member states; implemented by member states by 2023. Potential to significantly increase non-US liability aggregation. VER
  • 2022: EU revised Product Liability Directive proposal — strict liability extended to software, AI systems, circular economy products; development risk defence narrowed. Final text 2024. VER
  • 2024: EU General Product Safety Regulation (GPSR) effective 13 December 2024 — replaces GPSD 2001; extends to online marketplace operators; new traceability requirements; mandatory digital product passports. VER

Lloyd's Non-US GL: From Generalist to Specialist

Lloyd's has written non-US general liability continuously since the late nineteenth century, evolving from broad indemnity covers adapted from marine precedents to highly structured, jurisdiction-specific international casualty programmes. Three structural features distinguish Lloyd's participation in this market:

  • Long-tail expertise: Non-US GL, particularly products and contractors, can generate claims 10–30 years after the policy year. Lloyd's syndicates have developed actuarial and reserving capability specifically for long-tail development, distinguishing them from short-tail-focused insurers.
  • Worldwide jurisdiction cover: Lloyd's uniquely offers non-US GL policies with worldwide jurisdiction clauses (excluding US/Canada) — covering claims brought in any non-US court. This is essential for multinational manufacturers with complex supply chains and multiple selling territories.
  • Excess and umbrella capacity: Lloyd's provides substantial excess of loss capacity above primary layers placed in local markets — critical for large industrial, pharmaceutical and construction risks where primary limits are insufficient for potential worst-case scenarios.
