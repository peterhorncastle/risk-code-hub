Module 01 — History & Origins

Credit & Contract Frustration — History

Six eras of credit insurance — from 19th-century merchant credit societies through post-war ECA structures, Cold War sovereign defaults, the 1997 Asian crisis, the 2008 credit collapse, and the COVID-era government guarantee scramble.

Overview History Timeline Loss Database Underwriting Rates & ROE Risk Mitigation Global Programme References Rater

Credit insurance is among the oldest formal insurance products — commercial merchants in 19th-century Europe were extending open-account credit to distant buyers long before any formal market infrastructure existed. The evolution of modern credit insurance tracks the evolution of global trade itself: from colonial-era merchant credit, through the catastrophic sovereign defaults of the 1930s, to the ECA frameworks built after World War II, and finally to the private market consolidation and digitalisation of the 21st century. VER

Era 1 — Pre-1900

Merchant Credit, Early Insurance Experiments & Lloyd's Trade Finance Roots

The commercial need for credit insurance predates formal insurance markets. Merchants extending trade credit to distant buyers faced two fundamental risks: the buyer might be unable to pay (insolvency) or the buyer might simply refuse (protracted default). The 19th-century solution was primarily the letter of credit — a bank guarantee that the buyer would pay — rather than insurance. Early credit insurance experiments in the 1830s–1860s in the UK, France, and Germany largely failed, primarily because insurers lacked the credit intelligence infrastructure to assess buyer creditworthiness on a portfolio basis. VER

The first commercially successful credit insurance company is generally considered to be the Hermes Kreditversicherungs-AG, established in Germany in 1917 (later to become HERMES Kreditversicherung, and subsequently part of Euler Hermes / Allianz Trade). In the UK, the Trade Indemnity Company was established in 1918. These companies were the direct ancestors of the modern specialist credit insurance market. VER

Lloyd's underwriters participated in trade finance-related risks from an early stage — particularly in marine cargo trade credit and documentary credit risks — establishing the Lloyd's CR code as an extension of the market's historical trade finance expertise. EST

Era 2 — 1918–1945

Post-WWI Reconstruction, Great Depression Defaults & ECA Origins

The post-World War I period saw rapid expansion of credit insurance, driven by the reconstruction of European trade and the need to extend credit to war-damaged economies. The UK's Export Credits Guarantee Department (ECGD) — the world's first export credit agency — was established in 1919 specifically to support British exporters selling to post-war European markets where private credit insurance was unavailable. VER

The Great Depression (1929–1933) was the first major test of credit insurance. The cascade of sovereign defaults (Germany, Austria, Hungary, and most of Latin America) produced losses that overwhelmed the nascent private credit insurance industry. The Hoover Moratorium of 1931 — suspending war debt payments — triggered widespread cross-default of commercial credit obligations. Most private credit insurers either failed or withdrew entirely from sovereign and export credit risk. The lesson — that systemic economic downturns produce correlated credit losses across entire portfolios — has recurred in every subsequent global recession. VER

Following the Depression, ECA expansion accelerated globally. The US Export-Import Bank (EXIM) was established in 1934. France's COFACE (Compagnie Française d'Assurance pour le Commerce Extérieur) was created in 1946. These agencies were explicitly designed to fill the gap left by private markets unwilling to assume correlated sovereign and political credit risk. VER

Era 3 — 1945–1980

Post-War ECA Dominance, Berne Union & Private Market Rebuilding

The post-war period saw the formalisation of ECA co-operation under the Berne Union (International Union of Credit and Investment Insurers), established in 1934 but formalised and expanded post-1945. Berne Union members agreed to common minimum premium rates and maximum credit terms under the OECD Arrangement on Officially Supported Export Credits (formalised 1978), preventing a race to the bottom in subsidised export credit. VER

Private credit insurance — particularly whole turnover — grew steadily through the 1950s–1970s as Western European intra-regional trade expanded. The common market (Treaty of Rome, 1957) dramatically increased the volume of short-term domestic and intra-European trade credit. Companies selling across national borders with open account terms needed credit protection against buyer insolvency in unfamiliar markets. VER

The Suez Crisis (1956), Cold War expropriations in Cuba (1960), the Algerian War, and the Iranian nationalisation of the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company (1951) established contract frustration as a distinct product. CF was the commercial insurance response to contracts frustrated by government action — a peril that neither standard commercial insurance nor political risk (which focused on property assets) had cleanly addressed. EST

The 1970s brought two significant shocks: the 1973 oil crisis created liquidity problems for many developing country importers, and the 1979 Iranian Revolution (which frustrated hundreds of export contracts) demonstrated the acute need for CF coverage. The US EXIM and European ECAs were overwhelmed with Iranian CF claims, crystallising the need for private market capacity in CF. VER

Era 4 — 1980–1997

Latin American Debt Crisis, Private Market Consolidation & OECD Arrangement

The 1982 Mexican debt default triggered the Latin American debt crisis — 16 countries rescheduled external debt between 1982 and 1985. For credit insurers, this was a major CF and sovereign non-payment event: export contracts to Latin American state buyers were frustrated by government-imposed payment moratoriums; trade credit insurers suffered significant losses on shipments to Mexican, Brazilian, and Argentine buyers. The crisis reinforced the importance of country risk limits and portfolio concentration controls. VER

The 1980s saw significant private market consolidation. The three dominant specialty credit insurers — which would become Allianz Trade (Euler Hermes), Atradius, and Coface — emerged through a series of acquisitions. Euler & Hermes merged their credit insurance businesses; NCM (Netherlands Credit Insurance Company) became the nucleus of what would become Atradius. COFACE expanded from a purely French ECA function into a private credit insurance competitor. By 1990, the private market had largely recovered from Depression-era weakness, though ECAs retained dominance in medium-term export credit. VER

At Lloyd's, the CR and CF codes developed as specialist lines for single-risk, large-ticket export credit and CF — where the specialist insurers had concentration limits or appetite restrictions. Lloyd's underwriters brought capacity and flexibility for unusual buyer/country combinations that fell outside the mainstream insurers' parameters. EST

Era 5 — 1997–2010

Asian Crisis, Russian Default, Argentine Collapse & GFC Credit Crunch

The 1997–1998 Asian financial crisis was the largest coordinated credit insurance loss event since the Latin American debt crisis. Currency devaluations in Thailand (July 1997), Indonesia, South Korea, Malaysia, and the Philippines triggered cascading buyer insolvencies across the region. Korean chaebol insolvencies (Daewoo Group's 1999 collapse was the largest corporate insolvency in Korean history) generated significant credit insurance losses. Indonesian rupiah devaluation of over 80% rendered most Indonesian buyers unable to service hard-currency obligations. The crisis demonstrated that export whole turnover portfolios concentrated in high-growth emerging market regions carried systemic correlation risk. VER

Russia's August 1998 sovereign default and debt moratorium frustrated hundreds of export contracts and triggered CF and CR losses across European and US exporters. The default wiped out most trade credit to Russia — a market that had been growing rapidly post-Soviet liberalisation — and forced major credit insurers to significantly tighten their Russia credit limits. VER

The Argentine sovereign default of December 2001 — the largest sovereign default in history at the time, $93 billion — produced the decade's largest CF wave. Argentine state-owned enterprise buyers defaulted on commercial contracts; currency convertibility was suspended (corralito and corralón); export contracts were frustrated by import restrictions and repatriation controls. CF claims from the Argentine default exceeded $2 billion across the insurance market. VER

The 2008–2009 global financial crisis (GFC) was the most severe peacetime stress test the credit insurance market had ever faced. The collapse of Lehman Brothers in September 2008 triggered a global trade finance freeze — banks withdrew letters of credit and trade finance lines simultaneously. Whole turnover insurers faced an unprecedented increase in claims as buyer insolvencies surged globally. Some specialist credit insurers reduced or cancelled credit limits mid-policy to control aggregate exposure, creating severe tensions with policyholders who relied on those limits for their own forward planning. The incident raised fundamental questions about the counter-cyclical behaviour of private credit insurance — the industry was reducing credit availability at precisely the moment when it was most needed. VER

Era 6 — 2010–2024

Digitalisation, COVID Guarantee Schemes & Supply Chain Concentration Risk

The COVID-19 pandemic (2020) was the most significant structural stress test of credit insurance market architecture in the modern era. As global lockdowns caused widespread buyer distress, specialist credit insurers began reducing limits en masse — threatening to collapse the trade credit markets and starve solvent companies of credit. Governments across Europe intervened with unprecedented credit insurance guarantee schemes. The UK government's Trade Credit Reinsurance (TCR) scheme — announced in May 2020 — guaranteed £10 billion of credit limits, effectively mutualising the losses between government and the private market. Germany (Schutzschirm), France, and the Netherlands enacted similar schemes. Total European government credit insurance guarantee schemes peaked at approximately €180 billion of exposure by mid-2020. VER

The pandemic also accelerated the digitalisation of credit insurance distribution. InsurTech platforms (Hokodo, Nimbla, Tinubu Square) emerged to provide SMEs with on-demand, transactional credit insurance via API integration with e-invoicing and trade finance platforms — a radical departure from traditional annual whole turnover policy structures. EST

The Russian invasion of Ukraine (February 2022) triggered the largest CF and sovereign credit event since Argentina 2001. SWIFT exclusion of Russian banks, asset freezes, and export prohibition effectively frustrated thousands of export contracts to Russian buyers. Credit insurers with Russian exposure — primarily those with whole turnover limits on Russian buyers — faced significant CF and protracted default losses. The invasion also disrupted Ukrainian grain export contracts, triggering CF losses in commodity trade. VER

Supply chain concentration risk emerged as a structural underwriting concern through the 2020s. The failure of Greensill Capital (March 2021) — a supply chain finance specialist — exposed an estimated $5 billion of credit insurance risk underwritten by Tokio Marine HCC, with knock-on implications for the structure of supply chain finance credit insurance. The episode illustrated how credit insurance could be used to support financial structures at the edge of its intended purpose. VER
