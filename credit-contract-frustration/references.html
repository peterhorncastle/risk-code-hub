Credit insurance loss data is among the least transparent of any insurance class. Unlike property cat losses — which are extensively modelled and publicly reported by Munich Re, Swiss Re, and Aon — credit insurance losses are privately settled between insurers and policyholders without public disclosure requirements. Aggregate statistics are published by the Berne Union and ICISA but not disaggregated by event or buyer. The most reliable loss data comes from judicial proceedings, regulatory investigations (Greensill), or sovereign default research (IMF, World Bank). Premium rate data is similarly opaque — the specialist credit insurers do not publish rate information, and Lloyd's market data is not segment-specific for CR/CF/WT. VER

Six Landmark Events in CR/CF/WT Insurance History

December 2001

Argentine Sovereign Default — $93bn & Corralito

Argentina's December 2001 default — the largest sovereign default in history at the time — and the subsequent corralito banking freeze and peso convertibility suspension produced the insurance market's largest CF wave since the Latin American debt crisis. State-owned enterprise buyers defaulted on commercial contracts; import restrictions frustrated hundreds of export contracts; currency inconvertibility prevented dollar-denominated payment settlement. Total insurance market CF/CR/WT losses exceeded $2 billion across all markets. The Argentine default remains the benchmark CF event against which all subsequent country CF scenarios are modelled. It demonstrated that even large, upper-middle-income economies can experience complete payment system collapse, and that CF coverage must explicitly address currency convertibility suspension separately from simple government force majeure. VER

2008–2009

Global Financial Crisis — Credit Insurance Under Systemic Stress

The 2008–2009 GFC was the most severe systemic test of the modern credit insurance market. Whole turnover combined ratios exceeded 100% for most specialist credit insurers in 2009 — the first time in the modern era. The crisis exposed the pro-cyclical nature of private credit insurance: as losses rose, insurers reduced credit limits across their portfolios, withdrawing credit availability at precisely the moment when policyholders most needed it to manage their cash flows. This counter-productive behaviour — rational from an individual insurer's risk management perspective — was collectively disastrous for trade credit markets. Governments intervened with emergency guarantee schemes in the UK, Germany, France, and the Netherlands. The episode generated lasting debate about whether private credit insurance can function as a reliable trade credit mechanism during systemic recessions, or whether a permanent government backstop is required. VER

March 2021

Greensill Capital Collapse — Supply Chain Finance Insurance Failure

The collapse of Greensill Capital in March 2021 was the most significant credit insurance market event since the GFC. Greensill had used credit insurance — underwritten by Tokio Marine HCC — to enable its supply chain finance (reverse factoring) business. When TMHCC declined to renew Greensill's credit insurance in early 2021, citing concerns about the structure of the underlying receivables, Greensill's business model collapsed within weeks. The resulting estimated $5 billion insurance exposure was contested by TMHCC, which raised defences including misrepresentation and policy compliance issues. The episode raised fundamental questions about: the use of credit insurance to support financial structures (rather than trade transactions); the concentration of credit insurance within a single SCF platform; and the regulatory gap between credit insurance and financial regulation of supply chain finance. VER

May 2020

COVID-19 — European Government Credit Guarantee Schemes (€180bn)

The COVID-19 pandemic created the largest peacetime government intervention in credit insurance markets. As global lockdowns caused mass buyer distress, specialist credit insurers began reducing limits — threatening to create a credit crunch that would compound the economic damage. The UK government's Trade Credit Reinsurance scheme (TCR, announced May 2020) guaranteed £10 billion of credit limits, effectively transferring the credit risk of the specialist insurers' UK portfolios to the government. Germany, France, and the Netherlands created equivalent schemes. Total European government credit insurance guarantee exposure peaked at approximately €180 billion by mid-2020. The schemes successfully prevented a credit insurance market collapse, but their design and the ultimate cost to taxpayers revealed significant weaknesses in the private credit insurance market's ability to function as a counter-cyclical economic stabiliser without public backstop. VER

August 1998

Russian Sovereign Default — GKO Moratorium

Russia's August 1998 default — triggered by oil price collapse, capital flight, and IMF programme failure — was the first major sovereign default in the post-Soviet era and the first to affect a market where Western credit insurers had been actively building exposure. Russia's moratorium on GKO (rouble treasury bond) payments and suspension of foreign debt service caused the rouble to devalue by 75%. Trade credit losses to Russian buyers crystallised rapidly across European and US exporters; CF claims were filed for frustrated export contracts. The 1998 Russian default became a benchmark for modelling sovereign default impacts on trade credit portfolios and was a direct precursor to the much larger 2022 market closure following the Ukraine invasion. VER

February 2022

Russia/Ukraine War — Largest CF Event Since Argentina

The February 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine triggered the largest CF event in the credit insurance market since the Argentine default of 2001. SWIFT exclusion of Russian banks, EU/US asset freezes, export prohibitions, and import bans frustrated thousands of export contracts to Russian buyers simultaneously. Ukrainian grain, steel, and commodity trade halted. Credit insurers with Russian whole turnover exposure — particularly those who had expanded Russian limits in the post-2014 normalisation period — reported significant CF and protracted default losses across 2022–2023. The event demonstrated once again that country risk limits can change from manageable to total-market-closure virtually overnight, and confirmed that any credit insurance programme with Russia concentration above 5% of aggregate turnover required fundamental risk management review. VER

Known Data Gaps

  • Lloyd's CR/CF/WT GWP and loss ratio by sub-line: Lloyd's Aggregate Accounts do not disaggregate credit lines (CR, CF, WT) from each other or from political risk. No public source provides Lloyd's-specific credit insurance combined ratio by sub-line.
  • Private credit insurance rate data: Allianz Trade, Atradius, and Coface do not publish premium rate schedules or rate change data. The premium rate charts in this hub are constructed from broker commentary and industry publications — they are directionally indicative but not authoritative.
  • Greensill final settlement: The ultimate settlement between TMHCC and Greensill's administrators has not been publicly disclosed. The claimed $5 billion exposure and the insurer's contested position remain unresolved in public reporting.
  • Chinese credit insurance market data: SINOSURE (China Export & Credit Insurance Corporation) — the world's largest ECA by premium volume — does not publish detailed product-level data in English. China's domestic credit insurance market data (domestic whole turnover, internal trade credit) is substantially unavailable to international researchers.
  • Supply chain finance credit insurance aggregate exposure: The total volume of credit insurance written to support SCF/receivables financing platforms is not separately tracked by Berne Union or ICISA. The Greensill episode revealed that significant credit insurance exposure had accumulated in non-traditional SCF structures without systematic market monitoring.
