Module 06 — Risk Mitigation

Credit & Contract Frustration — Risk Mitigation

Credit management controls, buyer monitoring tools, CF risk reduction strategies, regulatory standards, and practical claim guidance for policyholders.

Overview History Timeline Loss Database Underwriting Rates & ROE Risk Mitigation Global Programme References Rater

Credit insurance is most effective when it complements — not replaces — sound credit management. The best policyholders maintain disciplined buyer credit assessment processes, actively monitor their debtor book, and respond promptly to early warning signals of buyer distress. Insurers reward this discipline with lower rates and broader credit limits. The worst claims arise from policyholders who treat credit insurance as a substitute for credit management and extend open credit to buyers they should have declined. VER

Prevent — Credit Management Controls

Prevent · Credit Assessment

Buyer Due Diligence & Credit Assessment

All new buyers above a minimum threshold (typically £10,000–25,000) should be credit-assessed before open credit is extended. Standard tools: Dun & Bradstreet credit report, Experian commercial credit check, Companies House filing review, trade reference checks (2–3 supplier references). For overseas buyers: local credit agency report (Creditreform, BvD Orbis). Assessment documented and stored against buyer record.

Underwriting credit: −5 to −15%; mandatory for WT limits >£100k
Prevent · Limit Setting

Internal Credit Limit Policy

Formal internal credit limit policy — specifying who can approve limits, at what level, on what information, and how frequently limits are reviewed. Credit committee with approval authority for limits above threshold. Credit limits should be set conservatively — the buyer's ability to pay must be assessed independently from their current relationship importance. Limits set in excess of the buyer's debt capacity increase moral hazard.

Policy documentation required for WT underwriting approval
Prevent · Diversification

Debtor Book Diversification

Avoiding over-concentration in single buyers, single sectors, or single geographies reduces systemic portfolio risk. No single buyer should represent >15–20% of total insured turnover without explicit underwriter approval and individual buyer sub-limit endorsement. Sector concentrations >40% in any single industry (retail, construction, media) require additional scrutiny. Geographic diversification reduces correlated country-risk exposure.

Concentration management reduces pricing and improves limit availability
Prevent · Documentation

Trade Documentation & Terms Compliance

All credit sales must be supported by: signed purchase order or contract, delivery evidence, invoice compliant with policy terms, and clear credit terms documented. Payment terms must align with policy waiting period. Goods delivered on consignment, where title is not passed, may not meet policy coverage conditions. Confirm with broker/insurer that standard commercial terms are within policy scope before committing to unusual arrangements.

Documentation failure is the most common claim defence used by insurers
Prevent · Country Risk

Country Risk Monitoring & Limit Controls

For export policyholders: maintain a country risk watch list aligned with OECD country risk categories and political risk indicators (Oxford Analytica, EIU, Coface country ratings). Apply portfolio concentration limits by country tier — more conservative limits for cat 4–7 countries. Review country limits after material political events (elections, coups, sanction announcements, currency crises). Pre-emptively reduce limits on countries showing deterioration before insurer acts unilaterally.

Active country monitoring prevents forced limit reductions at worst time
Prevent · CF

Contract Structuring for CF Protection

For export contracts to high-risk country buyers: negotiate progressive payment structures (milestone payments) rather than end-loaded payment; include governing law in neutral jurisdiction (English law preferred); include force majeure clause compliant with policy CF trigger definitions; avoid arbitration in the buyer's country; ensure payment mechanism (LC, bank guarantee) is from an internationally accepted bank rather than a local bank subject to CI/TR risk.

Contract structuring directly affects CF coverage scope and claims quality

Detect — Early Warning Systems

Detect · Buyer Monitoring

Real-Time Buyer Credit Monitoring

Credit monitoring services (D&B Credit Monitor, Creditsafe Alerts, Graydon) provide automated alerts when monitored buyers' credit scores deteriorate, County Court Judgements (CCJs) are filed, insolvency events are registered, or payment behaviour changes materially. Many credit insurers also share their own credit intelligence on buyer populations — limit reductions by the insurer are themselves an early warning signal. Act on insurer limit reductions immediately — do not continue supplying on credit to a buyer whose limit has been cut without a coverage endorsement.

Detect buyer stress 30–90 days earlier than invoice overdue trigger
Detect · Accounts Receivable

Debtor Ageing Analysis & Overdue Tracking

Weekly debtor ageing reports — segmented by buyer, country, and credit terms — enable early identification of buyers approaching the policy waiting period. Buyers with debts overdue 30–60 days should trigger account review. Sales team incentives should not suppress credit management reporting of overdue debts. Early engagement with overdue buyers (payment plan, security agreement, credit note) often prevents a claim and preserves the trading relationship.

Weekly monitoring is mandatory best practice; monthly monitoring is inadequate
Detect · Country Risk

Political Risk Intelligence — CF Early Warning

Subscribe to political risk intelligence services (Oxford Analytica, EIU, Verisk Maplecroft, Control Risks) for key export markets. Monitor: election calendars, central bank FX reserve levels, IMF programme status, sanctions announcements (OFAC, EU, OFSI), government-seller relationship signals. Proactive reduction of credit exposure to deteriorating markets before a CF event occurs is far more effective than filing a CF claim after the fact.

Pre-emptive exposure reduction prevents uncovered CF losses
Detect · Sector Intelligence

Sector Credit Intelligence & Insolvency Tracking

Credit insurers publish quarterly sector intelligence reports highlighting industries under stress. Allianz Trade, Atradius, and Coface all publish sector risk assessments and country risk barometers. Policyholders should monitor their sector exposure against these intelligence reports — tightening internal credit limits in advance of predicted sector deterioration rather than reacting to defaults after the fact.

Sector anticipation prevents concentrated sector loss events

Respond — Overdue Debt & Claims Management

Respond · Overdue

Overdue Debt Notification to Insurer

Most WT and CR policies require notification to the insurer when a buyer's debt becomes overdue beyond a specified threshold (typically 30–60 days beyond the due date, or when the overdue amount exceeds a notified limit). Failure to notify within the required period can prejudice the claim or trigger a policy condition breach. Notify the insurer in writing at the first sign of material overdue debt — do not wait for the waiting period to expire.

Mandatory obligation; notification breach can void coverage
Respond · Legal Action

Prompt Legal Action Against Defaulting Buyer

Credit insurance does not remove the insured's obligation to pursue the buyer through legal channels. Most policies require the insured to take reasonable collection action before a claim can be paid in full — including issuing a formal demand letter, initiating legal proceedings if the amount justifies it, and filing for proof of debt in insolvency proceedings. Delay in legal action prejudices both the claim and the insured's recovery position. Retain specialist debt collection/insolvency solicitors immediately on buyer default.

Duty to mitigate loss; passive policyholders lose a portion of indemnity
Respond · Proof of Debt

Insolvency Claims — Proof of Debt Filing

In formal insolvency proceedings (administration, liquidation, CVA), the insured must file a Proof of Debt with the insolvency practitioner (IP) within the filing deadline. Failure to file means the insured's claim against the insolvent estate is lost — reducing recoveries and potentially reducing the net insured loss calculation. Co-ordinate with insurer on subrogation: the insurer takes over the debt claim once the indemnity is paid and has its own proof of debt rights.

Filing deadline typically 8–16 weeks from insolvency event; critical action
Respond · CF Claims

CF Claim Documentation & Cause Evidencing

For CF claims, the insured must evidence that: (1) the contract was frustrated by a government or political act within the policy's CF cause definition; (2) the frustrating event is the proximate cause of non-payment; (3) the buyer's failure to pay is not a commercial default that happened to coincide with a political event. Gather: news reports, official government announcements, buyer correspondence confirming political cause, sanctions notices, import/export prohibition orders, and central bank currency restriction notifications.

CF claims are contested more frequently than WT claims; documentation is critical

Recover — Post-Claim Recovery & Subrogation

Recover · Subrogation

Subrogation Co-operation

Once a credit insurance claim is paid, the insurer is subrogated to the insured's rights against the defaulting buyer — the insurer steps into the shoes of the creditor to pursue recovery. The insured must: assign the debt to the insurer formally, provide all documentation relevant to the debt, assist in legal proceedings, and not take any action that compromises the insurer's recovery position. Recovery proceeds are split between insurer and insured in proportion to their respective shares of the loss (indemnity % vs first-loss %).

Co-operation with subrogation is a mandatory post-claim obligation
Recover · Country Debt

Sovereign Debt Restructuring — Paris Club

For CF and CR losses involving sovereign or state-entity buyers, recovery may be pursued through the Paris Club (bilateral sovereign debt renegotiation mechanism), G20 Common Framework, or ICSID arbitration. ECAs typically co-ordinate recovery through these channels. Private market insurers may participate in Paris Club discussions as observers. Recovery timelines are long (3–10 years is common), and haircuts are typical in sovereign restructurings. Manage expectations with policyholders regarding recovery timelines.

Paris Club/G20 CF recovery timeline: 3–10 years; haircuts typical
Recover · Residual Credit

Residual Credit Relationship Management

Following a buyer default and credit insurance claim, the trading relationship with the buyer — if it survives restructuring — must be managed carefully. New credit should not be extended on open account terms to a buyer who has defaulted without explicit insurer approval and reinstatement of credit limits. Buyers emerging from insolvency (CVA, administration) may be reinstated on reduced, secured, or prepayment terms. Monitor the buyer's insolvency proceedings and trading recovery actively.

Post-default credit management prevents second-loss event on same buyer

Practical Claim Guidance

Before a Claim — Ongoing Obligations

  1. Maintain complete buyer records: credit assessment documentation, credit limit approval records, full trade history, all invoices, delivery notes, and correspondence.
  2. Review policy schedule annually — confirm coverage basis (claims-made vs occurrence), waiting period, indemnity percentage, maximum credit period, and excluded buyers or countries.
  3. Monitor insurer credit limit decisions in real time. A credit limit reduction is an early warning signal — reduce exposure immediately, do not continue trading on the old limit.
  4. Keep country risk watch list current — align internal country exposure limits with OECD category updates and insurer country guidance bulletins.
  5. Confirm that all insured buyers are within the policy's approved buyer scope — new buyers above the discretionary credit limit (DCL) threshold need insurer approval for coverage to apply.
  6. Understand the policy's maximum credit period — sales extended on terms longer than the maximum credit period may not be covered even if the invoice amount is within the approved limit.
  7. Review any potential exclusions in the policy wording that might apply to your trading arrangements (consignment stock, assignment of receivables, construction milestones, retentions).

During & After a Buyer Default — Critical Steps

  1. Notify immediately: As soon as a buyer payment is overdue by the policy's notification threshold (typically 30–60 days post-due date), notify the insurer in writing. Retain proof of notification.
  2. Stop shipments on open credit to the defaulting buyer immediately. Do not extend further unsecured credit while the debt remains overdue — this increases uninsured exposure.
  3. Issue a formal written demand letter to the buyer within 7 days of deciding to pursue a claim — date-stamp and retain all demand correspondence.
  4. For insolvency events: file a Proof of Debt with the appointed IP immediately on appointment — do not wait for insurer instruction. Missing the proof of debt window permanently prejudices recovery.
  5. Gather all supporting documentation: signed contracts, purchase orders, delivery evidence, invoices, payment records, and all correspondence with the buyer regarding the debt.
  6. For CF claims: document the political cause — official announcements, sanctions notices, government prohibition orders. Buyer's own written acknowledgement of political cause is valuable evidence.
  7. Co-operate fully with the insurer's loss adjuster — provide all requested documentation promptly. Delay increases the risk of partial or contested settlement.
  8. Once indemnity is paid, formally assign the debt to the insurer for subrogation and assist actively in all recovery proceedings. Do not independently settle the debt without insurer consent.
Anti-Avoidance Reminder — Insured's Duty of Utmost Good Faith: Credit insurance is subject to the doctrine of utmost good faith (uberrimae fidei) under the Insurance Act 2015 (UK). Material facts known to the insured that would affect the insurer's decision to accept risk or set terms must be disclosed at inception and renewal. This includes: known buyer distress, overdue debts at inception, previous credit limit declines by other insurers, disputes with the buyer, and any changes to payment terms or credit arrangements with insured buyers during the policy year. Failure to disclose material facts entitles the insurer to avoid the policy or reduce the claim proportionally. Credit insurance fraud — fabricated invoices, inflated credit limits, fictitious buyers — is a criminal offence.
