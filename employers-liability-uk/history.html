Era History
Pre-1880 — Common Law and the "Unholy Trinity"

The Doctrine of Common Employment and Employer Immunity

Before 1880, injured workers faced an almost insurmountable set of common law doctrines that effectively barred recovery from employers. These became known as the "unholy trinity": contributory negligence (any fault by the employee defeated the whole claim), volenti non fit injuria (workers were presumed to voluntarily accept the ordinary risks of their employment), and the doctrine of common employment (established in Priestly v Fowler [1837]) — an employer was not liable for injuries caused by the negligence of a fellow worker.

The Industrial Revolution created vast new categories of workplace risk — steam engines, railways, textile mills, coal mines — but the legal framework provided workers with virtually no redress when injured. The economic logic was explicit: courts felt that holding employers liable would suppress industrial enterprise. In practice, injured workers and bereaved families were left destitute. VER

Campaigners and trade unions agitated through the 1870s for legislative reform. The scale of industrial accidents was becoming a political embarrassment — newspaper reports of factory accidents and pit disasters drove public pressure for change. The select committee inquiry of 1876–77 documented the inequality between workers and employers before the law.

1880–1948 — Reform Legislation and Early Compensation Schemes

Employers' Liability Act 1880, Workmen's Compensation Acts, and the Road to Universal Cover

The Employers' Liability Act 1880 was the first significant legislative reform — it partially abrogated the doctrine of common employment, allowing workers injured by a fellow employee's negligence to sue in certain defined circumstances. However, the act was limited in scope, required proof of negligence, and was subject to contracting-out by employers. It fell far short of providing meaningful protection.

The Workmen's Compensation Act 1897, championed by Joseph Chamberlain, represented a revolutionary conceptual shift: for the first time, employers in defined industries (railways, mining, factories, quarries, laundries) were liable for compensation regardless of fault — a no-fault liability scheme that did not require the worker to prove negligence. Compensation was fixed: 50% of average weekly earnings up to a maximum. VER

The Workmen's Compensation Act 1906 extended the scheme to virtually all occupations and introduced disease coverage for a scheduled list of industrial diseases. Employers were required to carry insurance or demonstrate financial capacity to meet claims. This created the first de facto employers' liability insurance market in the UK.

The National Insurance (Industrial Injuries) Act 1946, implementing the Beveridge Report's recommendations, replaced the Workmen's Compensation scheme with a state-run industrial injuries benefit — removing the direct employer liability for industrial accidents and replacing it with a social insurance model. However, the right to sue the employer in tort for negligence was preserved alongside the state benefit.

1948–1980 — The National Scheme, NHS Creation and the EL (Compulsory Insurance) Act 1969

Making EL Compulsory: The 1969 Act and Its Regulatory Framework

The post-war welfare state created a dual system: state industrial injuries benefits for all workers, plus the right to sue the employer in tort for negligence or breach of statutory duty. The NHS (National Health Service) created in 1948 treated injured workers free at the point of need, but this did not remove the employer's civil liability — it merely meant the NHS rather than the worker bore the immediate medical cost, and the court could recoup NHS costs (later formalised in the NHS Injury Costs Recovery scheme).

By the 1960s it had become clear that many employers — particularly smaller businesses — carried no liability insurance. When they became insolvent following a serious accident, injured workers received no compensation despite having won in court. This scandal of uninsured judgment-proof employers drove the Employers' Liability (Compulsory Insurance) Act 1969, which came into force on 1 January 1972. VER

The Act is simple but powerful: every employer carrying on business in Great Britain who employs one or more employees must maintain approved EL insurance for at least £2 million (subsequently raised to £5 million by the EL (Compulsory Insurance) Regulations 1998 VER). The certificate must be displayed. Failure to insure is a criminal offence.

The Health and Safety at Work Act 1974 VER (HSWA 1974) simultaneously created the modern regulatory framework for workplace safety: the Health and Safety Executive, the general duty on employers to ensure the health, safety and welfare of employees "so far as is reasonably practicable," and powers of improvement and prohibition notices. HSWA 1974 created both criminal and civil exposure for employers — the regulatory framework that underpins the EL insurance product to this day.

1980–2000 — COSHH, the Asbestos Disease Wave and Ogden Tables

Long-Latency Disease Claims Emerge as the Dominant EL Liability Challenge

The 1980s brought the first wave of asbestos-related disease claims. Mesothelioma — a cancer caused by asbestos fibre inhalation, with a latency period of 20–50 years — began appearing in courts in significant numbers, filed by workers exposed to asbestos in shipyards, construction and manufacturing in the 1940s–1970s. The disease is always fatal; claims typically involved full life expectancy loss of earnings and substantial general damages.

The Control of Substances Hazardous to Health Regulations 1988 (COSHH) imposed new duties on employers to assess and control exposure to hazardous substances. While prospectively important, COSHH could not undo decades of past asbestos exposure — the liability pipeline had already been created.

Vibration white finger (hand-arm vibration syndrome, HAVS) became the other major occupational disease claim of the 1990s. British Coal miners who had used pneumatic drills for decades suffered serious vascular damage to hands and fingers. The Government settled a group action in 1999 for approximately £2 billion — the largest occupational disease settlement in UK history at that time. VER Lloyd's syndicates writing W3 in the relevant periods faced significant legacy reserve development.

The introduction of Ogden Tables (actuarial tables for multipliers in personal injury claims) provided a systematic basis for calculating future loss of earnings and care costs. The Courts Act 2003 VER later gave Ogden Tables statutory status, ensuring consistent application. The discount rate applied to Ogden multipliers became a critical variable for EL reserving.

2000–2015 — MOJ Portal, Jackson Reforms and LASPO

Civil Procedure Reforms Transform the EL Claims Environment

The early 2000s saw the EL market grapple with the legacy asbestos disease wave alongside rising frequency claims through the employer reform agenda. The Fairchild v Glenhaven decision VER in 2002 established the "material contribution to risk" test for mesothelioma causation — allowing claimants to recover from any one of multiple employers who had exposed them to asbestos, even where scientific uncertainty prevented identification of which exposure caused the cancer. This dramatically increased the scope of mesothelioma liability.

The MOJ Pre-Action Protocol for EL Claims (launched 2010) created a portal system for lower-value EL claims up to £25,000, with fixed recoverable costs and strict timetables. The portal intended to reduce friction and legal costs — in practice it generated significant volumes of lower-value claims and a cottage industry of claims management companies.

The Legal Aid, Sentencing and Punishment of Offenders Act 2012 (LASPO VER) reformed the conditional fee arrangement (CFA) regime — success fees were no longer recoverable from defendants, and after-the-event insurance (ATE) premiums were no longer recoverable. This significantly reduced claimant costs but also created new dynamics around claimant access to justice, with some cases becoming uneconomic to pursue.

The Jackson reforms, based on Lord Justice Jackson's 2010 review, introduced costs budgeting, proportionality in costs and Qualified One Way Costs Shifting (QOCS) — removing the exposure to adverse costs for personal injury claimants, effectively eliminating a key deterrent to weak claims.

2015–Present — Discount Rate Turbulence, Civil Liability Act and COVID

Ogden Rate Shock, Whiplash Reform, and Emerging Mental Health Claims

The single most disruptive event for EL (and all personal injury) reserving in the 2010s was the discount rate change. In February 2017, the Lord Chancellor set the Ogden discount rate at −0.75% — a rate that reflected investment in ILGS (index-linked government securities) but was dramatically lower than market expectations. At −0.75%, lump sum awards for future losses increased substantially, causing major reserve strengthening across the market. Lloyd's syndicates and commercial insurers collectively increased reserves by hundreds of millions of pounds.

After an industry-led consultation, the Civil Liability Act 2018 VER created a new framework for setting the discount rate: it is now reviewed every 5 years by the Lord Chancellor in consultation with the Government Actuary, using a "low risk" rather than "very low risk" investment assumption. The rate was revised to +0.25% in August 2019, providing partial relief but remaining significantly lower than the previous +2.5% that had applied from 2001 to 2017.

COVID-19 (2020–21) brought new EL exposures: key worker healthcare, care home, transport and retail employees contracting COVID-19 in the workplace pursued EL claims arguing employers failed to provide adequate PPE or implement social distancing. PTSD and mental health claims from frontline workers also increased significantly. EST The digital transformation of workplaces created new ergonomic injury and work-related stress claims from homeworkers.

Lloyd's W3 Market Context: Lloyd's syndicates write UK EL primarily as part of commercial package policies and standalone liability programmes for mid-market and large corporate risks — particularly those with complex risk profiles, international employee populations or industry sectors (construction, energy, healthcare, manufacturing) where admitted market capacity may be limited. Lloyd's estimated EL GWP (W3) is approximately £600–800 million. VER The asbestos disease long-tail remains a significant legacy run-off liability for syndicates that wrote UK casualty business in the 1970s–1990s.
Key Milestones
1880

Employers' Liability Act 1880

First legislative erosion of the doctrine of common employment — allowed workers injured by fellow employees to sue employers in defined circumstances.

1897

Workmen's Compensation Act 1897

Revolutionary no-fault compensation for workers in specified industries — regardless of negligence. Employer liability without fault established for the first time.

1906

Workmen's Compensation Act 1906

Extended no-fault compensation to virtually all occupations; introduced scheduled disease coverage. First de facto compulsory employer insurance market created.

1946

National Insurance (Industrial Injuries) Act

Replaced employer-funded Workmen's Compensation with state-run industrial injuries benefit, implementing Beveridge Report. Preserved civil right to sue employer.

1969

EL (Compulsory Insurance) Act 1969

Every UK employer must hold approved EL insurance. Criminal offence not to hold cover. Foundation of the modern W3 market. In force from 1 January 1972. VER

1974

Health & Safety at Work Act 1974

Created HSE and general employer duty to ensure health, safety and welfare of employees. Foundation of modern occupational safety regulation and EL civil liability. VER

1999

British Coal HAVS Settlement — £2bn

Group action by former coal miners suffering vibration white finger settled for approx. £2bn — largest occupational disease settlement in UK history at that time. VER

2002

Fairchild v Glenhaven [2002] UKHL 22

House of Lords establishes "material contribution to risk" for mesothelioma — any employer who materially contributed to asbestos exposure risk is fully liable. VER

2017

Discount Rate to −0.75%

Lord Chancellor sets Ogden discount rate at −0.75%, causing massive reserve strengthening across the EL market. Most significant single reserving event in modern EL history.

2019

Discount Rate Revised to +0.25%

Civil Liability Act 2018 framework sets rate at +0.25% — partial relief but structurally lower than previous +2.5%. New 5-year review cycle established. VER
