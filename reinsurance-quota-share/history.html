Quota Share — History & Evolution

How the simplest reinsurance structure became the workhorse of capital management — from the first professional treaties to Solvency II capital relief and the MGA capacity boom.

OverviewHistoryTimelineLoss DatabaseUnderwritingRates & ROERisk MitigationGlobal ProgrammeReferencesRater

Proportional treaty is the oldest form of modern reinsurance, and quota share its simplest expression. Its history is less about catastrophe and more about capital: each era found a new reason to share the whole book. VER

1846–1900 · Origins

The professional reinsurer and the treaty

The first professional reinsurers (Cologne Re 1846, Swiss Re 1863, Munich Re 1880) introduced treaty reinsurance — automatic, portfolio-wide cover agreed in advance — replacing risk-by-risk facultative placement. Proportional treaties, sharing premium and loss in a fixed ratio, were the natural first form and enabled primary insurers to grow beyond their own capital. VER

1900–1980 · Standardisation

Ceding & profit commission become the market

As proportional treaty matured, the ceding commission — reimbursing the cedant's acquisition and expense costs — became the central negotiation, and profit commissions and sliding scales emerged to align the two parties. The structure spread across every class as the standard way to share a book. EST

1980–2005 · Capital tool

Quota share as balance-sheet management

Insurers increasingly used quota share not just for capacity but as a deliberate capital lever — ceding a share of a growing book to release regulatory capital and fund expansion. "Financial" or structured quota shares blurred into finite reinsurance in this era. EST

2005–2016 · Solvency II

Capital relief formalised

Risk-based capital regimes — culminating in Solvency II (2016) — made the capital value of a quota share explicit: ceding a share of premium reduces the SCR proportionally. Quota share became a headline lever in every growing insurer's and Lloyd's syndicate's capital plan. VER

2016–Present · The MGA capacity era

Quota share powers delegated underwriting

The explosion of MGAs and fronting carriers made quota share the plumbing of delegated underwriting: the paper-issuer cedes most of the risk to reinsurers via quota share, earning a fronting fee. Ceding commissions and loss corridors became the key terms in a fast-growing, capacity-hungry market — and the first thing squeezed when a binder's loss ratio slips. VER

Forces That Shaped Quota Share

The treaty model

Automatic portfolio-wide cover, pioneered by the 19th-century professional reinsurers, made proportional sharing the default.

Commission structures

Ceding, sliding-scale and profit commissions turned a simple share into an aligned partnership.

Risk-based capital

Solvency II made the capital relief of a quota share explicit and central to insurer capital planning.

The MGA boom

Delegated underwriting made quota share the plumbing behind fronting and capacity provision.
