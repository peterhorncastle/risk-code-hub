Era History
Pre-1970 — Terrorism as a Perils-Based Coverage

Terrorism Included by Default: No Exclusion Required

Before the 1970s, terrorism was not systematically excluded from commercial property insurance. Politically-motivated violence was regarded as sufficiently rare that property insurers were content to write it as an unnamed peril within all-risks policies. The concept of a standalone terrorism market did not exist — terrorism losses were absorbed within the broader property loss pool alongside storm, flood and fire.

The IRA's early bombing campaigns in Northern Ireland during the late 1960s — the beginning of the Troubles — began to register in UK insurance statistics, but the losses were geographically concentrated and the insured values relatively modest by Lloyd's standards. The property market treated Northern Ireland as a rated hazard within property underwriting rather than a separate class of cover. VER

1970–1992 — IRA Mainland Campaign; Market Retreat Begins

Munich 1972, Brighton 1984, Baltic Exchange 1992 — Lloyd's Rethinks

The 1972 Munich Olympics massacre — though a travel and event loss rather than a UK property loss — signalled the globalisation of political terrorism as an insured peril. Through the 1970s and 1980s, the IRA extended its campaign to the British mainland: bombings at Harrods (1983), the Grand Hotel Brighton (1984, targeting the Conservative Party conference), and a sustained campaign against City of London financial infrastructure.

The scale of the IRA's mainland campaign forced Lloyd's syndicates to review their property policy wordings. By the late 1980s, terrorism exclusion clauses were increasingly being inserted into commercial property policies for UK risks, particularly for high-profile city-centre addresses. However, coverage practice was inconsistent — many policies still covered terrorism by default, and the market had not yet developed systematic terrorism underwriting. VER

The Baltic Exchange bombing on 10 April 1992 was a watershed. The IRA detonated a large truck bomb in St Mary Axe, killing three people and causing approximately £800m in insured losses — a single event that dwarfed all previous UK terrorism insurance losses. Lloyd's and the wider London market faced a shock that made the economics of unsystematic terrorism coverage starkly clear. The Baltic Exchange bomb was the direct commercial catalyst for Pool Re. VER

1992–2001 — Pool Re, Canary Wharf, and the US Pre-9/11 Market

UK Government Acts; US Market Complacent; Capacity Grows in London

Pool Re was established in 1993 under the Reinsurance (Acts of Terrorism) Act 1993, following industry and government negotiations prompted by the Baltic Exchange losses. Pool Re is a mutual reinsurance pool in which participating insurers can reinsure their UK commercial property terrorism exposure. The UK government stands as reinsurer of last resort for Pool Re — providing the ultimate backstop that makes the pool viable. At inception, Pool Re covered fire and explosion losses from terrorism; coverage was extended to include all-risks terrorism perils in 2002 and NBCR in 2017. VER

The Canary Wharf bombing on 9 February 1996 — another massive IRA truck bomb — tested Pool Re for the first time in earnest. The Docklands bomb caused approximately £185m in insured losses and confirmed that Pool Re's structure was working as intended, with the insurance market absorbing losses up to the pool threshold and Pool Re (and ultimately the government) picking up the excess. Lloyd's specialty terrorism capacity grew steadily through the late 1990s alongside Pool Re as the structural solution for UK risks.

Meanwhile, in the United States, commercial property policies still included terrorism coverage by default. The 1993 World Trade Center bombing ($750m insured), the Oklahoma City bombing (1995, $125m est.) and other events were absorbed within standard property policies without triggering any systemic rethink. US insurers and regulators did not foresee a catastrophic terrorism event — the market was entirely unprepared for what was coming on 11 September 2001. VER

2001 — 9/11: The Watershed That Created the Modern Market

$40bn+ Insured Loss; Universal Exclusions; TRIA Enacted

The terrorist attacks of 11 September 2001 produced an insured loss estimated at $40–47 billion — the largest insured loss from a single event in history at that time, surpassing Hurricane Andrew (1992). The losses were broad in scope: property damage, business interruption, workers' compensation, aviation hull, life insurance and liability claims all contributed. Lloyd's paid approximately $4.8bn of the total insured loss. VER

The immediate market response was decisive. Within weeks of 9/11, virtually every commercial property insurer and reinsurer globally inserted terrorism exclusion clauses into their policies. What had been a default-included peril became a default-excluded peril essentially overnight. The insurance market for terrorism collapsed — not because coverage was unavailable in theory, but because the risk was now recognised as catastrophic, correlated and uninsurable on standard terms.

The US Congress responded with the Terrorism Risk Insurance Act (TRIA) 2002, signed by President Bush in November 2002 (enacted December 2001, per implementation schedule). TRIA created a federal government backstop for certified acts of terrorism, obliging commercial insurers to make terrorism coverage available and sharing losses above insurer mandatory retentions. TRIA's enactment was the structural prerequisite for the US terrorism insurance market to function: without a government backstop for catastrophic terrorism events, private market capacity was insufficient to support the US commercial real estate and corporate sectors. VER

9/11 and the Birth of the Standalone Terrorism Market: Prior to 9/11, terrorism was an incidental peril within property insurance. After 9/11, it became a distinct, systematically priced class of insurance business. Lloyd's terrorism market (TO/TU/TW) grew from near-zero to hundreds of millions of pounds of GWP within five years of 9/11. Pool Re was reformed, TRIA enacted, GAREAT (France) created, and similar government pools established across Europe. The 9/11 attacks did more to shape modern terrorism insurance than any other event in history.
2002–2015 — TRIA Reauthorisations; Global Market Growth

Madrid 2004, 7/7 2005, Mumbai 2008, Boston 2013 — Steady Market Development

TRIA was reauthorised in 2005 (Terrorism Risk Insurance Extension Act, TRIEA) and again in 2007 (Terrorism Risk Insurance Program Reauthorization Act, TRIPRA). Each reauthorisation involved hard-fought negotiations in Congress, with insurers and commercial real estate interests lobbying strongly for continuation. The uncertainty around TRIA's future complicated long-term terrorism underwriting and drove demand for Lloyd's standalone capacity as a hedge against TRIA expiry. VER

Major terrorism events through this period reinforced the market's importance but did not produce losses approaching 9/11 scale. The Madrid train bombings (2004), London 7/7 attacks (2005), Mumbai 2008 attacks and Boston Marathon bombing (2013) all generated insurance claims — principally property damage, BI, and casualty — but were handled within the market's growing capacity without Pool Re or TRIA being triggered at scale. Lloyd's terrorism premium grew steadily as demand for coverage increased globally. VER

Political violence coverage also developed through this period: the Arab Spring (2010–11) and subsequent conflicts in Libya, Egypt and Syria created substantial political violence insurance claims — particularly for multinational corporates and project finance lenders with Middle East and North African exposure. RW and RS codes saw growing premium volumes. EST

2015–Present — Active Assailant, Ukraine War on Land, and Emerging Threats

Paris 2015, Las Vegas 2017, Ukraine 2022 — A Broadening Threat Landscape

The Paris attacks (November 2015), Nice Bastille Day attack (2016) and Manchester Arena bombing (May 2017) generated substantial insurance claims and also drove significant product innovation: the active assailant insurance product. Unlike traditional terrorism cover (which is primarily property-damage triggered), active assailant cover responds to mass casualty events at venues, hotels, retail spaces and offices — providing BI, medical costs, crisis management and third-party liability coverage even without significant property damage. EST

The Las Vegas Harvest music festival massacre (October 2017) — 60 killed, 400+ injured in the largest mass shooting in US history — produced an estimated insured loss of $750m+ across active assailant, general liability, event cancellation and workers' compensation policies. Las Vegas 2017 was the single event that established active assailant as a mainstream insurance product, driving rapid growth in Lloyd's AA capacity. VER

Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 was a transformative event for war on land (WL) underwriting. Lloyd's syndicates writing WL policies faced claims for property damage and business interruption from Ukraine-based insureds for the first time in the post-Cold War era. The invasion also prompted a fundamental rethink of aggregation management for war on land writers — Ukraine demonstrated that conflict could produce large and rapidly accumulating insured losses across multiple policyholders. EST

Emerging threats in 2023–26 include drone and UAV-delivered explosive attacks on critical infrastructure (relevant to both WL and terrorism covers), the cyber-terrorism interface (attacks that combine physical and cyber elements), and the geopolitical risk presented by the Israel/Gaza conflict (2023–) and rising tensions in the Taiwan Strait. The TRIA 2019 reauthorisation extended the backstop to December 2027. VER

Lloyd's Terrorism Market Today: Lloyd's is the global centre of gravity for standalone terrorism insurance. An estimated 30+ active syndicates write terrorism and political violence in various combinations of TO/TU/TW/RS/RW/WL. Market capacity (per risk limits) for major city-centre commercial properties can reach $1bn+, though realistic PML modelling and syndicate aggregation management typically constrain single-risk limits to $250–500m for high-exposure locations. Active assailant is the fastest-growing segment; war on land (WL) is under the most intensive review following Ukraine. EST
Key Milestones
1992

Baltic Exchange Bombing — £800m

IRA truck bomb in the City of London causes £800m insured losses — the direct commercial catalyst for Pool Re and the modern UK terrorism market.

1993

Pool Re Established

UK government-backed terrorism reinsurance pool established under the Reinsurance (Acts of Terrorism) Act 1993. Still the cornerstone of UK property terrorism coverage today.

1995

Oklahoma City Bombing — $125m

Domestic terrorism in the USA — first time a commercial building was the primary target. Absorbed within standard property policies; no structural market change results in US.

1996

Canary Wharf (Docklands) — £185m

IRA bomb tests Pool Re for the first time. Pool Re structure works as intended; Lloyd's specialty terrorism capacity grows. Docklands becomes a benchmark PML scenario.

2001

9/11 — $40–47bn Insured Loss

Largest insured terrorism event in history. Universal terrorism exclusions inserted within weeks. TRIA enacted December 2001/enacted 2002. Standalone terrorism market born globally.

2002

TRIA Enacted — US Government Backstop

Terrorism Risk Insurance Act creates a federal backstop for certified terrorism events, obliging US insurers to offer terrorism coverage and enabling the US market to function post-9/11.

2005

London 7/7 Bombings — £50m est.

Four coordinated suicide bombings on London Transport. Pool Re pays out; Lloyd's terrorism market handles claims efficiently. Demonstrates pool mechanism working at scale.

2017

Las Vegas — $750m Active Assailant

Largest US mass shooting loss. Drives rapid growth of active assailant insurance product globally. Lloyd's capacity for AA events doubles within two years of Las Vegas.

2019

TRIA 2019 Reauthorisation

Terrorism Risk Insurance Program Reauthorization Act extends TRIA to December 2027, providing long-term certainty for US terrorism market participants.

2022

Ukraine War — WL Policies Activated

Russia's invasion of Ukraine is the first major activation of Lloyd's war on land (WL) policies since the Cold War. Aggregation management and PML modelling for WL undergo fundamental review.
