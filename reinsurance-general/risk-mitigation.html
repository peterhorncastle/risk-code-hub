Reinsurance — Contract & Wordings

The clauses that decide who pays what: reinstatements, the hours clause, ultimate net loss, event definition, aggregation control, exclusions and collateral. For reinsurance, the wording is the risk mitigation.

Overview History Timeline Loss Database Underwriting Rates & ROE Risk Mitigation Global Programme References Rater

In primary insurance, risk mitigation is sprinklers and MFA. In reinsurance, the equivalent is precise contract wording: the clauses that define when the layer responds, how much it pays, how many times, and what is carved out. A single ambiguous word — what counts as "one event" — can be the difference between one claim and five. VER

Core Clauses Every Placement Turns On

Reinstatements

How many times the layer refills after a loss, and at what cost. "One reinstatement at 100%" means one more full limit, paid for by a reinstatement premium pro-rata to time and amount. Caps the reinsurer's annual exposure.

Hours Clause

Defines the time window (e.g. 72 hours for a hurricane, 168 for a windstorm) within which losses count as "one occurrence" — controlling how a rolling catastrophe aggregates into the layer.

Ultimate Net Loss (UNL)

Defines exactly which loss amounts the reinsurer indemnifies — including or excluding loss-adjustment expense — after all other recoveries. The base on which the layer attaches.

Event / Occurrence Definition

What binds separate losses into a single event. The Canterbury quake sequence showed how contested this can be, and how many millions ride on the wording.

Aggregation & Clash

How exposures across policies, lines and territories combine. Clash covers respond when one event hits multiple insureds or lines — the 9/11 problem.

Follow-the-Fortunes / Settlements

Binds the reinsurer to the cedant's good-faith claims decisions, provided they fall within the treaty terms — the glue of the proportional relationship.

Reinstatement Mechanics — Worked Illustration

£10m xs £10m, one reinstatement at 100% A first event costs the layer its full £10m. The layer reinstates, and the cedant pays a reinstatement premium equal to 100% of the original layer premium, pro-rated for the fraction of limit used (here, all of it) and the time remaining. The reinsurer is now exposed to a second full £10m for the rest of the period. After the second loss, with no further reinstatements, the layer is exhausted for the year.

Managing Counterparty & Aggregation Risk

Before binding

  • Check reinsurer security rating against your minimum (typically A-/A or better).
  • Model how the cede correlates with the retained book and existing retro.
  • Confirm reinstatement and event-definition wording match the programme.
  • For collateralised capacity, verify the trust and trapped-collateral terms.

During the period & at claim

  • Notify promptly and document the event against the hours clause.
  • Track cumulative erosion of each layer and reinstatements used.
  • Manage the aggregate: watch how one event erodes multiple programmes at once.
  • Monitor counterparty credit — a downgrade mid-period is itself an exposure.

Reinsurance Credit Risk — Recoverables

A reinsurer's promise is only as good as its rating and its collateral. The cedant carries reinsurance recoverables — amounts owed by reinsurers on paid and outstanding claims — as an asset, and a recoverable that does not pay converts a reinsured loss straight back into a net loss. Four checks define recoverable quality. VER

Concentration

Top-5 reinsurers' share of total recoverables. A net combined ratio of 88% means little if 30% of the recoverables behind it sit with one carrier — a single downgrade or dispute then threatens the whole net result.

Rating mix

Percentage of recoverables owed by carriers rated A- or better. Sub-investment-grade or unrated counterparties (often captives or lightly-capitalised collateralised vehicles) carry materially higher default risk.

Collateralisation

For non-admitted or unrated carriers, the share secured by trust funds, letters of credit or funds withheld. Collateral converts counterparty risk into custody/operational risk — which must itself be verified (see Vesttoo below).

Aging & disputes

Overdue and disputed balances. Reinsurance contracts are bespoke and slow to settle; wording disputes (occurrence definition, hours clause, contingent BI, silent cyber) have historically driven commutations at a discount — the COVID-19 BI test cases are the recent example.

Where to read recoverable quality In the US statutory return, Schedule F is the single best public disclosure — it imposes a statutory penalty (the "Schedule F charge") on uncollateralised recoverables from unauthorised carriers and on overdue balances, hitting statutory surplus directly. For non-US groups, the equivalent starting points are the IFRS 17 disclosures on reinsurance contracts held and Solvency II SFCR Section C (counterparty default risk). VER

Case study — the Vesttoo collateral fraud (2023)

Collateral only protects the cedant if it is real. Vesttoo positioned itself as a tech platform matching MGAs and fronting carriers needing capacity with institutional investors providing collateralised reinsurance via letters of credit. In mid-2023 it emerged that a large proportion of the LOCs — purportedly issued by a major bank — were forged, with industry exposure estimated at roughly $1.3–4bn; Vesttoo entered Chapter 11 in August 2023. VER

The lesson

Trace every letter of credit to the issuing bank and verify it independently — do not rely on the broker or the capacity manager. The chain of intermediaries can each disclaim responsibility.

The stress test

Ask what happens if the alternative capital evaporates mid-policy-year: does the fronting carrier retain the risk on its own balance sheet, and can it? A reinsurance-quality failure at the front becomes the cedant's existential crisis.

⚠️ The event-definition trap

Ambiguous occurrence and hours wording is the single most litigated feature of reinsurance. Before binding, be able to answer precisely: if two storms make landfall three days apart, or a quake sequence spans months, is that one event or several? The answer changes the recovery by multiples of the limit.
