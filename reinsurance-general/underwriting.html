Reinsurance — Pricing Methodology

How reinsurance is actually priced: proportional cession economics, and the three non-proportional methods — burning cost, exposure rating and rate-on-line — plus reinstatements, payback and the worked examples behind the Rater.

Overview History Timeline Loss Database Underwriting Rates & ROE Risk Mitigation Global Programme References Rater

Reinsurance pricing does not use rate-on-value like a primary policy. Proportional treaties are priced through cession economics — sharing premium and losses and negotiating a ceding commission — while non-proportional (excess-of-loss) layers are priced by estimating the expected loss to the layer and loading it for expenses, capital and profit. Getting fluent in both is the point of this module. VER

1 · Proportional Treaty Economics (QS & Surplus)

Under a proportional treaty the reinsurer takes a fixed share of the cedant's premium and pays the same share of losses. Because the reinsurer inherits the cedant's underwriting expenses, it pays a ceding commission back to the cedant, and often a profit commission if the treaty performs. The reinsurer's result is simply the ceded premium less ceded losses, ceding commission and brokerage.

Proportional — reinsurer result
Ceded Premium = GNPI × Cession %
Ceded Losses = Ceded Premium × Expected Loss Ratio
Ceding Commission = Ceded Premium × Ceding Comm %
Reinsurer Result = Ceded Premium − Ceded Losses − Ceding Commission − Brokerage
GNPI = Gross Net Premium Income (premium net of other reinsurance)
Ceding commission is the negotiation In a soft market cedants push ceding commissions up (35%+); in a hard market reinsurers pull them down. A sliding-scale commission that moves with the actual loss ratio, plus a profit commission, aligns both parties — this is the heart of every proportional renewal discussion.

2 · Non-Proportional Pricing — Three Methods

An excess-of-loss layer ("£5m xs £5m" means the reinsurer pays up to £5m of any loss above a £5m retention) is priced by estimating how much loss will fall into that layer, then loading it. There are three classic approaches, usually blended.

① Burning Cost (experience rating)

Trend and develop the cedant's historical losses, cap them to the layer, and divide by premium to get a rate. Best where there is credible loss history — working layers of casualty and per-risk property.

② Exposure Rating

Use exposure curves (property) or increased-limit factors (casualty), or a cat model (catastrophe), to estimate layer loss from the underlying portfolio — essential where history is thin or for high, rarely-hit layers.

③ Rate-on-Line / Market

Benchmark against what the market charges per unit of limit. Rate-on-line = premium ÷ limit; its reciprocal is the payback period. The sense-check every layer must pass.

Non-proportional — layer pricing
Expected Loss to Layer = Σ (developed losses capped to layer) ÷ years (burning cost)
Loss on Line (LoL) = Expected Loss to Layer ÷ Layer Limit
Technical Premium = Expected Loss × (1 + Risk Load) ÷ (1 − Expense % − Brokerage %)
Rate on Line (RoL) = Layer Premium ÷ Layer Limit
Payback Period = 1 ÷ RoL  ≈  Layer Limit ÷ Layer Premium
Reinstatement premium reloads the layer after a loss — see Risk Mitigation for the mechanics
Rate-on-line and payback intuition A cat layer priced at 10% RoL earns £1m for every £10m of limit — a 10-year payback if it loses a full limit once a decade. If the modelled loss-on-line is only 4%, that 10% RoL carries a healthy margin; if it's 9%, the layer is barely priced for its expected loss and leaves nothing for capital or volatility. RoL vs LoL is the fastest read on whether a layer is worth writing.

Worked Example A — Quota Share Treaty

40% Quota Share of a UK motor book

Cedant GNPI £50,000,000 · Cession 40% · Expected loss ratio 62% · Ceding commission 30% · Brokerage 2.5%
Ceded premium (£50m × 40%)£20,000,000
Ceded losses (£20m × 62%)−£12,400,000
Ceding commission (£20m × 30%)−£6,000,000
Brokerage (£20m × 2.5%)−£500,000
Reinsurer technical result+£1,100,000
Reinsurer combined ratio94.5%

The reinsurer keeps £1.1m, a 5.5% margin on ceded premium. Note how sensitive this is to the ceding commission: at 35% commission the treaty runs at a small loss, which is exactly why the commission is the negotiated pressure-point.

Catastrophe Pricing Metrics — Where the Modelled Loss Comes From

For a catastrophe layer there is rarely enough event history to burn a rate directly, so exposure rating leans on a catastrophe model (Verisk/AIR, Moody's RMS, CoreLogic) that simulates tens of thousands of synthetic years across a peril region. The model returns a small set of metrics — and the "modelled expected loss to layer" used in the worked example below is one of them. Fluency in these five metrics is what separates a cat underwriter from a pricing spreadsheet. VER

AAL — Average Annual Loss

The modelled expected annual loss, integrated over the full event set — the "actuarial premium" for cat exposure. The AAL falling into a layer is that layer's expected loss (the £600k in the example below). VER

PML — Probable Maximum Loss

Modelled loss at a stated return period — 1-in-100, 1-in-200, 1-in-250 are conventional. The single most-cited cat-exposure figure; it drives how high the tower is built and where layers attach. VER

OEP vs AEP

Occurrence (single largest event) vs Aggregate (sum of events in a year) Exceedance Probability. A per-occurrence programme can be well-bought on OEP yet under-bought on AEP in a multi-event season — see 2017 HIM and 2024 Helene/Milton. VER

TVaR / CTE

Tail Value at Risk / Conditional Tail Expectation — the expected loss given the loss exceeds the PML point. Captures the severity of the tail beyond the point estimate that a single PML misses; increasingly the Solvency II / ORSA standard. VER

From cat model to layer price
Expected Loss to Layer = AALlayer = modelled event losses capped to [attachment, attachment + limit], averaged over all simulated years
Loss on Line = AALlayer ÷ Layer Limit
→ feeds directly into the Technical Premium and Rate-on-Line formulas above
Lloyd's Realistic Disaster Scenarios (RDS) Every Lloyd's syndicate must test a compulsory set of RDS — two Atlantic hurricanes (Gulf, Florida–Carolinas), European windstorm, Japanese earthquake and typhoon, Californian earthquake, US terrorism, marine and aviation collisions, a satellite loss and a cyber scenario — plus two of its own. Each is reported gross, net of reinsurance, and net of reinstatement premiums. RDS is where cat pricing meets accumulation control: the same modelled event that prices a layer also sizes the syndicate's worst-case net exposure. VER
⚠️ The OEP / AEP trap A reinsurance programme structured purely against the single-event (OEP) view can leave a cedant badly exposed to a season of several sub-PML events whose aggregate (AEP) breaches the retention many times over. Always price and buy against both curves. VER

Worked Example B — Catastrophe Excess of Loss

£10m xs £10m property catastrophe layer

Layer limit £10,000,000 · Attachment £10,000,000 · Modelled expected loss to layer £600,000 · Risk load 40% · Expenses 5% · Brokerage 10% · 1 reinstatement at 100%
Expected loss to layer (modelled AAL to layer)£600,000
Loss on line (£0.6m ÷ £10m)6.0%
Loaded for risk (×1.40)£840,000
Grossed up for expenses + brokerage (÷ 0.85)£988,000
Rate on line (£0.988m ÷ £10m)9.9%
Payback period (1 ÷ RoL)~10.1 years
Margin over expected loss£388,000

A 6% loss-on-line priced at ~10% rate-on-line gives roughly £0.39m of margin per limit to cover capital cost and volatility. The single free reinstatement means the reinsurer can be exposed to two full-limit losses in the year for one up-front premium — the reinstatement premium partially funds the second.

Pre-Bind Checklist — Any Reinsurance Placement

  1. Structure & scope. Proportional or non-proportional? Treaty or facultative? Which classes, territories and perils are in and out?
  2. Exposure data. Is there credible loss history (burning cost) or must this be exposure/cat-model rated? How complete is the underlying portfolio data?
  3. Layer definition. Limit, attachment, per-risk vs per-occurrence, hours clause, and how "one event" is defined.
  4. Reinstatements. How many, at what cost, pro-rata to time and/or amount — and what is the maximum annual exposure?
  5. RoL vs LoL sense-check. Does the rate-on-line carry adequate margin over the modelled loss-on-line for capital and volatility?
  6. Aggregation & clash. How does this cede correlate with the rest of the book and with retrocession already purchased?
  7. Wording & exclusions. Ultimate net loss, cyber, communicable disease, terrorism, sanctions and follow-the-fortunes provisions.
  8. Counterparty & collateral. Reinsurer rating and, for collateralised/ILS capacity, the collateral trust and trapped-capital terms.
