Reinsurance — History & Evolution

How reinsurance grew from a fourteenth-century marine side-bet into a $648bn global capital market — shaped at every stage by the catastrophes that overwhelmed the capacity of the day.

Overview History Timeline Loss Database Underwriting Rates & ROE Risk Mitigation Global Programme References Rater

Reinsurance has always advanced in response to shock. Each generation's defining catastrophe exposed the limits of existing capacity and wordings, and the market's response — new companies, new structures, new capital — defined the era that followed. The through-line is the same across seven centuries: spreading a risk too large for one balance sheet across many. VER

1370 – 1842 · Origins

The Marine Side-Bet

The earliest known reinsurance contract was written in Genoa in 1370, ceding the riskier leg of a marine voyage to a second underwriter. For four centuries reinsurance remained an informal, individual marine practice. English law was suspicious of it — a 1746 statute effectively banned marine reinsurance in Britain until 1864 — so the practice migrated to the Continent, where it evolved into something more systematic. VER

1846 – 1905 · Professionalisation

The Birth of the Professional Reinsurer

The Cologne Reinsurance Company, chartered in 1846, was the first company formed solely to write reinsurance. A catastrophic 1861 fire in the Swiss town of Glarus prompted the founding of Swiss Re in 1863; Munich Re followed in 1880. These professional reinsurers introduced treaty reinsurance — automatic, portfolio-wide cover agreed in advance — replacing the risk-by-risk facultative model and enabling primary insurers to grow. VER

1906 – 1945 · The Catastrophe Age Begins

San Francisco and the Test of Capacity

The 1906 San Francisco earthquake and fire was the first modern catastrophe to test the global reinsurance system. Munich Re paid claims that confirmed the value of geographic diversification; the Lloyd's underwriter Cuthbert Heath famously instructed his agent to "pay all of our policyholders in full irrespective of the terms of their policies", cementing London's reputation. Two world wars then demonstrated the need for war exclusions and the accumulation risk of concentrated exposures. VER

1945 – 1991 · Expansion & Standardisation

Treaties, Towers and the Global Market

Post-war economic growth drove decades of expanding capacity. The modern architecture matured: layered excess-of-loss programmes, standardised treaty wordings, the retrocession market, and the reinsurance broker as a central intermediary. Reinsurance became genuinely global, with capital flowing between Europe, North America, Bermuda (from the 1970s) and Lloyd's to diversify peril and geography. EST

1992 – 2000 · The Modelling Revolution

Hurricane Andrew and the Bermuda Class of 1993

Hurricane Andrew (1992) caused roughly $15.5bn insured loss, bankrupted eleven insurers, and revealed that the market had systematically under-priced catastrophe risk because it lacked the tools to measure it. The response was twofold: catastrophe modelling firms (AIR, RMS, EQECAT) became central to pricing, and a wave of new, model-literate reinsurers capitalised in Bermuda — the "Class of 1993" — to supply the capacity the crisis had destroyed. VER

2001 – 2011 · Post-9/11 and the Second Bermuda Class

Terror, Katrina and Alternative Capital

The 11 September 2001 attacks produced an unprecedented multi-line loss (property, aviation, liability, life, workers' comp from a single event) and a fresh capacity crisis, answered by the "Class of 2001" Bermuda start-ups. Hurricane Katrina (2005) — around $65bn insured — triggered the "Class of 2005" and the first sidecars and catastrophe-bond growth, marking the arrival of alternative capital as a permanent feature. VER

2012 – 2023 · Convergence & the Solvency Era

ILS, Solvency II and the Hardening Market

Record-low interest rates pushed pension-fund capital into insurance-linked securities, softening rates through 2013–2017 as "convergence" capital competed with traditional reinsurers. Solvency II (2016) formalised the capital value of reinsurance across Europe. The 2017 hurricane trio (Harvey, Irma, Maria), successive secondary-peril years, social inflation and loss creep eventually drove a multi-year hardening that peaked at the January 2023 renewals — the sharpest property-cat repricing in a generation. VER

2024 – Present · Record Capital, Softening Rates

The Peak-Capital Market

Two years of exceptional reinsurer profitability rebuilt balance sheets to a record $648bn of dedicated capital by end-2025, while the cat-bond market grew 24% to $61.3bn outstanding. That weight of capital reversed the cycle: at the January 2026 renewals global property-catastrophe rate-on-line fell around 12%, the sharpest risk-adjusted decline since 2014 — the classic reinsurance cycle turning once more. VER

Figures Who Shaped the Market

Cuthbert Heath

Lloyd's underwriter whose "pay all" response to the 1906 San Francisco earthquake defined London's claims-paying reputation and pioneered excess-of-loss reinsurance.

The Cologne / Swiss / Munich Founders

Nineteenth-century industrialists and bankers who created the first standalone professional reinsurers and the treaty model that underpins the market today.

The Bermuda Classes (1993 / 2001 / 2005)

Successive waves of entrepreneurial start-ups — often ex-broker and ex-modelling-firm teams — who rebuilt catastrophe capacity after each crisis.

The Cat-Modelling Firms

AIR (1987), RMS (1988) and EQECAT turned catastrophe pricing from judgement into simulation, permanently changing how reinsurance is underwritten.
