History & Origins

From the Cuban revolution and Cold War nationalisations to the Iran hostage crisis, Venezuela's resource nationalism, and Russia's invasion of Ukraine — the story of how political risk insurance emerged as a specialist class and why it matters more than ever.

Political risk insurance is one of the most intellectually demanding classes written at Lloyd's. Unlike fire or hurricane, the peril is human, sovereign, and often opaque. Governments do not telegraph their intentions to underwriters. The class was born of historical catastrophe — the sudden, wholesale seizure of private property by revolutionary states — and has evolved over six decades to address the full spectrum of sovereign interference with commercial activity.

Era 1 · 1959–1970

Cuba and the Cold War — Birth of Political Risk Insurance

The Cuban Revolution (January 1959) and Fidel Castro's subsequent nationalisation of US and foreign business assets — estimated at $1.8bn (1960 values) — marked the event that galvanised the political risk insurance market into existence. VER US sugar companies, oil refineries (Esso, Texaco, Shell), banks and hotels were seized without compensation. The US had no insurance mechanism; businesses simply lost their investments.

  • The US government responded by creating the Overseas Private Investment Corporation (OPIC) in 1969 — a government agency providing PRI for US investors in developing countries, still operating today as the DFC (Development Finance Corporation). VER
  • Lloyd's syndicates began writing political risk (initially called "confiscation insurance") in the early 1960s, largely for extractive industry clients — oil majors, mining companies — with assets in newly decolonising African and Latin American states
  • The Congo Crisis (1960–65): Belgian withdrawal from the Congo; foreign business assets seized; demonstrated the speed at which nationalisation could destroy investment in former colonies
  • Algeria nationalisations (1963–71): Algerian government progressively nationalised French-owned assets following independence; key early PRI loss event
  • Indonesia: President Sukarno's nationalisation of Dutch assets (1957–65); Pertamina oil company created from seized Shell/Caltex operations
Era 2 · 1970–1980

Resource Nationalism Wave — OPEC, Chile, Iran

The 1970s was the defining decade for political risk insurance. Resource nationalism — the doctrine that a country's natural resources belong to its people, not foreign investors — swept through the developing world. The OPEC oil embargo (1973) demonstrated the power of collective sovereign action, while a wave of individual nationalisations targeted foreign mining, oil and industrial companies across three continents.

  • Chile (1971): President Allende nationalised Anaconda and Kennecott copper mines — among the world's largest copper producers. Insured losses of ~$500m (1971). Preceded by Pinochet's coup (1973) — demonstrating how political risk cuts both ways: left-wing nationalisation and right-wing coup violence. VER
  • Libya (1973): Gaddafi nationalised 51% stakes in all oil companies; BP among those affected; Libyan assets effectively taken without adequate compensation
  • Iran (1979): The Islamic Revolution and subsequent hostage crisis (444 days) produced the most complex political risk event of the 20th century. US companies lost ~$2bn in assets; Iran-US Claims Tribunal at The Hague took 30+ years to resolve claims. VER
  • MIGA (Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency) established by World Bank in 1988 as a result of the recognition that bilateral political risk insurance mechanisms were insufficient. VER
  • The Energy Charter Treaty (ECT) — conceptualised in this era, formally signed 1994 — established the first multilateral investment protection framework for the energy sector
Era 3 · 1980–2000

BIT Proliferation — The Treaty Framework Develops

The 1980s and 1990s saw an explosion of Bilateral Investment Treaties (BITs) — legal agreements between two countries that set minimum standards of treatment for each other's investors, including protection against expropriation without prompt, adequate and effective compensation. By 2000, over 2,000 BITs had been signed globally, fundamentally changing the political risk landscape. VER

  • The UK model BIT (first UK BIT signed with Egypt 1975; over 100 UK BITs now in force) gave Lloyd's-market insureds strong treaty protection frameworks in most key emerging market territories VER
  • ICSID (International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes) established at World Bank 1966 but grew rapidly in importance through 1990s; provided neutral arbitration forum for investor-state disputes VER
  • Former Soviet Union dissolution (1991): 15 new states; privatisation of state assets; foreign direct investment surge into Russia and Central Asia; new PRI demand for oil & gas, telecoms, financial services
  • Iraq invasion of Kuwait (1990): Kuwait's sovereign wealth fund (KIA) — $100bn+ of overseas investments — demonstrated that even a sovereign could need political risk protection for its own outward investments
  • South Africa political transition (1990–94): peaceful democratisation; Nelson Mandela presidency — positive political risk story; investor confidence restored; shows political risk evolves in both directions
  • Asian financial crisis (1997–98): currency inconvertibility losses; capital controls imposed by Malaysia; Thailand, Indonesia devaluations — CI/TR covers triggered extensively
Era 4 · 2000–2014

Venezuela, Bolivia, Argentina — Latin American Resource Nationalism Redux

The commodities supercycle (2002–2014) coincided with a wave of left-wing governments across Latin America — Venezuela (Chavez), Bolivia (Morales), Ecuador (Correa), Argentina (Kirchner) — that reasserted state control over natural resources. This era produced the highest volume of political risk claims in the class's history, concentrated in the oil, gas and mining sectors.

  • Venezuela (2002–2013): Chavez nationalised the oil industry (PDVSA), gold mines, steel mills, agricultural land, banks and telecommunications. Estimated $30bn+ in investor losses. ConocoPhillips and ExxonMobil brought ICSID claims; ConocoPhillips was eventually awarded $8.7bn in 2019. VER
  • Bolivia (2006): President Morales nationalised the gas industry on 1 May 2006; military deployed to 56 oil fields. Repsol, Total, Petrobras and British Gas all affected. ICSID claims filed; settlements reached. VER
  • Ecuador (2009–10): President Correa renegotiated oil contracts; OXY (Occidental) had its licence terminated in 2006; $1.8bn ICSID award against Ecuador in 2012. VER
  • Argentina (2012): President Fernandez nationalised YPF (formerly Repsol); $5bn ICSID claim; settled for $5bn in 2014. VER
  • Zimbabwe land seizures (2000–2008): Mugabe's fast-track land reform programme; white-owned commercial farms seized; agricultural devastation; PRI claims from foreign agricultural investors and their banks
  • Arab Spring (2011): Egypt, Libya, Tunisia, Syria, Yemen; political violence losses; CEND losses in Libya (Gaddafi regime collapsed); contract frustration across MENA
Era 5 · 2014–2024

Crimea, Coups, and Ukraine — The Modern PRI Market

Russia's annexation of Crimea in March 2014 was the first major political risk event in Europe since the Cold War. Foreign investors in Crimea — banks, utilities, telecoms companies — lost assets literally overnight. The Ukrainian government maintained that Crimea was Ukrainian territory; Russian law applied de facto; international law recognised neither. PRI policies faced entirely novel coverage questions. VER

  • Crimea 2014: ~$3bn investor losses; PRI claims paid across Lloyd's market and commercial insurers; set precedents for territorial seizure coverage interpretation
  • Sahel coup wave (2020–2023): Mali (Aug 2020, May 2021), Guinea (Sep 2021), Burkina Faso (Jan 2022, Sep 2022), Niger (Jul 2023), Gabon (Aug 2023) — seven coups in three years; French military expelled; contracts cancelled; mining licences revoked VER
  • Myanmar coup (February 2021): military seized power; Tatmadaw imposed CEND on foreign-JV businesses; Total/TotalEnergies and other oil majors faced forced exit VER
  • Russia/Ukraine war (February 2022): Largest political risk event in modern insurance history. Western sanctions triggered Russian counter-confiscation of assets; hundreds of multinationals lost tens of billions in Russian investments. Rosneft BP stake (19.75% = ~$14bn) — BP took $24bn write-down. McDonald's, IKEA, Renault, Shell, BP, Shell all wrote off Russian assets. Ukraine — simultaneous war damage claims under political violence covers. Estimated $40bn+ total insured exposure — much still unresolved. VER
  • ECT withdrawal crisis (2022–24): EU members withdrew from Energy Charter Treaty following adverse rulings requiring compensation for fossil fuel phase-out (e.g., Germany's nuclear phase-out claim); significant policy uncertainty for long-term energy investment protection

The Russia Problem — Why Ukraine 2022 Changed the Market

Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine created a perfect storm for political risk insurers:

  • Simultaneous bilateral exposure: Policies written on Ukraine suffered political violence losses from the invasion; policies written on Russia suffered CEND losses from confiscation and sanctions-forced exit — both at the same time
  • War exclusion interaction: Traditional PRI war exclusions were designed for foreign wars, not the host country's own military action against another state
  • Sanctions complexity: Western sanctions on Russia prevented insurers from paying Russian counterparties; Russian counter-sanctions created their own coverage questions
  • Scale: The combined insured exposure to Russia and Ukraine across Lloyd's, AIG, Zurich and others may exceed $40bn — dwarfing any previous political risk event
  • Market response: Russia and Belarus excluded from all new PRI placements from March 2022; premiums for adjacent high-risk markets (Central Asia, MENA) increased 30–80%
