Six Landmark Cases

1964 — House of Lords — Foundational

Hedley Byrne & Co Ltd v Heller & Partners Ltd [1964] AC 465

The House of Lords established that a duty of care can arise for negligent misstatement causing purely economic loss, where there is a 'special relationship' characterised by the defendant assuming responsibility for the statement and the claimant reasonably relying on it. The case involved a credit reference given by Heller about a client of Hedley Byrne; Heller escaped liability on the facts due to a disclaimer, but the principle of negligent misstatement liability was established. Every FI PI claim traces its legal foundation to this case — it created the framework for adviser negligence liability that has been applied and developed in financial services for 60 years.

Foundation of all financial adviser negligence claims
1994 — FSA Pensions Review — Systemic Redress Precedent

UK Pensions Transfer Mis-Selling Review (1994–2002)

Following the FSA's investigation of approximately 2 million pensions transfer recommendations (1988–1994), the regulator imposed a systematic review and redress programme. The review required firms to compensate all clients who had been transferred from defined-benefit occupational schemes to personal pensions where the transfer had been detrimental. Total redress exceeded £12 billion. The process established the template for regulatory-driven mis-selling redress — a model subsequently applied to endowment mortgages, split capital trusts, PPI, and DB pension transfer advice. The scale of the claims caused multiple small IFA insurers to become insolvent; Lloyd's capacity was strained.

Established systemic redress programme template; caused first FI PI insolvencies
2012 — Court of Appeal — Retail Suitability Standard

Rubenstein v HSBC Bank plc [2012] EWCA Civ 1184

Mr Rubenstein sought a safe haven for funds pending a property purchase. HSBC's adviser recommended the AIG Enhanced Variable Rate Fund, characterised internally as a 'cash equivalent'. The fund suffered losses of approximately 70% during the GFC. The Court of Appeal found that HSBC had breached its duty of care — the fund was not the safe, cash-equivalent investment the client required, and the adviser had not conducted an adequate assessment of the client's risk appetite and investment purpose. The case sets the benchmark for retail suitability claims: advisers must positively ascertain client objectives and risk tolerance, not assume. Failure to do so establishes negligence regardless of any general risk warnings given.

Primary retail suitability benchmark; widely cited in FI PI litigation
2012 — Federal Court Australia — Rating Agency Liability

Bathurst Regional Council v Local Government Financial Services [2012] FCAFC 78

ABN AMRO structured and sold CPDOs (Constant Proportion Debt Obligations) rated AAA by Standard & Poor's. Australian local councils invested AUD 20 million relying on the AAA rating. When the CPDOs collapsed, the Federal Court found S&P liable for negligently assigning an AAA rating that was not justified by the product's risk profile; ABN AMRO was liable for assisting in the issue of misleading ratings information. The case was the first successful legal challenge to a credit rating agency's structured credit ratings globally and established that rating agencies owe a duty of care to investors who rely on their ratings in making investment decisions.

First successful rating agency negligence claim for structured credit; CPDO precedent
2021 — Court of Appeal — SIPP Operator Duty of Care

Adams v Options UK Personal Pensions LLP [2021] EWCA Civ 949

Mr Adams was introduced to Options SIPP by an unregulated introducer and directed his pension funds into non-standard investments that proved worthless. The Court of Appeal confirmed that SIPP operators can owe a duty of care to members even where they do not give investment advice, in circumstances where they know or should know that the member's investment decision has been made without understanding the true nature of the investment or following improper inducements. The decision significantly expanded the PI exposure of SIPP operators by establishing that a platform that accepts non-standard assets without adequate due diligence may be liable for consequent losses — regardless of whether the operator gave any advice.

Extended SIPP operator duty of care beyond advice; affects non-advised platform exposure
2024 — Court of Appeal — Consumer Credit Commission Disclosure

Johnson v Firstrand Bank Ltd / Wrench v Firstrand Bank Ltd [2024] EWCA Civ 1229

The Court of Appeal ruled that motor finance dealers who arranged discretionary commission arrangements (DCAs) with lenders acted as brokers in a fiduciary relationship with customers, and were required to disclose the commission arrangement. Failure to disclose amounted to a breach of fiduciary duty and an unlawful financial promotion. The ruling creates an estimated £16bn+ industry redress exposure across motor finance lenders and dealers. The FCA immediately opened a broad review of historical motor finance commission arrangements. The case represents the most significant expansion of consumer credit PI exposure since PPI, and is expected to generate substantial FI PI claims against brokers and introducers who facilitated DCA arrangements without disclosure.

Biggest consumer credit mis-selling exposure since PPI; £16bn+ estimated redress

Identified Data Gaps

GAP 1 — Lloyd's FI PI GWP by Sub-Segment

Lloyd's Performance Management Directorate publishes combined professional lines GWP but does not provide publicly available breakdowns between FI PI (F2/F3), general PI (E2/E3), tech PI (F4/F5), and construction PI (E6/E7). Segment GWP percentages in this hub are estimated from broker market intelligence. A granular public breakdown would significantly improve premium volume accuracy.

GAP 2 — FI PI Loss Ratio by Advice Category

No public dataset disaggregates FI PI loss ratios by advice category (DB transfer vs vanilla funds vs structured products vs ESG). This is the most critical underwriting intelligence gap — the loss ratio for DB transfer advice business is materially higher than the market average, but underwriters must estimate this from experience rather than published data. A class-level loss development study by advice category would transform underwriting precision.

GAP 3 — Consumer Duty Claims Quantum (2023–2026)

Consumer Duty came into force July 2023. Claims arising from Consumer Duty failures are expected to materialise 2025–2027 as the ongoing monitoring obligation creates new grounds for complaint. No actuarial study has yet modelled Consumer Duty-specific FI PI claims quantum; existing loss ratio data does not reflect Consumer Duty obligations. This is the principal unknown in forward-looking FI PI reserving.

GAP 4 — ESG Greenwashing Civil Claim Volume

Regulatory ESG enforcement actions (SEC/DWS, BaFin, FCA SDR) are established, but the volume and quantum of resulting civil PI claims against fund managers and advisers is not yet quantified. The 2–3 year enforcement-to-civil-claim lag means the first significant ESG PI claims are expected 2025–2027. No published market study models ESG PI claims exposure for the Lloyd's FI PI class.

GAP 5 — Motor Finance DCA Claims Allocation (PI vs Consumer Credit)

The Court of Appeal's October 2024 motor finance DCA ruling generates exposure that sits at the intersection of consumer credit liability, broker PI, and potentially D&O. It is not yet clear what proportion of the estimated £16bn+ industry exposure will generate FI PI claims against credit brokers vs direct bank liability vs D&O claims. FCA's ongoing review and any subsequent Supreme Court decisions will determine the allocation. This gap is material for FI PI aggregate modelling.
