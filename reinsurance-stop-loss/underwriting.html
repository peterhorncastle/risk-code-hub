Stop-Loss — Underwriting & Pricing

A stop-loss is priced from the distribution of the whole-year loss ratio: how likely it is to breach the attachment, and how much of the layer it fills when it does.

Because a stop-loss responds to the sum of a year's losses, it cannot be priced from individual losses. The underwriter needs a view of the aggregate loss-ratio distribution — its mean, its spread and its shape — and then integrates the layer against it. VER

The Stop-Loss Expected Cost

Expected loss to layer = E[(L − a)⁺] − E[(L − b)⁺]  (as loss-ratio points)

where L is the whole-year loss ratio, a the attachment and b the exhaustion.

Expected recovery = Expected loss to layer × (1 − co-participation) × Subject premium

Technical premium = Expected recovery × (1 + risk load) ÷ (1 − expenses − brokerage)

Rate-on-line = Premium ÷ Layer £  ·  Payback = 1 ÷ Rate-on-line

Modelling the Aggregate

Empirical / historical

Use the cedant's own loss-ratio history, trended and on-levelled, and read the layer cost directly off the years that breached it. Needs a long, stable history.

Fitted distribution

Fit a distribution to the loss ratio — normal for a first cut, but usually lognormal or a compound frequency-severity model because aggregate losses are right-skewed.

Frequency × severity

Simulate the number and size of losses and sum them, repeating many times, to build the aggregate distribution from the ground up — the most rigorous approach.

Why the normal model is only a teaching tool The rater on this hub models the loss ratio as a normal distribution because it is transparent and computes in the browser. Real aggregate losses are skewed — big years are further from the mean than small years — so production pricing uses a lognormal or a simulated compound distribution. Treat the rater's numbers as illustrative of the mechanics, not as a market quote.

Worked Example — A Loss-Ratio Stop-Loss

Setup: Subject premium £50m; cover 90%–110% loss ratio; expected loss ratio 80%; volatility (σ) 12 points; 5% cedant co-participation; risk load 25%; expenses 5%; brokerage 10%. ILLUS

  • Probability the loss ratio breaches 90% ≈ 20% (about a 1-in-5 year).
  • Expected loss to the 90%–110% layer ≈ 1.34 points of loss ratio.
  • Expected recovery = 1.34% × (1 − 5%) × £50m ≈ £0.64m.
  • Technical premium ≈ £0.64m × 1.25 ÷ 0.85 ≈ £0.94m.
  • Layer £ = 20 points × £50m = £10m; rate-on-line ≈ 9.4%; payback ≈ 11 years.

A low rate-on-line and long payback are typical of a genuine tail stop-loss — it rarely pays, but caps a disaster year.

The Moral-Hazard Problem

Because a stop-loss covers the whole account, it can dull the cedant's incentive to underwrite well once a bad year is already breaching the layer. Co-participation, a sensible attachment above the expected loss ratio, an annual cap and alignment features are how the reinsurer keeps the cedant's skin in the game. VER

Pre-Bind Checklist

Build the aggregate loss-ratio distribution from a long, on-levelled history.
Set the attachment clearly above the expected loss ratio to avoid pricing attrition.
Agree the co-participation and the annual aggregate cap (exhaustion).
Check that the stop-loss does not simply duplicate the per-risk and cat towers below it.
Stress the moral-hazard exposure and confirm alignment features.
