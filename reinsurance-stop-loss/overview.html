Stop-Loss / Aggregate — Overview

The backstop of the reinsurance programme: a stop-loss caps the cedant's whole-year result. When the total loss ratio breaches an attachment — say 90% — the reinsurer pays the excess up to an exhaustion point, protecting against a bad year however it arises.

An aggregate stop-loss (also "stop-loss" or "aggregate excess") is non-proportional cover on the sum of a year's losses, usually expressed as a loss ratio. Where per-risk XoL answers one big loss and catastrophe XoL answers one big event, stop-loss answers a bad year — from any mix of frequency and severity. It is the widest, last-resort protection a cedant can buy for its net account. VER

How a Stop-Loss Works

Attachment (as a loss ratio)

Cover begins when the year's loss ratio breaches the attachment — e.g. 90%. Below it the cedant carries the whole result.

Exhaustion (the top)

The reinsurer pays up to an exhaustion loss ratio — e.g. 110% — so a 90%–110% stop-loss covers a 20-point band of loss ratio.

Co-participation

The cedant usually keeps a share (say 5–10%) of the losses within the layer, to preserve underwriting discipline.

Annual aggregate cap

Because it responds to the whole year, a stop-loss is always capped — the exhaustion point is the reinsurer's maximum.

Priced on the aggregate

Rating uses the distribution of the whole-year loss ratio — from history or a fitted aggregate model — not individual losses.

Widest protection

It catches frequency, severity, attrition and accumulation together — which also makes it the hardest to price and the most exposed to moral hazard.

Where It Is Used

Crop & agriculture

Its natural home — weather-driven books where a whole season can turn on one drought or flood.

New or volatile lines

A cedant entering an unproven class buys a stop-loss to cap the worst-case first-year result.

Capital & volatility management

Smooths the net result and reduces the capital a volatile book demands under Solvency II.

Small / mutual insurers

A whole-account backstop for insurers without the balance sheet to absorb a bad year.
