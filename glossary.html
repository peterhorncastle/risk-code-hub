Catastrophe modelling 28

AEP

Aggregate exceedance probability — the EP curve built from the total of all events in each simulated year.

Answers "how bad can a year be?" and is the curve that aggregate covers and the capital standing behind a whole year respond to. AEP is never below OEP and exceeds it wherever a year holds more than one event. Quoting the wrong one leads to buying the wrong protection or holding the wrong capital.

See also: oep · ep curve

Aleatory uncertainty

The irreducible randomness of the physical world — which events occur and at what intensity.

Quantified by the stochastic catalogue itself. It is the risk the insurer is paid to carry and no amount of additional science removes it. Contrast epistemic uncertainty, which can in principle be reduced and is the reason two vendors disagree.

See also: epistemic · secondary uncertainty · stochastic catalogue

Annual average loss (AAL)

The expected loss in a year, averaged across the whole distribution. The primary pricing reference from a cat model.

Computed as the sum of each event’s annual rate multiplied by its loss, and equivalently as the area under the EP curve. Expressed as a percentage of TIV it becomes a loss rate, comparable across portfolios of different sizes. It is the mean of a heavily skewed distribution, so in most years the actual loss falls below it.

See also: ep curve · tiv · pml

Catastrophe model

Software that estimates a portfolio’s loss distribution from catastrophes, built from four components run in sequence.

Hazard, exposure, vulnerability and financial. Hazard asks what the physical world does at a location; exposure describes what is standing there; vulnerability converts intensity into a damage ratio; the financial module applies the policy. The chain is run for every event in a catalogue of thousands, and the summary statistics — AAL, EP curve, PML — are descriptions of the resulting distribution. A cat model does not predict events; it reports what came out of many synthetic futures.

See also: hazard module · vulnerability module · financial module · ep curve

Catastrophe Model Return (CMR)

The Lloyd’s submission of modelled catastrophe losses at prescribed return periods and perils, using approved vendor models.

A hard regulatory deadline with a defined format, and where a syndicate’s EP curve actually ends up. Distinct from the RDS: the CMR reports probabilistic output, the RDS reports named deterministic scenarios, and the two answer different questions. Not to be confused with the road-haulage convention of the same initials.

See also: rds · ep curve

COPE

Construction, Occupancy, Protection, Exposure — the four building-attribute categories that decide which damage function applies.

Construction class and year built are usually the most consequential: they select the damage function, and a wrong construction code sends an entire location down the wrong curve. COPE is the bridge between the hazard module, which knows only intensity, and the vulnerability module, which needs to know what is being hit.

See also: sov · vulnerability module · damage function

Damage function

The curve mapping hazard intensity to expected damage ratio for one building type.

Convex in intensity: the last few metres per second of a storm do far more damage than the first few. That convexity is why a modest shift in modelled hazard produces a large shift in modelled loss, and it is the most important shape in catastrophe modelling. A separate curve exists for each combination of construction class, occupancy and age.

See also: vulnerability module · damage ratio · intensity metric

Damage ratio

The proportion of total insured value expected to be destroyed at a given intensity. The vulnerability module’s output.

A mean, not a certainty: actual damage at a known intensity scatters widely around it, and that scatter is secondary uncertainty. Multiply the damage ratio by TIV and you have the ground-up loss. A high damage ratio on a small value is a smaller loss than a low ratio on a large one, which is why the ratio alone says nothing about cost.

See also: damage function · secondary uncertainty · ground up loss · tiv

Demand surge

Post-event inflation of repair costs, driven by simultaneous excess demand for labour and materials across a whole affected region.

First systematically quantified after Hurricane Andrew and now a standard model component. It inflates every claim from one event at once, which is a correlation effect on top of the correlation the event itself creates. Loss amplification is the wider term, taking in litigation and claims-handling dynamics as well as construction costs.

See also: non modelled items · cat model

EP curve

Exceedance probability curve — every loss level mapped to its annual probability of being exceeded. The primary output of a cat model.

Built by arranging event losses into simulated years and sorting them. Individual return-period figures are single points read off this curve. The area under it is the annual average loss, which is why a model that changes its EP curve has necessarily changed its AAL.

See also: oep · aep · return period · aal

Epistemic uncertainty

Uncertainty from incomplete scientific knowledge — things the model could in principle know better.

How often a storm of a given severity really occurs, how a given building really responds. This is why two vendor models produce materially different losses for the same portfolio, sometimes by half as much again, without either being wrong. Epistemic uncertainty is loaded for; it is the reason a technical price carries a margin over the modelled expected loss.

See also: aleatory · non modelled items

Financial module

The cat model component that applies policy conditions, turning ground-up loss into insured loss.

Coverage scope first, then deductible, then limit, then reinsurance. Arithmetically simple and commercially decisive: deductibles bite hardest on the frequent moderate events, so a high-frequency peril can produce substantial physical damage and no insured loss at all.

See also: ground up loss · cat model · tiv

Geocoding

Converting an address into coordinates so a location can be placed on the hazard grid.

The accuracy of the coordinate sets a ceiling on the accuracy of everything after it. Where no usable address exists the model may fall back to a country centroid — a point in the middle of the country, often hundreds of kilometres from the real risk — which produces a hazard score that is not wrong so much as meaningless.

See also: sov · hazard module

Ground-up loss

Total physical damage before any policy terms. Gross loss is after primary policy conditions; net loss is after reinsurance recoveries.

Three different quantities that are frequently confused. Ground-up is what it costs to repair; gross is what the insurer owes the policyholder; net is what the insurer keeps and its capital must absorb. The gap between ground-up and gross is the deductible and the limit; the gap between gross and net is the reinsurance programme.

See also: financial module · damage ratio

Hazard footprint

The geographic spread of intensity from a single catalogue event — a hurricane’s wind field, an earthquake’s ground motion pattern, a flood’s inundation extent.

The footprint is what makes catastrophe loss correlated: one event touches many locations at once, so the claims arrive together. Spatial correlation within a footprint is one of the harder parts of a real model and is usually simplified heavily in teaching versions.

See also: stochastic catalogue · hazard module

Hazard module

The first cat model component: takes a coordinate and returns the distribution of physical intensities possible there.

Built on a stochastic event catalogue. For each event it reads the intensity at the location — gust speed for windstorm, peak ground acceleration for earthquake, inundation depth for flood — from a hazard grid. It knows nothing about what is standing at the coordinate, which is why a high-hazard location is not automatically a high-loss location.

See also: stochastic catalogue · footprint · intensity metric · cat model

Intensity metric

The physical quantity the hazard module reports, chosen per peril because it is what drives damage.

Gust speed for windstorm, peak ground acceleration and spectral acceleration for earthquake, inundation depth for flood. The choice of metric decides which exposure fields matter: soil type can amplify earthquake intensity several-fold, while for flood it is ground-floor elevation that counts.

See also: hazard module · damage function

Non-modelled items

Perils, geographies or coverage types a model does not attempt to represent — and reports as zero rather than as unknown.

Categorically different from uncertainty within the model. A zero from a cat model is not a statement that the exposure is zero; it is a statement that the question was not asked. The only defence is a documented list of what the model was not asked, and an explicit load for it.

See also: aleatory · epistemic · view of risk

OEP

Occurrence exceedance probability — the EP curve built from the single largest event in each simulated year.

Answers "how bad can one event be?" and is the curve a per-occurrence catastrophe excess-of-loss treaty responds to. Where a year contains several events, OEP records only the largest; the difference between it and AEP is accumulation risk.

See also: aep · ep curve · return period

Probable maximum loss (PML)

The largest loss a portfolio is likely to sustain from a single catastrophe event. A risk-management concept rather than a single defined statistic.

May be defined deterministically, from a named scenario, or probabilistically, as a point on the OEP curve — and the two are not interchangeable. Maximum foreseeable loss is the more extreme cousin: the worst outcome still considered physically plausible. A PML quoted without saying which definition produced it cannot be used.

See also: oep · rds · aal

Protection gap

The difference between total economic loss from a catastrophe and the insured loss the industry pays.

Driven by absence of cover, scope exclusions, or no viable insurance market at all. It is the reason a headline economic loss figure and an insured loss figure for the same event can differ several-fold, and the context in which parametric and sovereign risk products are usually proposed.

See also: non modelled items · cat model

Realistic Disaster Scenario (RDS)

A suite of specific catastrophe scenarios defined by Lloyd’s that every syndicate must estimate its exposure to.

The January 2026 compulsory set covers two consecutive Atlantic seaboard windstorms, Florida, Gulf of Mexico, European and Japanese windstorm, California, New Madrid and Japanese earthquake, UK flood, terrorism and cyber, plus two syndicate-defined events. Because every syndicate estimates the same named event, Lloyd’s can add the answers together — which is something an EP curve can never be used for.

See also: cmr · pml · oep

Schedule of values (SOV)

The structured list of insured locations submitted with a risk — one row per location, with address, value and building attributes.

The cat model’s primary input, and the main determinant of whether its output means anything. A model does not correct bad exposure data; it amplifies it. Where a required field is blank the model substitutes a default, designed to be representative rather than conservative, so a gap moves the answer in an unknown direction rather than a safe one.

See also: cope · tiv · geocoding · cat model

Secondary uncertainty

The spread of possible damage once the event intensity is already known.

Primary uncertainty is which events happen and how intense they are; secondary uncertainty is how much damage results given that intensity. It is a property of the physical world — construction quality, maintenance, exact exposure to the wind — not a defect in the model, and it matters most for the reinsurance layers that sit above an attachment point.

See also: damage ratio · aleatory · vulnerability module

Stochastic event catalogue

A synthetic database of simulated events, each with an assigned annual rate. The core of the hazard module.

Synthetic, not historical: it is constructed by simulation and deliberately contains events more severe than anything in the observed record — which is the whole point, since Hurricane Andrew had not happened before it happened. Each event carries an annual rate, and the return period you hear quoted is simply 1 ÷ that rate.

See also: hazard module · footprint · return period

Total insured value (TIV)

The value at risk at a single location, on a replacement-cost basis. The multiplier in the loss calculation.

Damage ratio × TIV = ground-up loss, so a proportional error in TIV produces a proportional error in every loss figure downstream. Replacement cost is the correct basis — what it costs to rebuild to current standards at current prices — which is neither market value nor depreciated book value.

See also: sov · damage ratio · ground up loss

View of risk

The professional position on a risk: model output plus documented adjustment for what the model does not cover.

The model produces a number; a person produces the view and signs it. Adjusting away from model output with a recorded reason is ordinary practice; adjusting without one is indefensible afterwards. Sign-off certifies that the run was correctly configured and its limitations recorded — never that the number is right.

See also: non modelled items · cat model

Vulnerability module

The cat model component that converts a hazard intensity and a building description into a damage ratio.

Sits between hazard and the financial module, and is where the building finally matters. It applies the damage function selected by the COPE fields. In a commercial model the damage functions are fitted to claims data and engineering studies and are the most closely guarded part of the product.

See also: damage function · damage ratio · cope · cat model

Economics of insurance 8

Base cost of capital

The market return shareholders could earn investing the same money themselves — a benchmark, not a source of value.

The base cost of capital is what a comparable leveraged fund would return; shareholders can replicate it directly, so it creates no value on its own. Value comes from beating it via underwriting and from managing frictional costs.

See also: frictional capital · cost of equity · economic value

Cost of equity

The return equity investors require to hold the stock — the hurdle rate ROE is judged against.

Derived via CAPM as risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium, the cost of equity is the minimum return shareholders demand for bearing insurance risk. ROE below it destroys value; above it creates value.

See also: roe · capm · pb multiple · base cost of capital

Economic net worth

Market value of assets less the economic value of liabilities — the tangible worth to shareholders.

Economic net worth is the market-consistent equity: what would be left for shareholders after settling liabilities at their economic value. Insurers usually trade above it, the excess being franchise value.

See also: economic value · franchise value · pb multiple

Economic value

Value on a market-consistent basis: assets at market value, liabilities at the replication value plus frictional costs.

Economic value strips out accounting smoothing to show the true value created. A contract creates economic value when its premium more than covers the replication value of its cash flows plus the frictional cost of the capital it consumes.

See also: replicating portfolio · frictional capital · economic net worth · franchise value

Franchise value

The premium over economic net worth — the present value of profits from future business.

Franchise value is the market's expectation that the insurer will keep writing profitable business. It is why insurers trade above economic net worth and why protecting reputation and capital adequacy matters.

See also: economic net worth · pb multiple · economic value

Frictional capital cost

The insurer-specific cost of holding risk capital: double taxation, financial distress, agency and regulatory costs.

Frictional costs are why holding capital inside an insurer is expensive beyond the plain market return. They are the true, distinctive cost of taking insurance risk and the real benchmark for whether underwriting creates value.

See also: base cost of capital · cost of equity · economic value · replicating portfolio

Price-to-book (P/B) multiple

Market capitalisation divided by book value; reflects whether the business earns above or below its cost of equity.

An insurer trades at 1.0× book when ROE equals the cost of equity, above book when it creates value and below when it destroys it. The multiple compresses as expected ROE falls, amplifying equity losses.

See also: roe · cost of equity · franchise value · economic net worth

Replicating portfolio

The traded portfolio that best matches a liability's cash flows; its market value prices the liability by no-arbitrage.

The replicating (or hedge) portfolio values non-traded insurance cash flows by reference to traded instruments, ensuring the price reflects the market view. The economic value of a liability is its replication value plus frictional capital cost, less the default and liquidity adjustments.

See also: economic value · frictional capital · base cost of capital

Frequency & severity 7

Aggregate loss

Total loss S over a period; in the collective risk model S = ΣX with random frequency N, so E[S] = E[N]·E[X].

The aggregate (or collective-risk) loss combines a random number of claims with random sizes. Its mean is frequency times severity; its variance and tail drive reserving, reinsurance and capital.

See also: frequency · severity · stop loss · monte carlo

Frequency

The number of claims N a risk generates in a period; modelled with Poisson or negative binomial.

Frequency is how often claims occur, and it scales with exposure. It is one half of the loss model — expected loss is frequency times severity — and is usually fitted with a Poisson GLM carrying exposure as an offset.

See also: severity · aggregate loss · poisson · glm · exposure

Gamma distribution

A positive, moderately skewed distribution used for claim severity, including on the severity side of a GLM.

The Gamma is a flexible positive distribution well suited to moderate-tailed severity. With a log link it gives a multiplicative severity model — the natural partner to a Poisson frequency GLM.

See also: severity · glm · lognormal

Lognormal distribution

A heavy-tailed severity/aggregate distribution; its stop-loss transform has a clean closed form.

The lognormal (the exponential of a normal) captures multiplicative loss processes and heavier tails. Its expected loss above a retention, E[(L−d)⁺], has a closed form used across raters, simulators and stop-loss pricing.

See also: severity · stop loss · aggregate loss · monte carlo

Poisson distribution

The canonical count distribution for claim frequency, with equal mean and variance.

The Poisson models the number of independent events in a period. It underpins frequency GLMs; when claims are over-dispersed (variance > mean) the negative binomial is used instead.

See also: frequency · glm · aggregate loss

Severity

The size X of an individual claim; modelled with skewed, heavy-tailed distributions (Gamma, lognormal, Pareto).

Severity is how big a claim is, given one occurs. It is right-skewed and heavy-tailed, so it needs positive skewed distributions and careful tail modelling — the domain of exposure curves for large losses.

See also: frequency · aggregate loss · lognormal · gamma · exposure curve

Stop-loss

The expected loss above a retention, E[(L−d)⁺] — the building block of layer and aggregate pricing.

The stop-loss transform gives the expected cost of losses exceeding a deductible or attachment. A layer's net cost is the stop-loss at its bottom minus the stop-loss at its top — the core of non-proportional pricing.

See also: excess of loss · lognormal · aggregate loss · exposure curve

History 3

Bottomry

Ancient marine loans where extra interest priced the risk of loss — the earliest explicit price of risk.

In a bottomry loan a lender advanced money on a ship or cargo; if it was lost the loan was cancelled, if it arrived the borrower repaid principal plus elevated interest. That extra interest is the first price of risk, later unbundled into a standalone premium.

See also: ruin theory · law of large numbers

Law of large numbers

Bernoulli's result (1713) that pooling enough independent risks makes the average outcome predictable.

The law of large numbers is the mathematical justification for insurance: individual losses are unpredictable, but the average over a large, diversified pool is stable — which is what lets an insurer price and reserve at all.

See also: frequency · ruin theory · aggregate loss

Ruin theory

Lundberg's 1903 idea that premiums need a safety loading so the probability of exhausting capital stays acceptably small.

Ruin theory models total claims as a random process over time and asks the chance that cumulative losses exhaust premiums plus capital. It first tied the price of risk to the capital behind it — the ancestor of the SCR's 1-in-200 standard.

See also: risk margin · scr · var · law of large numbers

IFRS 17 & financial reporting 23

Annual cohort

The rule that no group of contracts may span issue dates more than 12 months apart, keeping profitability trends visible over time.

After splitting a portfolio into profitability buckets, each group is further divided so it contains no contracts issued more than a year apart. The UK applies this in full (no EU carve-out). For annual GI it is rarely painful; it creates work for multi-year binders, tacit-renewal schemes and long treaties.

See also: paa · ifrs 17

Contract boundary

The line separating cash flows inside a recognised contract from those belonging to a future contract — set where you can reprice to fully reflect risk.

Cash flows are within the boundary while the insurer can compel premiums or has a substantive obligation to provide cover; the obligation ends when the insurer can reprice to fully reflect the individual's or the portfolio's risk. It keeps unincepted renewals off the balance sheet and differs (often shorter) from the Solvency UK boundary — a recurring reconciling item.

See also: fulfilment cash flows · technical provisions

Contractual Service Margin (CSM)

The store of unearned profit on a group of contracts, released to revenue as coverage is provided; it cannot be negative.

The CSM is set at inception to cancel any expected profit so no gain is booked on day one. It accretes interest at the locked-in rate, is unlocked for changes in future-service estimates (favourable up, adverse down), and releases to P&L over coverage units. If a group is loss-making there is no negative CSM — the loss is recognised immediately as a loss component.

See also: fulfilment cash flows · coverage units · loss component · gmm

Coverage units

The measure of service provided each period (duration × size of cover) that drives how the CSM releases to revenue.

The CSM released in a period equals the remaining CSM times this period's coverage units divided by all remaining coverage units. Coverage units reflect the quantity of insurance-contract service — the expected duration and level of cover — so the profit emerges in step with the service actually delivered.

See also: csm · insurance revenue

Discounting of insurance liabilities

Holding claims reserves at present value rather than at the undiscounted amount — the single largest measurement change IFRS 17 brought to general insurance.

Worth $195.6m of Hiscox’s $230.9m profit uplift when 2022 was restated. It is a timing benefit, not a profit: what discounting releases on the claims line, the unwind takes back through insurance finance expense over the following years. Term matters more than rate — three years at 4.5% is worth about 12.4% of the reserve, seven years at the same rate about 26.5% — which is why casualty writers were transformed by IFRS 17 and property writers barely noticed.

See also: lic · ifie · risk adjustment · combined ratio

FRS 103 (UK GAAP insurance)

The UK GAAP standard for insurance contracts: statutory reserves, GI usually undiscounted, no explicit risk adjustment or CSM.

FRS 103 (with FRS 102) governs UK statutory insurance accounts. GI claims reserves are typically undiscounted with prudence implicit rather than an explicit margin, an unearned premium reserve for remaining coverage, and an unexpired-risk reserve where the UPR is insufficient. It is the third of the three reserve bases a UK carrier reconciles.

See also: technical provisions · ibnr · ifrs 17

Fulfilment cash flows (FCF)

The current, explicit cost of meeting a contract: probability-weighted future cash flows + discounting + a risk adjustment.

The FCF is the honest present value of fulfilling the contract — an unbiased, probability-weighted, current estimate of the cash flows within the contract boundary, brought to present value by discounting, plus a risk adjustment for non-financial risk. It is the base on which the CSM (or a loss component) sits.

See also: csm · risk adjustment · contract boundary · discount rate

General Measurement Model (GMM / BBA)

The default IFRS 17 model: liability = fulfilment cash flows + CSM, with the CSM unlocked for future service and released over coverage units.

The GMM (or 'building block approach') applies to all contracts unless PAA or VFA is used. It tracks the full current-value liability plus a CSM, and is the model for long-tail, long-duration or highly variable non-participating business — the credit/surety and casualty end of a GI book.

See also: csm · paa · vfa · fulfilment cash flows

IFRS 17

The accounting standard for insurance contracts (effective 1 Jan 2023) that measures every contract at current value and defers unearned profit into the CSM.

IFRS 17 replaced IFRS 4 for annual periods beginning on/after 1 January 2023. It imposes a single, current-value measurement model, splits the underwriting result from the financial result, and — crucially — removes gross written premium as the P&L top line. The UK adopted it as issued (no annual-cohort carve-out). Its live Post-Implementation Review is under way.

See also: csm · fulfilment cash flows · paa · gmm · solvency ii

Insurance finance income/expenses (IFIE)

The effect of the time value of money and financial risk on insurance liabilities — the discount unwind plus rate-change effects.

IFIE sits below the insurance service result and captures the unwind of discounting and the effect of changes in discount rates. It can be presented wholly in P&L or, under the OCI option, split so a systematic charge stays in P&L and the discount-rate movement goes to OCI.

See also: oci option · insurance service result · discount rate

Insurance revenue

The IFRS 17 top line — earned coverage recognised as service is provided, excluding investment components. It replaces gross written premium.

Insurance revenue reflects the consideration expected for services provided in the period, recognised as coverage is delivered rather than when premium is received; deposit-like investment components are excluded. Its arrival — and the disappearance of gross written premium from the face of the P&L — rewires every legacy KPI.

See also: insurance service result · coverage units · combined ratio

Insurance service result

Insurance revenue less insurance service expenses — the IFRS 17 underwriting result, and the true measure to reconcile any combined ratio to.

The insurance service result is the underwriting profit under IFRS 17: revenue for coverage provided, less incurred claims, acquisition amortisation and loss-component moves. Because it is discounted and risk-adjusted and excludes investment components, it does not map cleanly to the classic combined ratio — which must be defined and reconciled to it.

See also: insurance revenue · ifie · combined ratio

Liability for incurred claims (LIC)

The liability for claims already incurred — always full fulfilment cash flows: discounted best estimate + risk adjustment.

Even under PAA the LIC is measured on the full basis: the discounted present value of claims to settle (case + IBNR + ULAE) plus a risk adjustment. Discounting can be omitted only where claims are expected to be paid within a year. For long-tail lines the LIC is where IFRS 17's discounting, risk adjustment and finance effects bite.

See also: lrc · ibnr · risk adjustment · chain ladder

Liability for remaining coverage (LRC)

The liability for cover not yet provided — under PAA, essentially the unearned premium reserve.

The LRC represents the obligation to provide future coverage. Under PAA it is premium received less amounts earned as revenue less acquisition cash flows, discounted only where there is a significant financing component. Under the GMM it is fulfilment cash flows for future service plus the CSM.

See also: lic · paa · csm · loss component

Loss component

The tracked balance for an onerous group's expected loss, recognised in P&L immediately and kept out of future insurance revenue.

When a group is expected to lose money there is no CSM to absorb it: the loss hits P&L at once and a loss component of the LRC is set up. It is systematically remeasured, and later favourable future-service changes reverse the loss component first before any positive CSM can rebuild. Under PAA the onerous test is run only when facts and circumstances indicate.

See also: csm · lrc · paa

Loss-recovery component

The up-front gain recognised on reinsurance held when the underlying contracts are onerous — the one place the deferral asymmetry is restored.

Added by the June 2020 amendments: when a loss is recognised on onerous underlying contracts and reinsurance was held at or before recognition, the insurer recognises income on the reinsurance equal to the expected recovery percentage times the underlying loss, and sets up a loss-recovery component of the reinsurance asset. It offsets the underlying loss immediately rather than deferring the reinsurance gain.

See also: reinsurance held · loss component

OCI option

The accounting-policy choice to route discount-rate movements on liabilities through OCI rather than P&L, damping earnings volatility.

Electing the OCI option puts a systematic finance charge in P&L and the discount-rate effect in OCI. Insurers choose it to match FVOCI backing assets, so asset and liability mark-to-market noise both sit in OCI and P&L shows a stable finance charge. The cost is tracking the locked-in rate and maintaining an OCI shadow.

See also: ifie · insurance service result

Onerous contract

A group of contracts expected to lose money over its coverage period. IFRS 17 requires the loss to be recognised immediately as a loss component.

The idea is the old unexpired risk reserve; the trigger is far more sensitive. IFRS 4 tested the whole portfolio, so an AURR was rare. IFRS 17 tests each annual cohort within a portfolio, so a single bad year on a single line books a loss up front while the rest of the book stays profitable — profitable business can no longer subsidise loss-making business within the same test. In a softening market this is the line that bites first, and a recognised loss component is real news a reader would not have got under IFRS 4.

See also: loss component · lrc · combined ratio

Premium Allocation Approach (PAA)

The GI-friendly simplification where the LRC behaves like the unearned premium reserve; the LIC is still full fulfilment cash flows.

PAA is available when each contract's coverage is one year or less, or when PAA is a reasonable approximation to the GMM for the LRC (para 53). Most annual London-Market business qualifies automatically. It simplifies only the unearned side; the liability for incurred claims is still measured as discounted, risk-adjusted fulfilment cash flows.

See also: gmm · lrc · lic · annual cohort

Reinsurance contracts held

Reinsurance you buy, measured separately from the underlying, with its own CSM for the net cost of cover and no onerous concept.

Reinsurance held is measured on an adapted GMM (or PAA), with a CSM representing the net cost (usually) or gain of buying cover, released as cover is received. The reinsurer's non-performance risk sits in the cash flows (changes to P&L), there is no onerous test, and it is presented net on the P&L and not offset against the underlying.

See also: loss recovery component · reinsurance · quota share

Risk adjustment for non-financial risk

The entity-specific compensation an insurer requires for bearing non-financial (insurance/lapse/expense) uncertainty; its confidence level must be disclosed.

Distinct from the Solvency UK risk margin, the IFRS 17 risk adjustment is entity-specific and technique-agnostic (confidence level, or cost-of-capital with the level backed out) and covers only non-financial risk. Para 119 requires disclosing the confidence level it corresponds to. It is not equal to the prescribed Solvency risk margin.

See also: fulfilment cash flows · risk margin · scr

Technical provisions (Solvency UK)

The Solvency UK value of insurance liabilities: best estimate + a prescribed cost-of-capital risk margin, with no CSM.

Technical provisions are the regulatory liability = best-estimate cash flows (on PRA curves and regulatory contract boundaries) plus a risk margin computed on a cost-of-capital basis (CoC cut from 6% to 4% at end-2023). Unlike IFRS 17 there is no CSM — expected profit sits in own funds at inception — so Solvency own funds will not tie to IFRS 17 equity.

See also: risk margin · scr · solvency ii · contract boundary

Variable Fee Approach (VFA)

A GMM variant for direct-participating contracts, where the CSM absorbs the entity's share of underlying-item and financial changes.

The VFA applies to direct-participating contracts meeting the three B101 criteria (a share of a clearly identified pool, a substantial share of fair-value returns, and payments varying substantially with those returns). Typical of with-profits / unit-linked savings — not GI. Reinsurance held or issued can never be VFA.

See also: gmm · csm

Methods & data 9

Bühlmann–Straub credibility

A greatest-accuracy credibility method that weights exposures of unequal size — ideal for blending a prior with emerging data.

Bühlmann–Straub derives the credibility weight from the ratio of within-risk to between-risk variance, handling exposures of different sizes. It is the rigorous way to move a new line's price from market prior toward its own experience.

See also: credibility · experience rating · parameter risk

CAPM

Capital Asset Pricing Model: only systematic (non-diversifiable) risk earns a return; price = risk-free + beta × equity risk premium.

Sharpe's 1964 model frames the fair return on capital. For insurers it sets the cost of equity, but it captures only systematic risk — the frictional costs that dominate insurance value are invisible to it.

See also: cost of equity · frictional capital · base cost of capital

Credibility

How much weight a risk's own experience gets vs the book/market: Rate = Z·own + (1−Z)·prior.

Credibility blends a risk's own (thin) experience with a broader prior, the weight Z rising with data volume. Classical (√-rule) and Bühlmann–Straub forms let a new product start from a market prior and shift onto its own data over time.

See also: buhlmann straub · exposure rating · experience rating · parameter risk

Experience rating

Pricing from the risk's own historical claims (burning cost) — useless with no data, powerful once a triangle exists.

Experience rating (burning cost) projects a risk's own past losses to the future. It needs credible own data, so a new product leans on exposure rating first and shifts onto experience as credibility builds.

See also: exposure rating · credibility · buhlmann straub

Exposure curve

Industry-derived curves (Swiss Re / MBBEFD, Riebesell) that encode how loss is distributed by size — external severity data.

Exposure curves describe the share of loss falling in each layer, calibrated by reinsurers across many cedants. They let you price a layer or an increased limit with no own claims — importing the reinsurer's data into your rate.

See also: exposure rating · severity · excess of loss · stop loss

Exposure rating

Pricing from the current risk's structure and external severity curves rather than its own past claims.

Exposure rating asks what a risk should cost given its make-up, using industry severity structure (exposure curves, increased-limit factors). It is the method of choice when a book's own experience is thin or absent.

See also: experience rating · exposure curve · credibility · parameter risk

Monte-Carlo simulation

Drawing many random futures and reading the distribution of outcomes off the sample.

When closed forms run out — cycles, catastrophes, path-dependence — simulation draws thousands of scenarios and summarises them as percentile fans. It underpins capital models and stochastic projection.

See also: aggregate loss · return period · underwriting cycle · nested stochastic

Nested stochastic projection

A real-world outer simulation where each path needs a risk-neutral inner valuation — very compute-heavy.

Nested (stochastic-on-stochastic) projection values path-dependent guarantees and capital by running an inner market-consistent valuation inside each outer real-world scenario. Its cost is combinatorial, so architecture — not approximation — makes it runnable.

See also: monte carlo · scr · solvency ii

Parameter (estimation) risk

The risk that your estimated mean is simply wrong — dominant when you have little or no data.

With thin data, estimation error dwarfs the random process error, so the true loss cost may differ materially from your best estimate. This is why a new line must carry a larger risk margin and more conservative capital.

See also: risk margin · credibility · exposure rating · scr

Products & structures 6

Binding authority (binder)

The delegated agreement letting an MGA bind cover on a carrier's paper within set rules and limits.

A binder sets the classes, limits, wordings and referral thresholds an MGA may write for a carrier. Breaching it is a serious conduct and capacity risk, so modern MGAs enforce the rules in the rating system itself.

See also: mga · bordereaux · fair value

Bordereaux

The periodic data returns (premiums, risks, claims) an MGA sends its carrier under the binder.

Bordereaux are the exposure and claims records that let a carrier monitor a delegated book and that build the MGA's own loss-and-pricing data asset over time.

See also: mga · binder · reserving

Deposit replacement

A tenant pays a fee instead of a cash deposit; the guarantee pays the landlord for damage/arrears and pursues the tenant.

Deposit replacement (Zero Deposit, Reposit, flatfair) behaves like surety/credit: it finances the landlord and recovers from the tenant rather than indemnifying the tenant. Its loss is tenant-default risk, correlated with the economic cycle.

See also: surety · mga · fair value

Legal indemnity / title insurance

Single-premium, perpetuity cover for a pre-identified title or conveyancing defect so a property sale can complete.

Legal indemnity insures the uncertain financial consequence of a known defect — a restrictive covenant, missing easement or absent consent — being enforced. It is low-frequency, high-severity, and rated on the limit of indemnity times a risk-category factor.

See also: mga · binder · excess of loss · exposure rating

Managing general agent (MGA)

An intermediary with delegated authority to underwrite and bind on an insurer's paper.

An MGA underwrites, prices and issues policies on behalf of a carrier under a binding authority, earning commission and often a profit commission. It is capital-light — the carrier holds the risk — which makes distribution and loss-ratio discipline the value drivers.

See also: binder · bordereaux · quota share

Surety / guarantee

A three-party promise to pay a beneficiary if the principal defaults, with a right to recover from the principal.

Surety differs from indemnity insurance: the surety pays the beneficiary then subrogates against the defaulting principal, so the principal is financed, not protected. Deposit-replacement and guarantor-replacement products are surety-like in substance.

See also: deposit replacement · reinsurance

Rating & premium 10

Deductible / excess

The amount of a loss the policyholder retains before cover responds.

A deductible removes small, high-frequency claims, lowering the pure premium and discouraging minor claims. It is one of the main severity/frequency levers in product design.

See also: policy limit · stop loss · severity

Exposure

The measure of how much risk is present (vehicle-years, £m revenue, sum insured) that premium scales on.

Exposure is the unit the price is built on. Frequency scales with it (carried as an offset in a GLM), and rate-on-value products multiply an exposure base by a rate and factors.

See also: frequency · glm · relativity · exposure rating

Generalised linear model (GLM)

The workhorse rating model: a log link makes the linear predictor additive, so the rate is multiplicative (base × relativities).

GLMs fit frequency (Poisson) and severity (Gamma) with a log link, turning a sum of coefficients into a product of relativities. This is exactly the multiplicative tariff of risk classification.

See also: relativity · frequency · severity · exposure · credibility

Grossing-up

Turning cost into price by dividing by (1 − variable% − profit%), because variable loads are charged on the final premium.

Commission, tax and profit are percentages of the final premium, not the cost, so you can't just add them — you divide the cost by (1 − their sum). This 'grossing-up' converts a technical cost into an office premium.

See also: technical premium · expense ratio · rate adequacy

Policy limit

The maximum the insurer will pay; caps severity and drives increased-limit pricing.

The policy limit (or limit of indemnity) caps what the insurer pays on a claim. Pricing higher limits uses increased-limit factors or exposure curves, since each extra layer of cover adds less expected loss.

See also: deductible · exposure curve · severity · legal indemnity

Pure premium

The expected cost of claims — E[Loss] — the first rung of the technical-price ladder.

The pure (or risk) premium is the raw expected loss before any loadings, expenses, capital cost or profit. Everything else in the price is built on top of it via the technical-price ladder.

See also: technical premium · frequency · severity · grossing up

Rate adequacy

Charged premium ÷ technical premium — how close the price is to the risk-adequate level.

Rate adequacy below 1.0 means the underwriter is charging under the technically indicated price — often a symptom of a soft market. It is the discipline metric of the underwriting cycle.

See also: technical premium · underwriting cycle · combined ratio

Relativity

A multiplicative rating factor exp(β); the base level is fixed at 1.0.

Relativities express how much each risk-factor level multiplies the base rate — e.g. a region factor of 1.5 means 50% more expected loss than the base region. They are the output of a GLM and the levers of a rater.

See also: glm · exposure · pure premium

Technical premium

The risk-adequate cost: pure premium plus loss loadings, expenses and the cost of capital.

The technical premium is what the risk 'should' cost to be adequate — pure premium plus catastrophe/trend/IBNR loadings, fixed expenses and a capital/profit load. The charged price is measured against it as rate adequacy.

See also: pure premium · grossing up · rate adequacy · combined ratio

Underwriting cycle

The multi-year oscillation of rates and combined ratios driven by capital flows and competition.

When capital is plentiful, competition softens rates (soft market); after big losses capital withdraws and rates harden. The cycle drives rate adequacy over time and is central to multi-year projection.

See also: rate adequacy · combined ratio · monte carlo

Ratios & KPIs 7

Combined ratio

Loss ratio + expense ratio. Below 100% is an underwriting profit; above 100% an underwriting loss.

The combined ratio is the headline measure of underwriting profitability: the total cost of claims and expenses per unit of premium. A combined ratio of 95% means the book keeps 5p of every premium pound before investment income.

See also: loss ratio · expense ratio · roe

EBITDA

Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation — the cross-sector comparability metric. IFRS 17 shares its goal and reverses its method.

EBITDA and IFRS 17 answer the same complaint — that one firm's reported profit is not the same kind of thing as the next firm's — by opposite means. EBITDA buys comparability by REMOVING the items that differ for non-operating reasons; IFRS 17 buys it by MEASURING them, and accepts the volatility that follows: $137.8m of Hiscox's 2022 restatement uplift is nothing but the yield curve moving, exactly the kind of item EBITDA exists to exclude. The closest IFRS 17 analogue to a transferable economic result is not a profit line at all but the CSM, a stock of unearned profit on business already written — though most short-tail general insurance sits on the premium allocation approach and presents no explicit CSM. In insurance the part of the market that genuinely trades on an EBITDA multiple is the MGA sector, which holds no reserves and no capital.

See also: csm · combined ratio · discounting · insurance service result · paa

Expense ratio

Operating expenses (acquisition + management) as a percentage of premium.

The expense ratio captures the cost of acquiring and administering business — commission, underwriting, admin and levies — expressed as a share of premium. Added to the loss ratio it gives the combined ratio.

See also: combined ratio · loss ratio · grossing up

Gross written premium (GWP)

Premium contracted in the period, before reinsurance and before earning. No longer an IFRS 17 measure — it survives only as an alternative performance measure.

GWP was the top line of every insurance P&L until IFRS 17 removed it from the face of the accounts. Every listed insurer still publishes it as an APM because the market runs on it: Hiscox call theirs insurance contract written premium (ICWP), Beazley call theirs insurance written premiums. Volume is still volume — it simply is not revenue. Written premium and insurance revenue sit close together on a steady annual book and diverge sharply at half-year, when much of what has been written is still unearned.

See also: icwp · insurance revenue · upr · combined ratio

Insurance contract written premium (ICWP)

The alternative performance measure insurers publish in place of gross written premium under IFRS 17.

Not an IFRS 17 concept — a disclosed APM that keeps the volume measure the market wants. Hiscox use ICWP ($4,979.0m at FY2025 against insurance revenue of $4,883.7m); Beazley use "insurance written premiums" ($6,100.7m against revenue of $6,064.8m). Definitions differ slightly between insurers, and neither reconciles exactly to insurance revenue, so never treat the two as interchangeable.

See also: gwp · insurance revenue · combined ratio

Loss ratio

Incurred claims as a percentage of earned premium — the core measure of underwriting performance.

The loss ratio divides the claims an insurer expects to pay (or has paid) by the premium it earned for the same period. It is the single biggest driver of profitability: a small change in the loss-ratio assumption cascades through reserves, capital and equity value.

See also: combined ratio · expense ratio · pure premium · ibnr

Return on equity (ROE)

Net profit as a percentage of shareholders' equity — the primary shareholder-value metric in insurance.

ROE measures the return earned on the capital shareholders have put at risk. It is benchmarked against the cost of equity: when ROE exceeds the cost of equity the business creates value and can trade above book.

See also: cost of equity · pb multiple · scr · combined ratio

Regulation & governance 3

Fair value (Consumer Duty / PROD)

The FCA requirement that price is reasonable relative to benefits — tested via claims ratio vs commission, especially for add-ons.

Under PROD 4 and Consumer Duty a manufacturer must assess and monitor fair value before and after launch. The GAP-insurance case (≈6% of premium paid in claims, ~70% commission) is the benchmark failure a data-poor add-on must avoid.

See also: mga · binder · deposit replacement · target market

Solvency II

The EU risk-based capital regime (in force 2016); market-consistent balance sheet, SCR at 99.5% VaR, explicit risk margin.

Solvency II institutionalised the 'cost of capital' view of insurance: assets and liabilities valued market-consistently, capital held against a quantified downside, and a risk margin computed with a cost-of-capital charge.

See also: scr · risk margin · var · parameter risk

Target market

The defined group a product is designed for — a required part of product governance under PROD.

Identifying the target market (and the customers the product is not right for) is central to PROD 4 product oversight. It shapes the distribution strategy and the fair-value assessment.

See also: fair value · mga

Reinsurance & capital 15

Catastrophe bond

Capital-markets risk transfer: investors take catastrophe risk for a spread; the multiple is spread ÷ expected loss.

A cat bond passes tail risk to capital-market investors instead of a reinsurer. Its economics are summarised by the spread (premium ÷ principal) and the multiple (premium ÷ expected loss).

See also: reinsurance · excess of loss · return period

Excess-of-loss (XoL)

Non-proportional reinsurance covering the slice of loss between an attachment and a limit — caps large losses.

XoL responds only above a retention, capping single large or catastrophe losses. Priced via rate-on-line and the loss to the layer, it protects the volatile tail that concentrates in specialty and commercial books.

See also: reinsurance · rate on line · exposure curve · cat bond

Losses-occurring basis

A treaty covering losses that happen during the treaty year, whenever the underlying policy was written — so the coverage period is the treaty term.

The simpler of the two attachment bases to measure. Because cover responds to the date of the loss rather than the date the underlying policy incepted, the reinsurer's coverage period equals the treaty period — so a twelve-month losses-occurring treaty clears the ≤1-year PAA limb automatically, where its risks-attaching twin does not.

See also: risks attaching · reinsurance issued · paa

Payback period

The reciprocal of rate-on-line: how many years of premium equal the layer limit.

Payback tells you how long a reinsurance layer would take to 'pay for itself' in premium terms. Short paybacks signal expensive, loss-exposed layers; long paybacks signal remote ones.

See also: rate on line · excess of loss

Quota share

Proportional reinsurance that cedes a fixed percentage of every risk — sharing premium, losses and capital from the ground up.

Under a quota share the reinsurer takes a set share of all premiums and claims. It smooths net earnings and relieves solvency capital proportionally, which is why it is the tool of choice for scaling a new or correlated book.

See also: reinsurance · excess of loss · scr · risk margin

Rate-on-line

Layer premium ÷ limit; its reciprocal is the payback period.

Rate-on-line expresses a reinsurance layer's price as a fraction of the limit it buys. A 10% rate-on-line implies a 10-year payback if the layer is never breached — a quick read on how a layer is priced.

See also: excess of loss · payback · reinsurance

Reinstatement premium

Paid to restore a limit after a loss. Because it is triggered by the claim, it nets against incurred claims rather than being counted as revenue.

IFRS 17 sorts cash flows by what triggers them, not by what they are called. A mandatory reinstatement premium is contingent on the claim, so it reduces insurance service expenses; an adjustment premium that moves with the cedant's subject premium is exposure-driven and is revenue. The presentation matters to any ratio reader: on a cat writer in a heavy loss year, reinstatements suppress the claims line rather than inflating the premium line, and on a plausible set of numbers the combined ratio comes out about 2.6 points better than the naive presentation with no difference in the cash.

See also: reinsurance issued · combined ratio · insurance revenue

Reinsurance

Insurance for insurers: transferring part of the risk (and capital need) to another carrier.

Reinsurance reshapes the tail and relieves capital. Proportional forms (quota share) cede a fixed share; non-proportional forms (excess-of-loss) cede a slice above an attachment. For a data-poor line it is also price discovery.

See also: quota share · excess of loss · cat bond · scr · exposure curve

Reinsurance contracts issued

Reinsurance written by the reinsurer. Measured as ordinary insurance issued — the IFRS 17 asymmetries belong to contracts held, not issued.

A reinsurance contract issued is grouped, measured, discounted, risk-adjusted, CSM'd and onerous-tested exactly like direct business, and presented inside insurance revenue and insurance service expenses — never in the reinsurance-held block below the service result. Netting inwards business into that block is the most common presentation error. The genuine difficulties are elsewhere: coverage periods that outrun the treaty term on risks-attaching business, premiums that stay estimates for years, and cash flows contingent on claims that are not revenue.

See also: risks attaching · reinstatement premium · paa · onerous · reinsurance held

Return period

The average interval between losses of a given size — a 1-in-200 loss is the 99.5% quantile.

Return period is the inverse of an annual exceedance probability: a 1-in-200-year loss has a 0.5% chance each year. It is the language of catastrophe and capital modelling and maps directly to VaR quantiles.

See also: var · scr · cat bond · monte carlo

Risk margin

The loading over the best estimate for bearing uncertainty; larger when data is scarce.

The risk margin (or safety loading) compensates for the uncertainty around the expected loss. Under Solvency II it is a cost-of-capital charge on future non-hedgeable capital; for a data-poor line it should be deliberately generous.

See also: parameter risk · scr · solvency ii · ruin theory

Risks-attaching basis

A treaty covering the underlying policies that incept during the treaty year — so the reinsurer's coverage period runs beyond the treaty term.

A policy incepting on the last day of a twelve-month risks-attaching treaty runs a further twelve months of its own, so the reinsurer is on risk for up to twenty-four months. The consequence under IFRS 17 is that the ≤1-year limb of the PAA eligibility test is unavailable, and PAA can only be used by demonstrating it does not differ materially from the general model — even though the underlying direct policies were automatically eligible. Eligibility does not travel up the chain. Contrast losses-occurring, where cover attaches to events during the treaty year and the coverage period is the treaty term.

See also: losses occurring · reinsurance issued · paa

Solvency Capital Requirement (SCR)

The capital an insurer must hold to survive a 1-in-200-year loss (99.5% VaR over one year) under Solvency II.

The SCR is the regulatory capital calibrated to a 99.5% one-year Value-at-Risk — a direct descendant of ruin theory. Coverage (capital ÷ SCR) is watched by regulators and rating agencies; a new line attracts the standard-formula factors with no credit for optimism.

See also: solvency ii · var · risk margin · ruin theory

Tail-VaR (TVaR)

The expected loss beyond VaR — a coherent risk measure that looks into the tail, not just at its edge.

Tail-VaR (or CTE) averages the losses worse than the VaR threshold, so it accounts for how bad the tail really is. It is coherent (it rewards diversification) where VaR alone can mislead.

See also: var · scr · return period

Value-at-Risk (VaR)

The loss at a given quantile (e.g. 99.5%); the SCR is calibrated to a 1-in-200-year VaR.

VaR is the loss that will not be exceeded with a stated probability over a horizon. Solvency II's SCR uses a 99.5% one-year VaR — a modern restatement of ruin theory's probability-of-ruin idea.

See also: tvar · scr · solvency ii · return period · ruin theory

Reserving 9

Bornhuetter-Ferguson

Blends chain-ladder with an a-priori expected ultimate for stability on immature years.

BF weights the chain-ladder projection by the proportion of loss still unreported and adds an a-priori expected loss for the rest. It is more stable than pure chain-ladder when a year is young and its data thin.

See also: chain ladder · reserving · ibnr · credibility

Chain-ladder

Projects a run-off triangle to ultimate using volume-weighted development factors.

Chain-ladder estimates age-to-age development factors from a cumulative loss triangle and multiplies the latest diagonal to ultimate, giving reserves and IBNR. It assumes the past development pattern repeats.

See also: reserving · development factor · ibnr · bornhuetter ferguson · run off triangle

Deferred acquisition costs (DAC)

Commission and other acquisition costs carried forward and expensed as premium earns. Under IFRS 17 the unamortised balance sits inside the LRC rather than being a separate asset.

Deferral still happens and the timing is broadly unchanged; the presentation is not. DAC ceases to be an asset on the other side of the balance sheet and nets off inside the liability for remaining coverage, so gross assets and gross liabilities both shrink while equity is untouched. Watch the related change: reinsurance ceding commission now reduces the reinsurance premium expense instead of reducing acquisition costs, which raises the expense ratio and lowers the claims ratio with no profit effect at all.

See also: lrc · upr · expense ratio · acquisition cost

Development factor

The ratio linking successive columns of a loss triangle; chained together they project to ultimate.

Development (age-to-age) factors measure how losses grow from one development period to the next. Their cumulative product to the tail is the factor that grosses the latest known amount up to ultimate.

See also: chain ladder · run off triangle · ultimate · reserving

IBNR

Incurred But Not Reported: reserves held for claims that have happened but aren't yet fully reported or settled.

IBNR is carried across multiple open accident years at once. When the ultimate loss-ratio assumption is revised, every open year's IBNR is restated simultaneously — the multiplier effect behind the loss-ratio cascade.

See also: reserving · chain ladder · loss ratio · ultimate

Reserving

Estimating the money to set aside for claims already incurred — closed, reported-but-not-settled, and IBNR.

Reserving projects incurred claims to ultimate so the balance sheet holds enough for future payments. It is where the loss-ratio assumption meets the P&L: strengthen reserves and current-year profit falls.

See also: ibnr · chain ladder · ultimate · run off triangle

Run-off triangle

A grid of cumulative losses by origin (accident) year and development period — the input to reserving.

The run-off triangle arranges losses by when they occurred (rows) and how far they have developed (columns). Reserving methods project the missing lower-right of the triangle to estimate ultimate losses.

See also: chain ladder · development factor · ultimate · reserving

Ultimate loss

The final expected cost of a cohort of claims once fully developed and settled.

The ultimate is where a claims cohort ends up after all reporting and settlement. Reserves are the ultimate less what has been paid; a revision to the ultimate loss ratio restates reserves across open years.

See also: reserving · ibnr · chain ladder · loss ratio

Unearned premium reserve (UPR)

The part of written premium relating to cover not yet provided. Under IFRS 17 it is subsumed into the liability for remaining coverage.

The UPR is how premium becomes earned over the coverage period — the mechanism is unchanged under IFRS 17, only the caption. It now sits inside the LRC together with unamortised acquisition costs and premium receivable, which is why the LRC is a much smaller number than the UPR it replaced.

See also: lrc · gwp · dac · insurance revenue

Statistical foundations 15

Central limit theorem (CLT)

Sums and averages of many independent risks tend to a Normal, whatever their individual shapes.

The CLT is why aggregate losses and estimates of a mean are approximately Normal, and why ±2 standard deviations ≈ 95% is such a useful rule. It underpins both capital modelling and confidence intervals.

See also: normal · law of large numbers · mle

Coefficient of variation (CV)

Standard deviation ÷ mean — relative spread, unitless. Higher CV means a heavier tail.

The CV expresses volatility relative to size, so books of different scale can be compared. In severity it is the key tail dial: raise the CV and the large-loss risk (and required capital) grows fast.

See also: standard deviation · lognormal · severity

Cumulative distribution function (CDF)

The probability of landing at or below a value — it climbs from 0 to 1.

The CDF accumulates the PDF/PMF: F(x) = P(X ≤ x). Quantiles (like the 99.5% VaR) are read off it by inverting — find the x where F(x) = 0.995.

See also: pdf · var · return period

Expected value (mean)

The probability-weighted average outcome — where the distribution balances.

The expected value is the long-run average. It is the fair price of a random outcome and the first thing a rate is built on: the pure premium is the expected loss.

See also: variance · pure premium · law of large numbers

Exponential distribution

The memoryless waiting-time distribution; a Gamma with shape 1.

The exponential models times between independent events and simple severities. It is 'memoryless' (the future doesn't depend on elapsed time) and is the building block of the Gamma and Poisson processes.

See also: gamma · poisson

Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)

Choosing the parameters that make the observed data most likely — the standard way to fit a distribution.

MLE picks the parameter values under which your actual data has the highest probability. Under mild conditions it is consistent, efficient and approximately Normal for large samples, which is what lets you attach a confidence band — and see parameter risk as the width of the likelihood peak.

See also: method of moments · parameter risk · credibility

Method of moments

Fitting by matching the model's mean and variance to the sample's — quick, if slightly less efficient than MLE.

Method of moments sets the theoretical moments equal to the sample moments and solves for the parameters. It is a fast, transparent way to fit distributions like the Gamma, often used as a starting point for MLE.

See also: mle · gamma · variance

Negative binomial

A frequency model for over-dispersed counts — a Poisson whose rate λ is itself random.

When claim counts have variance above the mean (heterogeneous risks, contagion), the Poisson under-states volatility. The negative binomial — a Poisson mixed over a Gamma-distributed λ — restores the extra spread.

See also: poisson · over dispersion · frequency

Normal (Gaussian) distribution

The symmetric bell curve set by a mean and standard deviation; averages of many risks tend to it.

The Normal is defined by its mean and standard deviation. Individual claims are rarely Normal, but sums and averages of many independent risks are — the central limit theorem — which is why it dominates aggregate and estimation results.

See also: central limit theorem · standard deviation · law of large numbers

Over-dispersion

Variance greater than the mean in count data — the sign a plain Poisson is too tight.

Over-dispersion signals heterogeneity or clustering the Poisson can't capture (it forces variance = mean). The fix is the negative binomial or a mixed/GLM approach that lets the variance exceed the mean.

See also: negative binomial · poisson · variance

Probability density function (PDF)

For a continuous amount, the curve whose area between two points is the probability of landing there.

The PDF describes where continuous outcomes are dense. Its height is not a probability — only the area under it is. Severity models (lognormal, Gamma) are PDFs.

See also: pmf · cdf · probability distribution · lognormal

Probability distribution

A rule spreading 100% of probability across possible outcomes; height is density, area is probability.

A distribution assigns probabilities to outcomes so they sum to 1. For counts it's a PMF (bar heights are probabilities); for amounts a PDF (area under the curve is probability). It is summarised by its mean and variance.

See also: pmf · pdf · cdf · expected value · variance

Probability mass function (PMF)

For a count variable, the probability of each exact value — e.g. P(exactly 2 claims).

The PMF gives the probability of each discrete outcome; the bars sum to 1. Frequency models like the Poisson are described by a PMF.

See also: pdf · poisson · probability distribution

Standard deviation

The square root of the variance — typical distance of an outcome from the mean, in the data's own units.

Standard deviation is the everyday measure of spread. For a Normal, about 68% of outcomes fall within ±1 of it and 95% within ±2 — the intuition behind capital held far out in the tail.

See also: variance · normal · coefficient of variation

Variance

The average squared distance from the mean — how much outcomes scatter.

Variance measures spread; its square root, the standard deviation, is in the same units as the data. Together with the mean it summarises a distribution and drives capital and reinsurance.

See also: standard deviation · expected value · coefficient of variation
