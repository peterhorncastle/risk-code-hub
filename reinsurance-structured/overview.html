Structured / Finite Reinsurance — Overview

Reinsurance that blends risk transfer with financing. Structured and finite covers manage reserves, capital and volatility over time — spreading a known liability, taking over a legacy book, or capping reserve deterioration — with a limited, defined amount of genuine risk transfer.

Structured (or finite) reinsurance sits at the boundary between insurance and finance. Instead of protecting against a future event, it usually manages an existing liability — a block of reserves, a run-off book, a volatile result — using the time value of money, experience accounts and a defined slice of real risk transfer. Its legitimate modern home is the legacy and run-off market. VER

The Main Structures

Loss portfolio transfer (LPT)

The cedant pays a premium and the reinsurer takes over a block of booked reserves — removing the liability (and its run-off risk) from the cedant's balance sheet.

Adverse development cover (ADC)

A layer that pays if the reserves on a book develop worse than carried — protecting against reserve deterioration above the current level.

Aggregate / spread-loss cover

Multi-year aggregate protection with an experience account, spreading a volatile result across years and sharing the account balance at the end.

Structured quota share

A proportional cover with financing features — funds withheld, experience accounts, sliding economics — used for capital relief.

What Cedants Use It For

Legacy & run-off

Exit a discontinued line or old accident years cleanly by transferring the reserves to a specialist acquirer.

Reserve protection

Cap the risk that old reserves prove inadequate with an adverse development cover.

Capital relief

Remove or reduce a liability to free regulatory and rating-agency capital.

Volatility & finality

Smooth results over time, or achieve legal finality on a closed book via transfer.

⚠️ The risk-transfer line

Because finite reinsurance blends financing with risk transfer, it must transfer real insurance risk to be accounted for as reinsurance rather than a loan. The AIG / General Re finite-reinsurance scandal of 2005 — a deal used to flatter reserves without transferring meaningful risk — led to prosecutions and a lasting tightening of the risk-transfer test. The History and Contract modules explain the standard.
