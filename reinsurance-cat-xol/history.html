Catastrophe XoL — History & Evolution

How catastrophe excess-of-loss went from judgement-priced cover to a model-driven, capital-markets-backed segment — reshaped at every step by the catastrophe that overwhelmed the capacity of the day.

Overview History Timeline Loss Database Underwriting Rates & ROE Risk Mitigation Global Programme References Rater

Cat XoL existed long before anyone could measure catastrophe risk properly. Its modern history is the story of the tools and capital that arrived after each shock exposed how badly the peril had been under-priced. VER

Pre-1992 · Judgement era

Cover without a model

Catastrophe layers were written on underwriter judgement, market experience and crude aggregate control. Without probabilistic tools, pricing lagged exposure growth in hurricane-prone coastal regions — a mispricing that a single storm would soon expose. EST

1992–1994 · The modelling revolution

Hurricane Andrew and the Bermuda Class of 1993

Andrew (1992, ~$15.5bn insured) bankrupted eleven insurers and proved cat risk was systematically under-priced because it could not be measured. Catastrophe models (AIR, RMS, EQECAT) moved from novelty to necessity, and a wave of model-literate reinsurers capitalised in Bermuda — the "Class of 1993" — to supply the cat XoL capacity the crisis destroyed. Northridge (1994) did the same for US quake. VER

1997–2005 · Convergence begins

Cat bonds, 9/11 and the birth of the sidecar

The first modern catastrophe bonds (1997) opened capital-markets access to cat XoL risk. The 2001 attacks triggered the Bermuda "Class of 2001". Hurricane Katrina (2005, part of ~$80bn KRW) launched the "Class of 2005", the first sidecars, and accelerating cat-bond growth — alternative capital became a permanent feature of the cat tower. VER

2011–2017 · Soft market & secondary perils

ILS floods in; discipline erodes

Record-low interest rates pushed pension capital into ILS, softening cat XoL rates through 2013–2017 even after the costly 2011 year (Tōhoku, NZ quakes, Thai floods). The 2017 hurricane trio (Harvey, Irma, Maria, ~$92bn) trapped ILS collateral and ended the soft market's momentum, ushering in years of secondary-peril (wildfire, convective storm) attrition and loss creep. VER

2022–2023 · The generational reset

Ian and the hardest renewal in decades

Hurricane Ian (2022, ~$50–60bn) tipped the market. At the January 2023 renewal cat XoL rate-on-line jumped sharply, attachment points rose materially, and aggregate covers largely withdrew — reinsurers reset the terms of trade, pushing more frequency loss back to cedants and reserving their capital for genuine tail events. VER

2024–Present · Peak capital, softening

Record capacity meets disciplined attachments

Two benign, highly profitable years rebuilt reinsurer capital to a record and grew the cat-bond market 24% to $61.3bn outstanding. That weight of capital softened January 2026 property-cat rate-on-line by ~12% — the sharpest risk-adjusted decline since 2014 — though the higher post-2023 attachment points largely held, keeping frequency loss with primary carriers. VER

Forces That Shaped Cat XoL

Catastrophe models

AIR (1987) and RMS (1988) turned cat pricing from judgement into simulation — the single biggest change in how cat XoL is underwritten.

The Bermuda classes

1993, 2001 and 2005 waves of model-literate start-ups rebuilt cat capacity after each crisis.

Alternative capital

Cat bonds, sidecars and collateralised re gave pension-fund capital direct access to cat XoL layers, permanently changing supply.

Attachment discipline

The 2023 reset — higher attachments, fewer aggregates — was as important as the rate rise, moving frequency loss back to cedants.
