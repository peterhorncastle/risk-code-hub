Retrocession — Overview

Reinsurance for reinsurers. A reinsurer that has assumed too much accumulation — usually catastrophe — cedes part of it on to another reinsurer. Retrocession is the top of the risk-transfer chain, and the place where the market's own concentration is managed.

When a reinsurer buys reinsurance, that cover is retrocession. The reinsurer ceding the risk is the retrocedant; the reinsurer accepting it is the retrocessionaire. Its dominant use is catastrophe: a reinsurer that has written cat XoL for many cedants ends up with a large aggregate cat exposure, and cedes the peak of it on so its own capital is protected. VER

Forms of Retrocession

Catastrophe retro XoL

A layer on the reinsurer's net catastrophe accumulation — the dominant form, priced like any cat layer off a modelled expected loss.

Whole-account / quota share retro

A proportional cession of a reinsurer's whole book to share capital and volatility — sometimes with strategic capital partners.

Industry loss warranty (ILW)

A binary cover that pays if a market-wide industry loss exceeds a trigger — simple, index-based, and heavily used by ILS capital.

Aggregate & second-event covers

Protections against a bad cat year or a costly second event, filling gaps left by per-occurrence retro.

Why Reinsurers Buy Retro

Peak-zone protection

Caps the reinsurer's exposure to a single mega-catastrophe in a peak zone (US wind, Japan quake) that would otherwise dominate its result.

Capital efficiency

Reduces the reinsurer's own required capital under Solvency II / rating-agency models, freeing capacity to write more.

Volatility management

Smooths the reinsurer's earnings so a single heavy cat year does not threaten its rating or dividend.

Accessing ILS capital

Retro is the main gateway through which capital-markets money (cat bonds, sidecars, collateralised reinsurance) supports the traditional market.

⚠️ The spiral — retro's original sin

In the late-1980s London Market Excess ("LMX") spiral, reinsurers retroceded to each other in a loop, so a single loss — Piper Alpha in 1988 — passed round the circle again and again, multiplying far beyond the original loss. Modern retro guards against this with clash analysis, transparency on underlying exposures and collateral. The History module tells the story.
