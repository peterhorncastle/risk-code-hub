Era History
Pre-1930 — The Birth of Motor Insurance

Early Motoring Risks: Horses, Cyclists and Public Fear

The world's first motor insurance policy is attributed to Travelers Insurance Company of Hartford, Connecticut, which issued a policy in 1897 covering a vehicle driven by Gilbert Loomis in Dayton, Ohio. The premium was US$7.50 for $500 of liability coverage. In Britain, the Royal Automobile Club and Lloyd's market began providing bespoke motor cover around the same period, as the nascent automobile industry struggled against public hostility and restrictive legislation (the Locomotive Acts required a man to walk in front of a motor vehicle waving a red flag until 1896).

The Paris Convention on Automobile Traffic (1926) was among the first international attempts to harmonise rules for motor vehicles crossing borders. Early international motor exposures were minimal — vehicles rarely crossed borders — but diplomatic missions, wealthy travellers and early commercial transport companies sought cover for Continental touring. Lloyd's syndicates accommodated these risks on a bespoke basis. VER

By the late 1920s, the growth of road transport was creating serious public policy concerns about uncompensated accident victims. Multiple jurisdictions simultaneously began debating compulsory third-party insurance legislation, setting the stage for the regulatory frameworks that define the international motor market today.

1930–1960 — Compulsion and the Green Card Precursor

Mandatory MTPL Laws and the First Cross-Border Framework

The 1930s saw a wave of compulsory motor third-party liability (MTPL) legislation. The United Kingdom's Road Traffic Act 1930 introduced mandatory third-party liability insurance for motorists — a world-leading move, driven by the appalling road accident toll of the late 1920s. France, Germany and other European nations followed through the 1930s and 1940s, creating a patchwork of national compulsory motor insurance systems. VER

The critical international problem emerged quickly: what happened when a vehicle insured in one country caused an accident in another? The victim in the country of accident had no direct right of action against a foreign insurer, and compulsory insurance in the home country did not protect victims in transit countries. This gap drove the development of what would become the Green Card system.

At the United Nations Economic Commission for Europe (UNECE) meetings in Geneva, a framework was agreed in 1949 — implemented through the Council of Bureaux in 1953 — creating the international motor insurance certificate, universally known as the Green Card (from the original green paper stock). Under the system, an insurer in the country of accident (the "bureau of the country of accident") would handle the claim on behalf of the foreign insurer, with recovery between bureaux handled centrally. VER

1960–1980 — EU Directives Begin; UK Road Traffic Act 1972

European Harmonisation and the Birth of MTPL Directives

The 1960s and 1970s saw the European Economic Community begin its long project of harmonising motor insurance rules across member states. The First EU Motor Insurance Directive (72/166/EEC) required all member states to abolish Green Card checks at internal EEC borders — a motorist no longer needed to show a Green Card when crossing from France to Germany within the EEC. Instead, domestic motor insurance automatically extended to cover third-party liability across all member states. VER

In the United Kingdom, the Road Traffic Act 1972 consolidated and strengthened compulsory motor insurance requirements, introducing the right of victims to claim directly against the insurer (direct action rights). The Act also established the framework for the Motor Insurers' Bureau to handle claims from uninsured and untraced drivers — a model widely imitated internationally. VER

The Lloyd's market during this period was writing international motor primarily as bespoke direct risk for wealthy individuals, diplomatic missions, and early multinational corporate fleet clients. The binder/delegated authority model for motor was still nascent; most risks were individually underwritten in the Room at Lloyd's. International motor premium was modest relative to today's scale.

1980–2000 — EU Motor Directives Expand; Delegated Authority Grows

Second and Third EU Directives; Lloyd's International Binder Market Develops

The Second EU Motor Insurance Directive (84/5/EEC, 1984) introduced minimum mandatory limits for MTPL cover across member states — a major step towards genuine harmonisation. It also required member states to establish guarantee funds to compensate victims of uninsured or unidentified vehicles. The Third EU Motor Insurance Directive (90/232/EEC, 1990) extended compulsory MTPL to cover passengers in the insured vehicle as standard, addressing a long-standing gap. VER

During the 1980s and 1990s, Lloyd's syndicates increasingly granted binding authorities to specialist motor managing general agents (MGAs) operating in international markets — Australia, South Africa, the Middle East, and later Latin America and South-East Asia. These MGAs had local market expertise, distribution networks and claims handling infrastructure that Lloyd's syndicates could not economically replicate directly. This delegated authority model became the structural foundation of the Lloyd's international motor class and remains dominant today. VER

Direct compensation regimes — where the victim's own insurer handles the claim and recovers from the at-fault party's insurer — were introduced progressively across EU member states, reducing the burden on victims but creating new intercompany recovery complexity for international motor insurers.

2000–2015 — Consolidated EU Directive; Telematics Revolution Begins

EU Motor Directive 2009 Consolidated; UBI and Telematics Launch

The EU Motor Insurance Directive 2009/103/EC consolidated the five preceding EU motor directives into a single instrument. Minimum MTPL limits were standardised at EUR 1m per victim (BI) and EUR 1m for property damage, with member states free to set higher minimums. The Directive also addressed the habitual residence principle for international accidents — where an EEC resident was injured in an accident abroad, their own member state's bureau would handle the claim. VER

The landmark Vnuk v Zavarovalnica Triglav (C-162/13) ECJ decision in 2014 dramatically expanded the scope of MTPL under EU law: the court held that MTPL must cover any use of a vehicle "consistent with its function as a vehicle," including on private land. This threatened to extend compulsory motor insurance to tractors, forklift trucks and ride-on lawnmowers — with major implications for motor insurers across Europe and for Lloyd's international motor risks. VER

Telematics and usage-based insurance (UBI) launched commercially in Italy (2002, the world's first mass-market telematics scheme), with Direct Line's UBI product launching in the UK in 2012 and Progressive Insurance operating its Snapshot programme in the USA. Internationally, telematics programmes spread to South Africa (Discovery Insure, 2011), Australia and the Middle East. For Lloyd's international motor delegated programmes, telematics data began offering a new source of risk selection and portfolio management information. VER

2015–Present — EV Revolution; ADAS; Autonomous Vehicles; COVID

Technology Disrupts the Motor Insurance Value Chain

The post-2015 period has been defined by simultaneous technology disruptions to the motor risk landscape. Electric vehicles have grown from a curiosity to 15–20% of new car sales in major markets by 2024 (IEA). For insurers, EVs present higher repair severity (battery damage is often uneconomic to repair), new fire peril (thermal runaway), range anxiety-related accidents, and different third-party liability profiles for autonomous modes. OEM data sharing remains a major unresolved issue for claim investigation. EST

Advanced Driver Assistance Systems (ADAS) — automatic emergency braking, lane-keep assist, adaptive cruise control — have demonstrably reduced accident frequency in equipped vehicles. Thatcham Research's data shows 27% reduction in injury accidents for ADAS-equipped vehicles in the UK. However, ADAS increases repair severity because sensors, cameras and radar units embedded in bumpers, windscreens and mirrors are expensive to replace and recalibrate — transforming minor incidents into complex repair jobs. EST

The COVID-19 pandemic (2020) produced a sharp frequency drop in motor claims as lockdowns removed vehicles from roads globally, generating unusually favourable loss ratios in 2020. The correction came in 2021–23: repair cost inflation (semiconductor shortages restricting new vehicle production, elevated second-hand vehicle values, supply chain disruption of OEM parts) pushed average claim costs 30–40% above pre-pandemic levels across many markets, compressing margins sharply. EST

Lloyd's International Motor Market Context: The Lloyd's international motor market (MF/MG/MH/MI/MP) is estimated at US$1.5–2bn GWP, written predominantly through delegated authority schemes. Lloyd's syndicates serve as wholesale capacity providers to specialist international motor MGAs covering markets where Lloyd's brand, financial strength and regulatory passport provide a competitive advantage over local admitted markets. The market has seen hardening rates from 2020 driven by repair cost inflation, EV severity uncertainty, and accumulation concerns in NatCat-exposed territories (Australian hail, Caribbean hurricanes). EST
Key Milestones
1897

World's First Motor Policy

Travelers Insurance issues what is considered the first motor vehicle insurance policy in Dayton, Ohio; $7.50 premium for $500 liability cover.

1930

UK Road Traffic Act

First compulsory motor third-party liability legislation, introducing mandatory MTPL insurance for all motorists — a model for international regulators.

1949

Green Card System Agreed

UNECE Geneva framework for international motor insurance certificates — bureau-to-bureau claim handling mechanism agreed, formalised 1953.

1972

First EU Motor Directive

72/166/EEC abolishes Green Card border checks within the EEC — domestic MTPL automatically extends across all member states.

1984

Second EU Motor Directive

84/5/EEC sets minimum MTPL limits and requires guarantee funds for uninsured/unidentified vehicle claims across all member states.

1990

Third EU Motor Directive

90/232/EEC extends compulsory MTPL to cover vehicle passengers as standard, addressing a historic coverage gap across European markets.

2002

Italy — First Mass Telematics

Italy launches the world's first mass-market vehicle telematics scheme under insurance industry agreements, precursor to global UBI programmes.

2009

EU Consolidated Motor Directive

2009/103/EC consolidates five motor directives; standardises minimum MTPL at EUR 1m+ and introduces habitual residence provisions for cross-border accidents.

2014

Vnuk ECJ Ruling

C-162/13: ECJ holds MTPL covers all vehicle use consistent with its vehicular function — potentially extending compulsory cover to agricultural and industrial vehicles.

2021

EU Directive Amendment — Higher Limits

EU Motor Insurance Directive amended: minimum BI limit raised to EUR 6.45m per incident; PD to EUR 1.22m — first significant limit increase since 2009 consolidation.
