Underwriting Methodology

BTE and ATE rating factors, merits assessment, worked examples & key exclusions

BTE vs ATE — Fundamentally Different Underwriting Approaches: Before-the-event (BTE) LEI is rated as a portfolio product — the underwriter does not know which specific dispute will arise. Rating is based on sector, employee count, scope of cover and statistical claims frequency. After-the-event (ATE) LEI is underwritten case by case — the underwriter acts as a quasi-legal assessor, evaluating the specific dispute's prospects of success, claim value, litigation stage and cost exposure. These are two very different underwriting disciplines.[I2]
BTE Rating Factors — High Impact
High Impact — BTE

Business Type & Employee Count

The primary BTE rating factor for commercial LEI. Employee count drives employment dispute frequency exposure — the main claims trigger. Business type determines the mix of contract, regulatory and property dispute risk. Hospitality and retail have high employment risk (high turnover, tip/wage disputes, zero-hours contracts). Professional services have high contract dispute risk. [I9]

Base rate: £12–60 per employee p.a. depending on sector and scope
High Impact — BTE

Industry Sector Employment Risk

Employment tribunal frequency varies substantially by sector: hospitality, retail and social care have highest employment tribunal rates (high staff turnover, casual employment, tip/wage disputes, zero-hours contracts). Professional services and finance have lower frequency but higher per-claim cost. Technology sector shows rising employment status and discrimination claims.[I4]

Sector loading: 0.7× (professional services) to 2.5× (hospitality/retail)
High Impact — BTE

Litigation History (3–5 Year)

Prior LEI claims are a strong predictor of future frequency, particularly for employment disputes. A pattern of employment tribunal claims may indicate systemic HR management weaknesses. A clean 5-year record attracts credit. Single large contract litigation loss assessed for circumstances — if due to specific transaction, may not indicate systemic risk.

Loading: +30 to +100% adverse history; credit: −10 to −15% 5-year clean
High Impact — BTE

Geographic Scope & International Exposure

UK-only cover vs European vs worldwide exposure creates significantly different risk profiles — employment laws vary enormously internationally. US exposure (employment class actions, FLSA) is particularly high risk and typically subject to significant surcharge or exclusion. German works council obligations, French prud'hommes system and Australian Fair Work provisions each create distinct legal exposures.[I8]

International loading: +25–80% for worldwide including US employment cover
ATE Rating Factors — High Impact
High Impact — ATE

Merits Assessment (Prospects of Success)

The single most important ATE rating factor. ATE underwriters assess prospects of success on the merits of the specific case — ATE cover is typically only available where prospects exceed 51% (more likely than not to succeed). The ATE underwriter acts as quasi-legal adviser, reviewing solicitor merits opinion, pleadings and evidence. ATE premiums are risk-rated against this merits assessment, not on portfolio statistics.[L5]

Merits below 51%: policy unavailable. 51–60%: high premium. 70%+: standard/lower premium
High Impact — ATE

Claim Type (PI / Commercial / Clinical Negligence)

ATE market segments by claim type with distinct pricing and capacity: personal injury (QOCS-modified market); clinical negligence (ATE expert report premium still recoverable — exception to LASPO); commercial litigation (non-recoverable ATE, litigation funder integration); employment (specific ATE market post-LASPO); group/class actions (aggregate ATE with litigation funders).[L1]

Clinical negligence: +30–60% vs equivalent-merits PI; commercial: case-specific
High Impact — ATE

Stage of Litigation at ATE Inception

The stage of the dispute at which ATE is written materially affects risk. Pre-litigation ATE: most uncertain, often highest premium (adverse costs risk if claim does not proceed). Post-issue pre-trial: clearer picture of merits and adverse costs quantum. Trial-stage ATE: highest premium rate but smallest number of cases. Late-stage ATE for specific risk events (Part 36 offers, specific hearing) also available.[I2]

Pre-litigation: 25–40% of adverse costs estimate. Trial stage: 40–60%+ of adverse costs
High Impact — ATE

Adverse Costs Exposure Quantum

ATE premium is driven by the quantum of the adverse costs risk being insured. For commercial litigation, adverse costs are assessed by reference to the defendant's estimated legal costs (often comparable to claimant's costs). For employment tribunal claims, the adverse costs risk is lower (employment tribunals rarely award costs except for unreasonable conduct). For PI post-QOCS, adverse costs risk is primarily fraud/dishonesty scenarios.

ATE premium typically 20–50% of the adverse costs exposure being insured
Medium Impact Rating Factors
Medium Impact

Limit of Indemnity (BTE)

BTE limits typically £25,000–£250,000 for SME commercial policies; £50,000–£500,000 for larger commercial risks. Higher limits attract increased limit factors. Limits must be realistic for the scope of cover — employment tribunal claim costs can reach £50,000+ for complex cases with senior employees. Tax investigation limits typically separate from employment/commercial limits.[I9]

ILF (£100k vs £50k): approx 1.5–1.8×; (£250k vs £100k): approx 1.6–2.0×
Medium Impact

Panel Solicitor Compliance (BTE)

BTE insurers manage claims costs through panel solicitor arrangements. Policyholders who use insurer-approved panel solicitors have claims handled at agreed rates, reducing average claims cost. Freedom of choice of lawyer (EU Directive right) means insureds can insist on own solicitor for proceedings requiring legal representation — but at their own risk if costs exceed policy limit. Panel compliance significantly reduces claims cost.[L7]

Panel solicitor claims average cost 20–35% below non-panel claims for equivalent matters
Medium Impact

Funding Structure (ATE)

Whether ATE is combined with a conditional fee agreement (CFA), damages-based agreement (DBA — post-PACCAR 2023 complexity), or used standalone alongside third-party litigation funding affects risk dynamics. CFA/ATE combination: solicitor has skin in the game, aligns incentives on merits. Litigation funder/ATE combination: large commercial disputes; funder conducts independent merits due diligence — positive signal.[I6]

Medium Impact

Scope of Cover (BTE)

BTE scope varies considerably: basic employment-only cover through to comprehensive (employment + contract + property + tax investigation + GDPR/data protection + regulatory investigations). Broader scope increases frequency but also makes the policy more valuable and commercially competitive. Tax investigation cover is often the highest-frequency trigger for many SME policyholders. GDPR cover is now commonly included or available as an add-on.

ATE Merits-Based Pricing Methodology
// ATE Insurance — Case-Specific Premium Calculation (Illustrative)
// (NOT rate-on-premium — each case individually assessed)

ATE Premium = Adverse Costs Exposure
  × Base Rate Factor // 15–50% of adverse costs exposure
  × Merits Adjustment // 51–60%: 1.8–2.5×; 70–80%: 0.8–1.0×; 80%+: 0.6–0.8×
  × Stage Multiplier // Pre-litigation: 1.0×; Post-issue: 1.3×; Pre-trial: 1.6–2.0×
  × Claim Type Factor // PI: 1.0× base; clinical neg: 1.3–1.5×; commercial: case-specific
  × Quantum Factor // Higher adverse costs exposure → typically lower rate on risk
  × Funding Structure Factor // Litigation funder present: credit (independent due diligence)

// ATE premium is DEFERRED if structured as a premium payable only on success

Speculative or frivolous claimsATEATE underwriter's merits assessment filters out speculative claims. ATE insurers reserve the right to withdraw cover if merits deteriorate below threshold during proceedingsStep-in right: ATE insurer may terminate if merits fall below threshold
ATE Withdrawal Risk: ATE policies typically give the insurer the right to withdraw cover if prospects of success fall below the threshold during proceedings (e.g. after disclosure reveals adverse evidence, or following an adverse interim ruling). This creates a risk for the funded claimant and their solicitor — if ATE cover is withdrawn mid-case, the claimant faces uninsured adverse costs exposure. Solicitors advising ATE policyholders must understand the insurer's step-in and withdrawal rights and manage the client's expectations accordingly.[I2]
