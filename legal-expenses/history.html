Era History
Pre-1960 — Origins: Germany, Trade Unions and Champertous Rules

Legal Costs Indemnity Emerges — Champertous Maintenance Restricts Growth in Common Law Jurisdictions

Legal expenses insurance originated in continental Europe, particularly Germany, where the concept of insuring legal costs was not constrained by common law doctrines of maintenance and champerty. These doctrines, rooted in medieval English law, prohibited third parties from funding litigation in exchange for a share of the proceeds — meaning that early English legal expenses insurance faced legal obstacles that did not apply in civil law jurisdictions. VER

DAS Rechtsschutz (Deutsche Automobil Schutz) was founded in Munich in 1928, becoming the world's first specialist legal expenses insurer and providing motor legal expenses cover to German motorists. This is widely regarded as the birth of the modern LEI market. DAS remains one of the largest LEI providers globally today. EST

In the UK, trade unions provided an important early access-to-justice mechanism — union legal assistance funds paid for members' legal representation in workplace disputes, personal injury claims and housing matters. This proto-LEI function of trade unions was economically significant in a market where individual access to solicitors was prohibitively expensive.

Champertous rules were progressively relaxed in the UK during the 20th century, enabling the later growth of conditional fee agreements and ATE insurance. The restriction that had kept the UK market underdeveloped relative to Germany began to lift. VER

1960–1990 — European LEI Market Develops; Lloyd's Enters

ARAG, DAS, Allianz Legal Expand; EC Motor Directive; UK Legal Aid Era

The German and Austrian LEI market developed substantially through the 1960s and 70s. Major specialist LEI providers including ARAG (founded Düsseldorf 1935), DAS, Allianz Legal Protection and Roland Rechtsschutz established the continental European model: broad-scope BTE cover sold as standalone policies or as motor policy add-ons, with panel solicitor management of claims. EST

The EC Motor Directive (later consolidated into the Motor Insurance Directive) required that motor third-party liability policies include legal protection for occupants to pursue claims — effectively mandating a form of legal expenses for motor policyholders across EC member states. This drove mass-market BTE penetration in motor insurance across Europe.

Lloyd's syndicates began writing legal expenses risks during this period, primarily for commercial and specialist risks. The London market offered greater flexibility on scope and limits than the heavily standardised continental LEI markets, attracting complex or large commercial risks.

In the UK, the era of generous legal aid (1949–1990) meant that many individuals qualified for state-funded legal representation. This reduced demand for private BTE insurance among individuals, though commercial LEI continued to develop. The erosion of legal aid eligibility from the 1990s would later drive BTE market growth as individuals lost access to state funding. EST

1990–2000 — Conditional Fee Agreements and the ATE Market Is Born

CFAs Introduced; ATE Insurance Created; Personal Injury Boom

The Courts and Legal Services Act 1990 VER gave the Lord Chancellor power to permit conditional fee agreements (CFAs) — the "no win no fee" arrangement by which solicitors could charge no fee if the case was lost, but an uplift (success fee) if it was won. CFAs were introduced in limited areas from 1995 (personal injury, insolvency and human rights cases).

CFAs created an immediate demand for after-the-event (ATE) insurance. A claimant proceeding on a CFA faced no solicitors' fee risk (covered by the CFA), but still faced the risk of an adverse costs order if the case was lost — having to pay the winning defendant's legal costs. ATE insurance emerged to cover precisely this adverse costs risk, enabling CFA-funded claimants to litigate without catastrophic downside exposure.

The personal injury sector embraced CFAs and ATE with enthusiasm. Solicitor firms developed volume personal injury businesses on CFA terms, backed by ATE insurance from specialist providers. The ATE market grew rapidly, with major providers including Temple Legal Protection, Litigation Protection and several Lloyd's syndicates entering the market. Crucially, under the rules at the time, the successful claimant could recover the ATE premium from the losing defendant — making ATE effectively cost-free to the claimant in successful cases. VER

The UK BTE market also grew as legal aid eligibility was progressively tightened. Household and motor policies increasingly bundled BTE legal expenses cover as standard. By 2000, the majority of UK motor policies included a BTE legal expenses add-on, often at negligible cost (£20–30 per year). EST

2000–2012 — ATE Market Flourishes; Jackson Review; LASPO Enacted

ATE Premiums Recoverable; Costs Inflation; Jackson Transforms the Landscape

The pre-LASPO decade was the golden era of the ATE market. ATE premiums were recoverable from losing defendants under the Access to Justice Act 1999 regime, making ATE insurance a commercially powerful mechanism. ATE providers competed to offer the best terms, and the market expanded into clinical negligence, commercial disputes and group litigation.

However, the system created significant costs inflation. ATE premiums — recoverable from defendants — grew to sometimes exceed the damages claimed in smaller cases. Clinical negligence ATE premiums could run to hundreds of thousands of pounds. Defendants and their insurers (particularly NHS Resolution and motor insurers) complained loudly about the costs burden. The litigation system was seen as imbalanced in favour of claimants. EST

Lord Justice Jackson was commissioned to undertake a Review of Civil Litigation Costs. His final report (January 2010) VER was comprehensive and transformative. Among his key recommendations: making success fees and ATE premiums non-recoverable; introducing qualified one-way cost shifting (QOCS) for personal injury claimants; and tightening costs management in the courts.

The Legal Aid, Sentencing and Punishment of Offenders Act 2012 (LASPO) VER enacted the core Jackson recommendations. From April 2013, ATE premiums and CFA success fees became non-recoverable from the losing party in most civil litigation. This was the most fundamental structural change to the UK legal costs market in a generation. VER

2012–2020 — QOCS, Unison, GDPR and BTE Growth

ATE Market Restructures; Employment Tribunal Fees Struck Down; GDPR Creates New Trigger

Post-LASPO, the ATE personal injury market collapsed in volume as the economics of the old model disappeared. Claimant solicitors could no longer offer truly "no cost" litigation backed by recoverable ATE. The market restructured around higher-merit, higher-value cases where the economics still worked with non-recoverable premiums.

Qualified One-Way Cost Shifting (QOCS), introduced alongside LASPO for personal injury cases, partially restored the balance — it meant that a losing personal injury claimant generally could not be made to pay the winning defendant's costs unless the claimant had behaved unreasonably. QOCS reduced (but did not eliminate) the need for ATE in PI cases.

In 2013, employment tribunal fees were introduced (£160–250 issue fee; £230–950 hearing fee). The Supreme Court's landmark judgment in R (Unison) v Lord Chancellor [2017] UKSC 51 VER unanimously struck down these fees as unlawful — they had reduced employment tribunal claims by approximately 70% and represented an unlawful restriction on access to justice. The Unison case became a landmark in access-to-justice law.

The EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) came into force in May 2018 VER, creating a wave of new legal disputes. Data breach claims, subject access request disputes, regulatory investigations by the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) and class action data breach claims (following Morrisons, British Airways and other data breaches) all generated LEI activity. Data protection disputes became the fastest-growing commercial LEI trigger.

Lloyd's LE Market Context: Lloyd's syndicates write legal expenses insurance primarily in the commercial BTE and ATE segments. The UK LEI market is dominated by DAS (part of Munich Re Group), ARAG, Allianz Legal Protection, Arc Legal Assistance and Markel. Lloyd's capacity is deployed across specialist commercial LEI, multinational LE programmes, ATE for high-value commercial and clinical negligence disputes, and costs and disbursements insurance for litigation funders. Estimated Lloyd's LE GWP is not publicly disclosed at sub-class level. VER
2020–Present — COVID Employment Surge, Litigation Funding and Fixed Recoverable Costs

Pandemic Disputes, Third-Party Litigation Funding Growth, FRC Reform

COVID-19 generated a wave of employment-related legal disputes from 2020: redundancy disputes, furlough (CJRS) eligibility disputes, flexible working refusals, and discrimination claims related to workplace safety. The volume of ACAS early conciliation notifications increased sharply in 2020–21, driving BTE employment claims. LEI providers reported elevated employment dispute frequencies. EST

The FCA Business Interruption Test Case (2020) — while primarily a property/BI coverage dispute — involved significant LEI and litigation funding activity. The FCA and policyholder groups brought coordinated test case litigation, and several ATE providers and litigation funders supported the proceedings. The Supreme Court's judgment in January 2021 confirmed coverage for many policyholders.

Third-party litigation funding (Burford Capital, Harbour Litigation Funding, Litigation Capital Management) grew substantially through this period, particularly for commercial disputes and group actions. Litigation funders became important buyers of ATE insurance (to protect their investment) and the relationship between ATE insurance and litigation finance evolved into an integrated funding structure for large commercial claims. EST

The Fixed Recoverable Costs (FRC) regime was extended in October 2023 to cover most civil cases valued up to £100,000 — a major further reform of the UK litigation costs landscape. FRC limits the legal costs that can be recovered between parties, changing the economics of both BTE (by capping insurer's indemnity in covered cases) and ATE (by changing adverse costs risk). Market participants are still assessing the full FRC impact. VER

The Employment Relations (Flexible Working) Act 2023 and ongoing employment law reforms continue to generate new dispute categories. AI-related employment disputes (algorithmic management, automated decision-making) are an emerging frontier for commercial LEI. VER

Key Milestones
1928

DAS Founded — Munich

Deutsche Automobil Schutz founded in Munich — the world's first specialist legal expenses insurer, providing motor LEI to German motorists.

1949

UK Legal Aid Act

Legal Aid and Advice Act 1949 establishes state-funded legal aid in England and Wales — reduces private LEI demand for individuals for three decades.

1990

Courts and Legal Services Act

CLSA 1990 enables conditional fee agreements — the legislative foundation for the ATE insurance market and "no win no fee" litigation. VER

1995

CFAs Introduced (Phased)

Conditional fee agreements permitted for personal injury, insolvency and human rights cases. The ATE insurance market is born — solicitors need adverse costs cover for CFA-funded clients.

1999

Access to Justice Act

ATE premiums become recoverable from losing parties — creates powerful commercial incentive driving ATE market growth from 2000 to 2012.

2010

Jackson Review Published

Lord Justice Jackson's Review of Civil Litigation Costs recommends making success fees and ATE premiums non-recoverable — transformative report. VER

2012

LASPO Enacted

Legal Aid, Sentencing and Punishment of Offenders Act 2012 — ATE premiums and success fees non-recoverable from April 2013. Fundamental market restructuring. VER

2017

Unison v Lord Chancellor

Supreme Court strikes down employment tribunal fees unanimously — unlawful restriction on access to justice. Employment tribunal volumes recover. VER

2018

GDPR — New LEI Trigger

EU GDPR in force May 2018. Data breach disputes, ICO investigations and subject access request disputes become a significant new commercial LEI trigger. VER

2023

Fixed Recoverable Costs Extended

FRC regime extended to civil cases up to £100,000 (October 2023) — reshapes economics of BTE and ATE insurance for lower-value commercial disputes. VER
