Module 01 — History & Origins

Agricultural Crop & Forestry — History

Six eras of agricultural and forestry insurance — from mutual benefit societies and early hail covers through the Great Depression, government MPCI programmes, and the climate-driven era of catastrophic drought and wildfire.

Agricultural insurance has its roots in mutual aid — farmers pooling risk against hail and fire long before formal insurance markets existed. The development of modern crop insurance is inseparable from the history of government intervention, food security policy, and the repeated discovery that catastrophic droughts and crop failures are simply too large for private markets to bear without public support. VER

Era 1 — Pre-1900

Mutual Aid, Early Hail Societies & Lloyd's Timber Trade

Organised crop protection began with European hail insurance societies in the early 19th century. The first dedicated hail insurance company, the Hagelversicherungs-Gesellschaft, was founded in Germany in 1797 — reflecting the particular importance of hail as an acute, verifiable, and catastrophic peril for grain and viticulture crops. Similar mutual hail societies proliferated across Central Europe (Switzerland, Austria, France) through the 1800s, funded by farmer premiums and operated as mutual benefit organisations. VER

In the UK, Lloyd's underwriters provided coverage for timber merchants' stock and timber cargoes from the 18th century — positioning the AG (forestry/silviculture) code as a natural extension of the market's historical marine timber trade coverage. Timber estates in Scandinavia and the Baltic were a major Lloyd's marine cargo risk throughout the 19th century, and this relationship with the forestry industry laid the groundwork for standing timber coverage in the 20th century. VER

In the USA, the 1880s saw the first attempts at formal crop insurance, but early providers failed due to adverse selection — only farmers in high-risk areas purchased coverage, causing loss ratios to exceed 100% for most early insurers. The lesson — that crop insurance requires broad participation to prevent adverse selection — would take another 50 years to be institutionally resolved through government-mandated programmes. VER

Era 2 — 1900–1945

Great Depression, Dust Bowl & the Birth of Government Crop Insurance

The 1930s Dust Bowl — a decade of catastrophic drought and wind erosion across the US Great Plains from 1930 to 1939 — was the defining agricultural disaster of the 20th century. Some 100 million acres of Great Plains topsoil were destroyed; millions of farming families abandoned their land. The private insurance market had no viable product capable of covering drought at this scale. VER

The US federal response was the Federal Crop Insurance Act of 1938, which created the Federal Crop Insurance Corporation (FCIC) — the first major government-backed crop insurance programme in the world. The initial programme was limited in crop and geographic coverage but established the fundamental principle that the federal government would backstop agricultural risk that private markets could not sustainably absorb. VER

In Europe, the inter-war period saw continued expansion of hail insurance mutual societies, but the devastation of World War II disrupted agricultural insurance markets profoundly — crop insurance was irrelevant in a context where farms were bombed and agricultural labour conscripted. Post-war reconstruction focused on food production rather than risk management. Lloyd's capacity for agricultural risks was limited and largely confined to timber stock and trade. EST

Era 3 — 1945–1980

Post-War Reconstruction, Green Revolution & Early Forestry Insurance

The post-war Green Revolution — the application of modern agronomy, hybridised seed varieties, synthetic fertilisers, and mechanised farming — massively increased global agricultural production between 1950 and 1980. As farm enterprises became larger, more capital-intensive, and more financially dependent on a single harvest, the demand for formal crop insurance grew. VER

In the USA, the FCIC programme expanded gradually through the 1950s–1970s but remained unprofitable for the federal government — drought years consistently produced loss ratios exceeding 200%. By 1980, it was clear the existing programme structure was financially unsustainable. The private sector played a minimal role. VER

European countries developed varied approaches to agricultural risk: France established a government-backed insurance system; Spain created Agroseguro (a public-private pool); Germany and Switzerland retained largely private hail insurance markets with limited government support. The Common Agricultural Policy (CAP), launched in 1957 with the Treaty of Rome, provided price supports rather than risk insurance — effectively insulating farmers from price risk through guaranteed purchase prices, reducing the perceived need for revenue insurance. VER

The forestry insurance market began to formalise in Scandinavia in the 1960s–1970s as commercial softwood plantations expanded. Finland, Sweden, and Norway became significant forestry insurance markets, with domestic insurers providing fire and windstorm coverage for state and private forest estates. Lloyd's began writing standing timber coverage for large commercial forest estates in this period. EST

Era 4 — 1980–2000

US MPCI Reform, El Niño Events & European Windstorm Disasters

The Federal Crop Insurance Act of 1980 fundamentally restructured the US crop insurance market — allowing private Approved Insurance Providers (AIPs) to sell and service FCIC-backed policies with the government providing reinsurance and subsidising premiums. The reform sought to replace ad hoc disaster payments with a systematic, insurance-based safety net. Participation remained low through the 1980s, however, with many farmers relying on expectation of disaster payments instead. VER

The 1988 US drought — the most severe since the 1930s — and the 1993 Midwest floods demonstrated the limits of the reformed system. Congress responded with the Federal Crop Insurance Reform Act of 1994, which made crop insurance effectively mandatory for farmers receiving federal farm programme benefits. Premium subsidies were increased. Participation surged and the US MPCI market entered its modern phase. VER

The El Niño event of 1997–1998 caused catastrophic crop losses across Southeast Asia, Australia, and Latin America — drought in one region paired with floods in another. This established climate pattern variability as a major underwriting consideration for global agricultural risk. Lloyd's began writing weather index products and reinsurance of Asian crop programmes in this period. VER

European forestry insurance suffered two catastrophic windstorm losses in this era: Windstorm Vivian/Wiebke (1990) destroyed an estimated 120 million cubic metres of timber across Central Europe (Switzerland, Germany, France), causing billions in forestry losses. Windstorm Lothar (December 1999) was even more destructive — 200 million cubic metres of blown-down timber across France, Switzerland, and Germany, with total forestry losses estimated at €3 billion. These events permanently established windstorm as the primary European forestry insurance peril and drove significant changes in European forestry underwriting. VER

Era 5 — 2000–2015

Climate Events, US Drought 2012 & Emerging Market Expansion

The 2003 European heat wave — the hottest summer in Europe for 500 years — caused an estimated €13 billion in agricultural losses across France, Germany, Italy, Spain, and the UK. French farmers suffered massive crop failures; wine harvests were severely affected. The event demonstrated that even temperate European agriculture was highly exposed to temperature extremes and prompted significant growth in European MPCI development. VER

The 2011–2012 US drought was the largest single crop insurance event in US history. The 2012 drought affected 80% of agricultural land in the continental US; corn and soybean yields collapsed. Total insured crop losses reached $17.3 billion — far exceeding previous records. USDA RMA paid out $17 billion in indemnities, demonstrating both the scale of the federal backstop and the systemic nature of drought risk. VER

Emerging market agricultural insurance expanded dramatically in the 2000s. India launched the National Agricultural Insurance Scheme (NAIS) in 1999 and progressively reformed it into the more ambitious PMFBY (Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana) in 2016. China's government-backed agricultural insurance programme grew to become the world's second-largest crop insurance market by premium volume. The World Bank, IFC, and development agencies supported weather index insurance pilots in sub-Saharan Africa, which demonstrated that parametric products could reach smallholder farmers profitably. VER

The bark beetle epidemic in British Columbia, Canada (2003–2012) killed over 700 million cubic metres of standing pine timber — the largest forest insect infestation in North American history. The sheer scale of the losses — well in excess of any standing timber fire loss — demonstrated that biological risk in forestry was a potential catastrophe exposure that most standing timber policies explicitly excluded or sub-limited. VER

Era 6 — 2015–2024

Wildfire Catastrophes, Precision Agriculture & Climate Stress

The period from 2017 to 2024 produced an unprecedented series of wildfire seasons — California (2017, 2018 Camp Fire, 2020, 2021), Australia (2019–2020 Black Summer), Portugal (2017), Canada (2023 worst season on record: 18 million hectares burned) — that transformed both the standing timber insurance market and climate risk underwriting globally. Forest estate owners who had carried adequate coverage often found their limits inadequate for the full combination of standing timber loss, salvage costs, reforestation expense, and business interruption. VER

Precision agriculture — the application of satellite imaging, drone surveys, AI-based yield prediction, and soil sensor networks — began to transform agricultural underwriting. For the first time, insurers and reinsurers could monitor crop conditions at individual field level remotely and in near-real-time. This dramatically reduced the moral hazard and adverse selection problems that had historically plagued crop insurance, enabling more accurate risk assessment and earlier loss detection. EST

The African Swine Fever (ASF) epidemic in China (2018–2020) killed an estimated 40–50% of China's pig population — approximately 100 million animals — causing livestock insurance losses on an unprecedented scale and reshaping global pork markets. The episode highlighted livestock mortality as a true catastrophe class requiring specific aggregate management. VER

Climate change intensification began driving systematic repricing of agricultural risk. IPCC projections pointed to increased frequency of drought, heat stress, and extreme weather events — all primary agricultural insurance perils. Underwriters faced the fundamental challenge that historical yield data (the primary basis for MPCI rating) was increasingly unreliable as a forward-looking predictor, while climate models indicated that weather extremes in many key agricultural regions would become more frequent and severe. EST
