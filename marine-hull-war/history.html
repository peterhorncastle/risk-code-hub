Era 1: Origins — Lloyd's Coffee House & the Birth of War Risk1688 – 1850

Marine war risk insurance is as old as Lloyd's itself. When Edward Lloyd opened his Coffee House on Tower Street in 1688, the primary threat to merchant shipping was not weather but privateers, pirates and naval warfare. Britain was frequently at war with France, Spain and the Netherlands throughout the eighteenth century; war perils were inseparable from marine underwriting from the outset. VER

Early Lloyd's underwriters priced war risk into general marine rates without formal separation. The concept of a distinct F.C.&S. (Free of Capture and Seizure) clause emerged gradually in the eighteenth century as underwriters sought to carve out the most politically volatile perils from straightforward hull policies. By the late eighteenth century, war perils were routinely written separately on the Lloyd's slip for major commercial voyages. VER

  • 1688: Lloyd's Coffee House opens — marine insurance including war risk from inception
  • 1720: Bubble Act — restricts marine insurance to Lloyd's and two chartered companies
  • 1779: Lloyd's standardised marine policy wording — predecessor of SG Policy (Ship and Goods)
  • Napoleonic Wars (1803–1815): major test of Lloyd's war risk capacity; French privateers devastating to English merchant shipping
  • 1824: Marine Insurance Act fails to pass; Lloyd's continues under 1779 policy form
  • F.C.&S. clause formalised progressively through 18th–19th century practice
Era 2: World Wars — Market Stress & Government Intervention1900 – 1945

World War I (1914–18) placed catastrophic pressure on the marine war market. German U-boats sank over 5,000 Allied merchant vessels; British shipping losses from enemy action exceeded 7 million gross tons. VER The commercial market could not absorb this systemic risk; the British Government War Risks Insurance scheme was established in 1914 to backstop private market capacity and keep merchant shipping sailing. Lloyd's syndicates wrote in parallel with the government scheme.

World War II (1939–45) repeated the pattern on an even greater scale. German U-boats (Battle of the Atlantic), Japanese naval action in the Pacific and Allied bombing of Axis shipping created losses far exceeding commercial market capacity. Again, government war risks insurance schemes operated alongside Lloyd's, with the private market writing within agreed limits. VER Post-WWII, the war risk market was rebuilt on a commercial basis as geopolitical competition shifted to the Cold War.

  • 1914: UK Government War Risks Insurance scheme — backstops Lloyd's as U-boats menace shipping
  • WWI: over 5,000 Allied merchant vessels sunk by enemy action VER
  • 1939: Government scheme re-established for WWII; Lloyd's writes within commercial capacity
  • 1941: US Lend-Lease Act — insuring US-flag vessels in war zones creates new market questions
  • 1945: Post-war reconstruction — war market rebuilds as a specialist Lloyd's class
  • Cold War era: superpower proxy wars create ongoing demand for war risk (Korea, Suez, Vietnam)
Era 3: Tanker War & Modern Institute Clauses1967 – 1990

The Six-Day War (1967) and subsequent closure of the Suez Canal (1967–75) disrupted tanker routing but did not produce major hull war claims. The decisive test came with the Iran–Iraq Tanker War (1980–88), the most significant marine war loss event of the twentieth century in peacetime commercial terms. Both nations attacked each other's oil tankers and those of neutral states; over 400 vessels were attacked during eight years of conflict, many within the Gulf declared "exclusion zones". VER

The Tanker War coincided with the 1983 revision of the Institute War and Strikes Clauses Hulls—Time, producing the CL.280 wording that remains the market standard today. Rates escalated dramatically in the mid-1980s; AWRP for Persian Gulf transits reached 0.5–1.0% per voyage at peak. The market demonstrated it could absorb the losses — but only barely. VER

  • 1967: Suez Canal closure — routing disruption; war risk demand for Cape route transits
  • 1980–88: Iran–Iraq Tanker War — 400+ vessel attacks; defining market event of the century VER
  • 1983: Institute War and Strikes Clauses Hulls—Time (CL.280) issued — current standard wording
  • 1984–87: Gulf AWRP at peak — significant Lloyd's market profitability despite large individual losses
  • 1988: Piper Alpha offshore explosion (not war but raises war/sabotage coverage boundaries)
  • 1990: Iraq invades Kuwait — vessels trapped; Persian Gulf war risk spikes again
CL.280 — Institute War and Strikes Clauses Hulls—Time (1983) The standard marine hull war wording. Published by the Institute of London Underwriters (now International Underwriting Association, IUA) in 1983 following the experience of the Iran–Iraq Tanker War. CL.280 defines the six covered war perils (war, capture/seizure, mines, strikes/terrorism, piracy, confiscation), the termination/cancellation provisions, and the navigational exclusions. Remains the primary Lloyd's market wording for marine hull war (Code W) to this day. VER
Era 4: Post-9/11, Somali Piracy & The War on Terror2001 – 2015

September 11 2001 restructured the entire war risk market — not through marine hull losses (no vessels were directly lost) but through the collapse of aviation war capacity. Aviation war underwriters suffered enormous losses from the four hijacked aircraft ($2bn+ direct hull losses); temporary government guarantee schemes were required as Lloyd's and company market aviation war capacity evaporated overnight. VER The marine war market tightened simultaneously as terrorism premium was loaded across all war-risk lines.

The Somali piracy epidemic (2008–2012) generated the next major structural event. Pirate gangs operating from failed-state Somalia used fishing dhow motherships to seize vessels in the Gulf of Aden and Indian Ocean, holding crews for multi-million dollar ransoms. Over 1,200 vessels were attacked and 33,000 seafarers taken hostage during this period. VER War risk underwriters — who cover piracy under CL.280 Clause 1.5 — faced a surge in claims; AWRP for Indian Ocean transits rose sharply. The response included international naval coordination (CTF 151), the Internationally Recommended Transit Corridor (IRTC) through the Gulf of Aden, and industry guidance (BMP — Best Management Practice).

  • 2001: 9/11 — aviation war market collapses; government aviation war pools established temporarily
  • 2003: Iraq War — Persian Gulf AWRP increases; vessel trading risk elevated
  • 2005: Somali piracy begins escalating — Gulf of Aden ambush model emerges
  • 2008–12: Peak Somali piracy — 1,200+ vessel attacks; Indian Ocean AWRP peaks VER
  • 2011: UKMTO operational; BMP4 published; naval task force CTF 151 operational
  • 2012: Somali piracy collapses — BMP adherence, naval deterrence and military intervention in Somalia responsible
  • 2012: Libya war — vessels trapped in Libyan ports; confiscation/war claims
  • 2014: Ukraine/Crimea — Black Sea first enters war risk focus; MH17 (AW) shot down
Era 5: Ukraine, Russia & the Red Sea Crisis2022 – 2024

Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine (24 February 2022) was the most significant marine war risk event since the Tanker War. Approximately 64 vessels were trapped in Ukrainian Black Sea ports within the first week; commercial shipping to Ukrainian and Russian ports halted almost immediately. The Black Sea was designated a JWC High Risk Area. Naval mines — both Russian-laid and Ukrainian counter-measures — drifted into international shipping lanes; several vessels were hit. VER

Total marine war losses from the Ukraine conflict are estimated at over $1 billion (hull and cargo combined), with individual vessel total losses, CTL claims, and prolonged detention. The parallel aviation war/confiscation loss (leased aircraft stranded in Russia — estimated $10–15bn) was the largest aviation war event in history by hull value. EST

The Red Sea / Houthi crisis (November 2023 onwards) escalated into a sustained campaign of Houthi drone and missile attacks on commercial vessels. The JWC designated the Red Sea, Gulf of Aden and Bab-el-Mandeb as a Listed Area in December 2023. War risk rates for Red Sea transits surged from approximately 0.07% to 0.5–1.0%+ per transit within weeks — a 10-fold increase. VER Major carriers began rerouting around the Cape of Good Hope, adding 10–14 days to Europe–Asia voyages.

  • February 2022: Russia invades Ukraine — 64+ vessels trapped Black Sea; JWC designates High Risk Area
  • February 2022: First naval mines in Black Sea shipping lanes — vessel damage claims
  • February 2022: Russia-retained leased aircraft (~$10–15bn AW confiscation event) EST
  • 2022–24: Black Sea grain corridor — vessels insured under special "grain corridor" arrangement
  • October 2023: Hamas attack on Israel — Gulf region war risk elevated across all Lloyd's classes
  • November 2023: Houthi attacks begin targeting commercial vessels in Red Sea
  • December 2023: JWC designates Red Sea / Gulf of Aden as Listed Area
  • 2024: Red Sea AWRP 0.3–1.0%+ per transit; major liner rerouting around Cape; war risk capacity tightens
